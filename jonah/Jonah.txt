Jonah 1:1
The word of the Lord came to Jonah, the son of Amittai, saying:
  Jonah 3:1 — same-book renewed prophetic commission: The opening verse introduces the Lord's word to Jonah. Chapter 3 explicitly says that the word came to him a second time, marking the renewal of this commission after his flight and rescue.

Jonah 1:2
“Rise and go to Nineveh, the great city, and proclaim in it, for the outcry of its wickedness has come up to Me.”
  Genesis 18:20-21 — city's wickedness brought before God: The Lord sends Jonah to Nineveh because its wickedness has come up before Him. Genesis similarly presents Sodom and Gomorrah's increasing cry and great sins as coming before the Lord ahead of intervention concerning the cities.

Jonah 1:3
But Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the Lord. He went down to Joppa, found a ship traveling to Tarshish, paid its fare and boarded it, to sail with them to Tarshish from the presence of the Lord.
  Psalms 138:7-10 — flight from God's presence and the sea: Jonah boards a ship in an attempt to flee from the Lord's presence. The psalm asks where anyone could flee from God's face and says that even at the sea's farthest reaches His hand remains present.

Jonah 1:4
The Lord stirred up a wind upon the sea, and there was a great surge on the sea; the ship was in danger of being shattered.
  Psalms 106:23-25 — the Lord raises a storm at sea: Jonah's ship is endangered when the Lord stirs up a wind and a great surge arises at sea. The psalm's account of seafarers likewise attributes a storm wind and rising waves to the Lord's command.

Jonah 1:5
The sailors were afraid, and each was crying out to their god. They threw the equipment in the ship into the sea, to lighten their load. But Jonah had gone down into the hollow of the ship, and was sleeping and snoring.
  Mark 4:37-38 — narrative parallel of a sleeper in a storm: Jonah sleeps below while the sailors fear for their storm-tossed ship. Mark likewise places a sleeping central figure in a boat endangered by a great storm. The comparison concerns the narrative situation; Jonah's flight and Jesus' later command have different roles in their respective stories.
  Acts 27:18-19 — parallel emergency unloading at sea: Jonah's sailors throw equipment into the sea to lighten the ship during the storm. Acts describes storm-battered sailors throwing cargo and then the ship's tackle overboard.

Jonah 1:6
The ship’s officer approached him and said, “Why are you snoring? Rise and call upon your god, so that the god may bring us safely through and we may not perish.”
  Mark 4:38 — narrative parallel of awakening the sleeper: The officer calls Jonah to rise from sleep and pray so that those aboard will not perish. Mark's disciples likewise awaken Jesus with a question about their perishing, connecting the awakening with the boat's danger.

Jonah 1:7
Each said to his neighbor, “Come, let us cast lots and learn on whose account this calamity is among us.” They cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah.
  Joshua 7:16-18 — parallel identification of an offender: The sailors use lots and Jonah is identified as the cause of their trouble. Joshua narrates the successive indication of Judah, a clan and finally Achar in the investigation of Israel's offense. The shared pattern is identification of a particular offender within a larger afflicted group; Joshua's supplied wording says indicated.

Jonah 1:8
They said to him, “Tell us on whose account this calamity is among us. What is your occupation? Where do you come from? What country and what people are you from?”
  Joshua 7:19-20 — parallel questioning after identification: After Jonah is identified, the sailors demand an account of the cause of their calamity and his background. Joshua similarly questions the identified Achar, demanding disclosure of what he has done, and receives a confession.

Jonah 1:9
He said to them, “I am a servant of the Lord, and I worship the Lord, the God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land.”
  Exodus 20:11 — shared confession of the Lord as creator: Jonah identifies the God he reveres as the Lord of heaven who made the sea and dry land. Exodus likewise describes the Lord as maker of heaven, earth and sea, explaining the scope of the deity Jonah names while at sea.

Jonah 1:10
The men were greatly afraid and said to him, “What is this you have done?” For the men knew that he was fleeing from the presence of the Lord, because he had told them.
  Jonah 1:16 — same-chapter progression of the sailors' fear: The sailors fear greatly and already know of Jonah's flight from the Lord because he had told them. After the sea is calmed, verse 16 again describes their great fear, now explicitly directed to the Lord and expressed in sacrifice and vows.

Jonah 1:11
They said to him, “What shall we do to you, so that the sea may subside for us?” For the sea kept raising a greater surge.
  1 Samuel 6:2-3 — parallel situation: In both, foreigners suffering under the Lord's affliction ask 'What shall we do' with the cause of it: the sailors ask what to do to Jonah so the sea may subside, and the foreigners ask what to do with the ark of the Lord so that His hand withdraws from them.

Jonah 1:12
Jonah said to them, “Lift me up and throw me into the sea; then the sea will subside for you. For I know that this great surge is upon you because of me.”
  Jonah 1:15 — same-chapter proposed remedy and narrated outcome: Jonah says that throwing him into the sea will make it cease raging against the sailors. Verse 15 narrates that action and the sea's resulting cessation, completing the proposal with its stated outcome.

Jonah 1:13
But the men strained to return to land and could not, for the sea kept rising more fiercely against them.
  Mark 6:47-48 — parallel struggle against hostile waters: The men strain to return Jonah's ship to land but cannot overcome the sea. Mark describes disciples straining at the rowing because the wind is against them, a corresponding scene of difficult human effort on hostile waters.

Jonah 1:14
They cried out to the Lord and said, “By no means, Lord! May we not perish for this man’s life, and do not lay innocent blood upon us, for Thou, Lord, hast done as Thou didst wish.”
  Deuteronomy 21:8-9 — shared prayer against innocent-blood guilt: The sailors ask the Lord not to lay innocent blood upon them as they prepare to throw Jonah overboard. Deuteronomy provides a communal plea that innocent-blood guilt not remain among the people; both prayers explicitly seek release from that bloodguilt.

Jonah 1:15
They took Jonah and threw him out into the sea, and the sea stopped its tossing.
  Psalms 106:28-29 — prayer followed by the storm's cessation: The sea ceases after the sailors' prayer and their action toward Jonah. The psalm likewise narrates seafarers crying to the Lord and the storm being commanded to settle, with the waves falling silent.
  Mark 4:39 — narrative parallel of sudden calm: Jonah's narrative moves from a raging sea to its cessation when he is thrown overboard. Mark narrates wind ceasing and a great calm after Jesus' command; both episodes emphasize the abrupt end of a dangerous storm, while specifying different actions preceding it.

Jonah 1:16
The men feared the Lord with great fear, offered a sacrifice to the Lord and made vows.
  Psalms 65:13-14 — sacrifice and vows in response to deliverance: The sailors offer sacrifice and vows to the Lord after the sea calms. The psalmist likewise promises burnt offerings and payment of vows declared in affliction, joining distress, deliverance and worship.
  Mark 4:41 — fear following the calming of the sea: Jonah's sailors greatly fear the Lord after the storm ends. Mark's disciples are greatly afraid after Jesus calms the wind and sea and ask who He is, connecting the resulting fear with power over the waters.

Jonah 2:1
The Lord commanded a great sea creature to swallow Jonah, and Jonah was in the sea creature’s belly three days and three nights.
  Matthew 12:39-40 — explicit later comparison to Jonah: Jonah is in the sea creature's belly for three days and three nights. Jesus explicitly cites Jonah's confinement for that duration when comparing it with the Son of Man being in the heart of the earth.

Jonah 2:2
Jonah prayed to the Lord his God from the sea creature’s belly,
  Psalms 114:3-4 — prayer from mortal danger: Jonah prays to the Lord from the sea creature's belly. The psalm describes the dangers of Hades and then a cry for the Lord to rescue the speaker's soul, corresponding to prayer from the peril described by Jonah's surrounding narrative and prayer.

Jonah 2:3
and said: “In my affliction I cried to the Lord my God, and He listened to me. From the belly of Hades Thou didst hear the voice of my cry.
  Psalms 17:6-7 — shared prayer language of affliction and Hades: Jonah recalls crying in affliction and being heard from the belly of Hades. The psalm likewise joins Hades, affliction, crying to God and God's hearing of the voice from His holy temple.

Jonah 2:4
Thou didst cast me into the depths of the heart of the sea, and rivers encircled me. All Thy billows and Thy waves passed over me.
  Psalms 41:8 — shared billows-and-waves prayer language: Jonah says that all God's billows and waves passed over him in the sea's depth. The psalm uses the same combination of God's billows and waves passing over the speaker in its language of the deep.

Jonah 2:5
I said, ‘I have been thrust away from Thine eyes. Shall I again look toward Thy holy sanctuary?’
  Psalms 30:23 — shared lament of exclusion from God's sight: Jonah says he has been thrust away from God's eyes and asks about looking again toward the holy sanctuary. The psalm similarly recalls the alarmed claim of being cast away from God's eyes, followed by acknowledgment that God heard the petition.

Jonah 2:6
Water poured around me up to my life; the uttermost abyss encircled me; my head sank into clefts of mountains.
  Psalms 68:2-3 — shared imagery of overwhelming waters: Jonah describes water poured around him as far as his life and the surrounding abyss. The psalm cries for rescue because waters have reached the soul and the speaker has entered the sea's depths, sharing the imagery of life overwhelmed by water.

Jonah 2:7
I descended into a land whose bars are everlasting restraints; and let the ruin of my life rise up, Lord my God.
  Psalms 29:4 — descent toward death and appeal for rising: Jonah describes descent into a land with eternal bars and asks that the ruin of his life rise up. The psalm credits the Lord with bringing the soul up from Hades and saving it from those descending into a pit. The connection is descent toward death and divine rescue; Jonah's supplied petition is preserved as a petition.

Jonah 2:8
As my life was failing me, I remembered the Lord; may my prayer come to Thee, into Thy holy sanctuary.
  Psalms 17:7 — prayer reaching God's holy temple: Jonah remembers the Lord as his life fails and says his prayer may come into the holy sanctuary. The psalm describes an afflicted cry heard by God from His holy temple, connecting distress with prayer reaching God's dwelling.
  Psalms 41:7 — remembering God when the soul is troubled: Jonah remembers the Lord when his life is failing. The psalm likewise connects the soul's trouble with remembering God, giving a close parallel to the prayer's movement from distress to remembrance.

Jonah 2:9
Those who guard vain and false things abandoned their mercy.
  Psalms 30:7 — shared rejection of empty vanities: Jonah says that those guarding vain and false things abandoned their mercy. The psalm also speaks of those guarding vanities to no purpose and contrasts that practice with hope in the Lord.

Jonah 2:10
But I, with a voice of praise and thanksgiving, will sacrifice to Thee. Whatever I vowed I will repay to Thee, for deliverance to the Lord!”
  Psalms 49:14-15 — sacrifice of praise and payment of vows: Jonah promises sacrifice with praise and thanksgiving and payment of his vows. The psalm explicitly commands a sacrifice of praise and payment of vows, followed by the promise of rescue when God is called upon in affliction.
  Psalms 3:9 — deliverance belongs to the Lord: Jonah closes his promise of worship with deliverance directed to the Lord. The psalm directly declares that salvation belongs to the Lord, matching the prayer's attribution of rescue to Him.

Jonah 2:11
The sea creature was commanded, and it cast Jonah out onto dry land.
  Jonah 2:1 — same-chapter commands framing confinement: The Lord first commands the sea creature to swallow Jonah. Later the creature is commanded, and it casts him onto dry land; the later command's content is unstated. The two reported commands frame his confinement and release.

Jonah 3:1
The word of the Lord came to Jonah a second time, saying:
  Jonah 1:1 — same-book renewed prophetic commission: The Lord's word comes to Jonah a second time here. The opening verse records the first coming of that word, so the repeated introduction explicitly marks the renewed commission.

Jonah 3:2
“Rise and go to Nineveh, the great city, and proclaim in it according to the former proclamation that I spoke to you.”
  Jonah 1:2 — same-book renewed commission to Nineveh: The renewed command sends Jonah to Nineveh and expressly refers to the former proclamation. The opening command names the same city and charges him to proclaim there because its wickedness has come before God.

Jonah 3:3
Jonah rose and went to Nineveh as the Lord had spoken. Now Nineveh was a city great before God, about a three-day journey.
  Jonah 1:3 — same-book reversal from flight to obedience: Jonah now rises and goes to Nineveh according to the Lord's word. Earlier he rose to flee to Tarshish from the Lord's presence; the two departures contrast resistance with obedience to the commission.

Jonah 3:4
And Jonah began to enter the city, about a day’s journey, and he proclaimed and said, “Three more days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown.”
  Matthew 12:41 — explicit later reference to Jonah's proclamation: Jonah's recorded proclamation warns that Nineveh will be overthrown in three more days in this source edition. Matthew explicitly refers to the Ninevites repenting at Jonah's proclamation, identifying the preaching whose effect the narrative records.

Jonah 3:5
The people of Nineveh believed God, proclaimed a fast and put on sackcloth, from their greatest to their least.
  Matthew 12:41 — explicit later reference to Nineveh's repentance: Nineveh's people believe God and proclaim a fast with sackcloth. Matthew explicitly describes the men of Nineveh as having repented at Jonah's proclamation and makes their response a witness at the judgment.
  Joel 2:12-13 — fasting joined to turning toward God: Nineveh's people respond to the divine warning with belief, fasting and sackcloth. Joel calls for turning to the Lord with fasting and wholehearted repentance, connecting the outward fast with the response to God.

Jonah 3:6
The word reached the king of Nineveh. He rose from his throne, took his robe off himself, wrapped himself in sackcloth and sat in ashes.
  1 Kings 20:27-29 — parallel royal humbling after a prophetic warning: Nineveh's king leaves his throne and royal clothing and puts on sackcloth upon hearing the message. Ahab likewise responds to the prophetic word by weeping, tearing his tunic, wearing sackcloth and fasting, after which the Lord acknowledges his humbling.

Jonah 3:7
It was proclaimed and declared in Nineveh from the king and his nobles: “Let people and livestock, cattle and sheep, taste nothing; let them not graze or drink water.”
  Esther 4:16 — parallel communal abstention from food and drink: Nineveh's royal decree forbids food and water for the people and livestock. Esther similarly commands the gathered Jews in Susa to fast without eating or drinking. The shared feature is a publicly directed fast with both food and drink withheld; Nineveh's decree additionally includes livestock.

Jonah 3:8
People and livestock clothed themselves in sackcloth, and they cried earnestly to God. Each turned away from his evil way and from the injustice in their hands, saying:
  Isaiah 58:5-7 — fasting accompanied by rejection of injustice: Nineveh's response joins sackcloth and crying to God with each person turning from evil and the injustice in their hands. Isaiah likewise says that sackcloth and outward fasting are inadequate and calls instead for releasing people from injustice and caring for the vulnerable.

Jonah 3:9
“Who knows whether God will relent and turn from His fierce anger, so that we certainly do not perish?”
  Joel 2:14 — shared hope that God may relent: Nineveh's people ask who knows whether God will relent and turn from wrath so they do not perish. Joel likewise asks who knows whether He will turn and relent, expressing hope rather than assuming the outcome.

Jonah 3:10
God saw their deeds, that they had turned from their evil ways. God relented concerning the calamity He had said He would bring upon them, and He did not do it.
  Jeremiah 18:7-8 — narrative example of conditional judgment: God sees Nineveh turn from evil and does not bring the announced calamity. Jeremiah explicitly states that when a threatened nation turns from its evils, the Lord will change His mind concerning the destruction He planned.

Jonah 4:1
Jonah was grieved with great grief and was disturbed.
  Luke 15:28-32 — narrative analogy of resentment at mercy: Jonah is grieved after Nineveh is spared, and his following prayer names God's mercy as the issue. The older son likewise objects when his father welcomes the returning son, and the father explains why restoration calls for joy. The relationship is a shared response of resentment at mercy extended to another.

Jonah 4:2
He prayed to the Lord and said, “O Lord, were these not my words while I was still in my own land? That is why I was quick to flee to Tarshish, for I knew that Thou art merciful and compassionate, patient, abundant in mercy, and relenting concerning calamities.
  Exodus 34:6-7 — shared declaration of God's merciful character: Jonah describes God as merciful, compassionate, patient and abundant in mercy when explaining his flight. Exodus's proclamation of the Lord's name uses the same cluster of merciful attributes.
  Joel 2:13 — shared mercy and relenting formula: Jonah explains that he knew God was merciful, compassionate, patient, abundant in mercy and one who relents concerning calamities. Joel uses that same sequence of attributes to ground the appeal to turn to the Lord.

Jonah 4:3
And now, Master Lord, take my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live.”
  1 Kings 19:4 — parallel prophet's request for death: Jonah asks the Lord to take his life because death seems better to him. Elijah likewise asks the Lord to take his life during his distressed withdrawal, giving a close parallel between the two prophets' death wishes.

Jonah 4:4
The Lord said to Jonah, “Are you deeply grieved?”
  Jonah 4:9 — same-chapter repeated question about grief: The Lord first asks whether Jonah is deeply grieved after Nineveh is spared. The question recurs with the gourd named explicitly in verse 9, drawing Jonah's reaction to the city and his reaction to the plant into comparison.

Jonah 4:6
The Lord God commanded a gourd, and it grew up above Jonah’s head, to provide shade over his head and shelter him from his troubles. Jonah rejoiced over the gourd with great joy.
  Psalms 120:5-6 — divine shelter from the sun: God provides the gourd to shade Jonah's head and shelter him. The psalm describes the Lord as a shelter and promises that the sun will not scorch the one He guards, corresponding to the protection the plant gives before its removal.

Jonah 4:7
God commanded a worm at dawn the next day; it struck the gourd, and it dried up.
  Jonah 2:1 — same-book motif of commanded creatures: God commands a worm that strikes Jonah's protective gourd. Earlier He commanded the sea creature to swallow Jonah. Both episodes show a creature changing the prophet's circumstances under divine command, within the book's larger contrast with Jonah's initial flight from his own command.

Jonah 4:8
As the sun rose, God commanded a burning, scorching wind. The sun struck Jonah’s head; he lost heart, renounced his life and said, “It is better for me to die than to live.”
  Jonah 1:4 — same-book motif of divinely sent winds: God commands a scorching wind on land, and the text then reports the sun striking Jonah's head. Earlier the Lord stirred up a wind at sea, explicitly followed by a great surge. Both episodes feature divinely directed winds, while describing their accompanying events differently.

Jonah 4:9
God said to Jonah, “Are you deeply grieved about the gourd?” He said, “I am deeply grieved, even to death.”
  Jonah 4:4 — same-chapter repeated question about grief: The Lord asks about Jonah's deep grief over the gourd, repeating the earlier question after the city's deliverance. The repetition prepares the comparison between the plant Jonah values and Nineveh's people and livestock.

Jonah 4:10
The Lord said, “You spared the gourd, over which you did not toil and which you did not nurture, which came to be in a night and perished in a night.
  Jonah 4:11 — same-chapter lesser-to-greater compassion argument: The Lord points to Jonah sparing a brief-lived gourd for which he had not labored. The next question applies that concern to the far greater case of Nineveh with its many people and livestock, completing the argument about compassion.

Jonah 4:11
And shall I not spare Nineveh, the great city, in which dwell more than one hundred and twenty thousand people who do not know their right from their left, and much livestock?”
  Psalms 144:8-9 — divine compassion extending across God's works: The Lord's question includes both Nineveh's numerous people and its livestock in the reason for sparing the city. The psalm describes the Lord as merciful and His compassions as extending over all His works, corresponding to the breadth of that concern.
  Hosea 11:8-9 — parallel divine questions about sparing a people: The Lord asks whether He should not spare Nineveh. Hosea similarly presents God's questions about how to deal with Ephraim and His refusal to act according to furious anger or erase them. The populations differ, but both speeches make divine compassion central to withholding destruction.
