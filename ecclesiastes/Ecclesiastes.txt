Ecclesiastes 1:1
The words of Ecclesiastes, son of David, king of Israel in Jerusalem.
  Proverbs 1:1 — parallel royal heading: Both headings present wisdom speech through a son of David who reigns in Israel; the parallel identifies a literary royal setting without proving identical authorship.

Ecclesiastes 1:2
Futility of futilities, said Ecclesiastes; futility of futilities, everything is futility.
  Ecclesiastes 12:8 — book framing repetition: The opening declaration that everything is futility returns at the close with the same attribution to Ecclesiastes, framing the intervening investigation.

Ecclesiastes 1:3
What surplus has a person in all his toil at which he toils beneath the sun?
  Ecclesiastes 3:9 — repeated question: Both ask what surplus the worker obtains from toil, making the later question a return to the book's opening problem.

Ecclesiastes 1:4
A generation goes, and a generation comes, but the earth stands for ever.
  Psalms 89:1-6 — contrast of generations and endurance: Passing human generations are set against the enduring earth in the source; the psalm contrasts passing human lives with God's endurance across generations.

Ecclesiastes 1:5
The sun rises, and the sun sets, and draws toward its place.
  Psalms 18:6-7 — shared solar journey imagery: The sun travels toward its place in the source; the psalm depicts its course from one edge of heaven to the other.

Ecclesiastes 1:6
Rising there, it goes toward the south and circles toward the north. Round and round the wind goes, and on its circuits the wind returns.
  Ecclesiastes 1:9 — same-chapter illustration of recurrence: The wind repeatedly returns on its circuits, providing a concrete natural example of the later claim that what has happened will happen again.

Ecclesiastes 1:7
All the streams go to the sea, yet the sea will not be filled. To the place where the streams go, there they return to go again.
  Ecclesiastes 1:9 — same-chapter illustration of recurrence: Streams continually return to their course without filling the sea, illustrating the chapter's subsequent description of repeated happenings beneath the sun.

Ecclesiastes 1:8
All words are wearisome; a man will not be able to speak. The eye will not be filled with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing.
  Proverbs 27:20 — parallel insatiability: The source's eye is never filled with seeing; the proverb likewise calls human eyes insatiable, supporting the shared observation about unfulfilled desire.

Ecclesiastes 1:9
What has come to be is what will come to be, and what has been done is what will be done; there is nothing fresh beneath the sun.
  Ecclesiastes 3:15 — repeated temporal claim: Both collapse the distinction between past and future by saying that what will happen has already happened.

Ecclesiastes 1:10
If anyone speaks and says, “Behold, this is new,” it has already come to be in the ages that were before us.
  Ecclesiastes 1:9 — same-chapter rhetorical expansion: The imagined announcement of something new is answered by appeal to earlier ages, elaborating the preceding denial of anything fresh beneath the sun.

Ecclesiastes 1:11
There is no remembrance of the first ones, nor will the last ones who have come to be have remembrance among those who will come at the end.
  Ecclesiastes 2:16 — repeated loss of remembrance: The general disappearance of earlier generations from memory becomes the more particular claim that neither wise nor foolish people retain enduring remembrance.

Ecclesiastes 1:12
I, Ecclesiastes, became king over Israel in Jerusalem.
  Ecclesiastes 1:1 — same-chapter narrative framing: The first-person declaration of kingship in Jerusalem takes up the royal identity supplied by the chapter's opening heading.

Ecclesiastes 1:13
I set my heart to seek out and examine by wisdom everything that happens beneath heaven, for God gave human beings a grievous distraction to be distracted by.
  Ecclesiastes 3:10-11 — repeated inquiry and its limits: God-given human distraction appears in both passages; the target adds that people cannot discover God's whole work despite their inquiry.

Ecclesiastes 1:14
I saw all the works done beneath the sun, and behold, everything is futility and resolve of spirit.
  Ecclesiastes 2:11 — repeated evaluation of works: The sweeping judgment of works beneath the sun as futility and resolve of spirit returns after the speaker examines his own achievements.

Ecclesiastes 1:15
What is twisted cannot be put in order, and what is lacking cannot be counted.
  Ecclesiastes 7:13 — repeated crookedness image: Both deny human power to put what is twisted in order; the later saying explicitly situates the difficulty within God's works.

Ecclesiastes 1:16
I spoke in my heart, saying: Behold, I became great and added wisdom beyond everyone who was in Jerusalem before me, and my heart saw much—wisdom and knowledge.
  1 Kings 3:12 — royal wisdom parallel: The speaker claims wisdom surpassing earlier Jerusalem figures; God's promise to Solomon likewise describes a wise heart without a comparable predecessor or successor.

Ecclesiastes 1:17
I set my heart to know wisdom and knowledge; I came to know comparisons and understanding, for this too is resolve of spirit.
  Ecclesiastes 7:25 — repeated program of inquiry: Both describe deliberately seeking wisdom and knowledge; the later search extends the inquiry explicitly to an ungodly person's folly, continuing and broadening the opening experiment.

Ecclesiastes 1:18
For in much wisdom is much knowledge, and whoever increases knowledge will increase pain.
  Ecclesiastes 12:12 — parallel cost of learning: Increasing knowledge increases pain in the source; the closing warning similarly recognizes bodily weariness from much study.

Ecclesiastes 2:1
I said in my heart: Come now, I will test you with gladness; experience good. But behold, this too was futility.
  Ecclesiastes 7:6 — evaluation of pleasure: The experiment with gladness proves futile; the later comparison of foolish laughter with crackling thorns likewise ends by declaring futility.

Ecclesiastes 2:2
Of laughter I said, “Delirium!” And to gladness, “What are you doing?”
  Ecclesiastes 7:4-6 — critique of foolish amusement: Calling laughter delirium anticipates the later preference for mourning and wise rebuke over the gladness, songs and laughter of fools.

Ecclesiastes 2:3
I examined in my heart how to draw my flesh toward wine—while my heart guided me with wisdom—and how to take hold of folly, until I might see what good human beings should do beneath the sun during the numbered days of their lives.
  Ecclesiastes 6:12 — repeated question of life's good: The experiment asks what people should do during their numbered days; the target asks who knows what is good during those same limited days.

Ecclesiastes 2:4
I enlarged my work; I built houses for myself and planted vineyards for myself,
  1 Kings 10:22a — royal building parallel: The source lists building houses among royal projects; the Kings unit explicitly includes the king's house and extensive building undertakings.

Ecclesiastes 2:7
I acquired male and female slaves, and I had slaves born in my house. I also had a great possession of herds and flocks, beyond everyone who was before me in Jerusalem.
  1 Kings 10:22b-22c — royal labor-force comparison: The source's acquired slaves belong to its account of royal possessions; Kings distinguishes subjected peoples and Israelites' royal service, supplying a concrete parallel for organized royal labor.

Ecclesiastes 2:8
I also gathered silver and gold for myself, and the riches of kings and regions. I provided male and female singers for myself, and human delights—a male cupbearer and female cupbearers.
  1 Kings 10:14-15 — royal wealth parallel: The source accumulates silver, gold and riches of kings and regions; Kings records Solomon's gold receipts and tribute from regional kings and officials.

Ecclesiastes 2:9
I grew great and surpassed everyone who was before me in Jerusalem; my wisdom also remained with me.
  1 Kings 10:23 — royal preeminence parallel: The speaker combines surpassing greatness with retained wisdom; Kings likewise combines Solomon's surpassing wealth with surpassing discernment.

Ecclesiastes 2:10
Whatever my eyes asked for, I did not withhold from them. I did not keep my heart from any gladness, for my heart rejoiced in all my toil, and this was my portion from all my toil.
  Ecclesiastes 3:22 — repeated portion in work: Joy in the speaker's toil is called his portion; the target likewise locates a person's portion in rejoicing in his works.

Ecclesiastes 2:11
Then I looked upon all my works that my hands had done and the toil with which I had labored to do them, and behold, everything was futility and resolve of spirit, with no surplus beneath the sun.
  1 Corinthians 15:58 — theological contrast in toil: The source finds no surplus in its completed works; Paul, after discussing resurrection, assures believers that their labor in the Lord is not empty.

Ecclesiastes 2:13
I saw that wisdom has an advantage over folly, like the advantage light has over darkness.
  Proverbs 4:18-19 — shared light and darkness comparison: The advantage of wisdom over folly is pictured as light over darkness; Proverbs contrasts illuminated righteous ways with dark ways that cause stumbling.

Ecclesiastes 2:14
The wise person’s eyes are in his head, but the fool walks in darkness. Yet I too knew that one event will happen to them all.
  Ecclesiastes 9:2 — repeated common fate: The source acknowledges one event for wise and foolish people; the later passage expands the same observation across righteous, ungodly and other contrasting groups.

Ecclesiastes 2:15
I said in my heart: The fool’s fate will happen to me too. Why have I been wise? Then I spoke excessively in my heart, because a fool speaks from excess, for this too is futility.
  Ecclesiastes 2:16 — same-chapter explanation of common fate: The speaker questions wisdom because he shares the fool's fate; the following verse specifies their shared death and absence of enduring remembrance.

Ecclesiastes 2:16
For there is no enduring remembrance of the wise person along with the fool; in the coming days everything will already be forgotten. How will the wise person die along with the fool?
  Psalms 48:10-11 — parallel death of wise and foolish: The source brings wise and foolish people together in death; the psalm likewise speaks of wise people dying and foolish people perishing.

Ecclesiastes 2:17
I hated life, because the work done beneath the sun was grievous to me, for everything is futility and resolve of spirit.
  Job 3:11-13 — parallel distress at living: The speaker hates life because its work is grievous; Job's lament likewise prefers having died immediately and found rest to his present suffering.

Ecclesiastes 2:18
I hated all my toil with which I toil beneath the sun, because I leave it to the person who comes after me.
  Sirach 14:15 — parallel loss of labors to an heir: Both expressly describe leaving one's labors to another, with Sirach naming the division of an inheritance.

Ecclesiastes 2:19
Who knows whether he will be wise or foolish? Yet he has authority over all the toil with which I labored and acted wisely beneath the sun. This too is futility.
  Luke 12:20 — parallel uncertainty over accumulated goods: The source cannot know the character of the person who will control its toil; the parable asks whose the stored goods will be when their owner dies.

Ecclesiastes 2:20
I turned to bid my heart farewell concerning all the toil with which I had labored beneath the sun,
  Ecclesiastes 2:18-19 — same-chapter emotional consequence: The speaker's withdrawal of heart from toil follows the stated distress that another person, possibly foolish, will control everything produced.

Ecclesiastes 2:21
for a person may toil with wisdom, knowledge and courage, yet will give his portion to someone who did not toil for it. This too is futility and a great evil.
  Sirach 14:15 — parallel transfer of toil: The source calls it grievous to give one's hard-won portion to someone who did not labor; Sirach similarly anticipates one's labors becoming another's inheritance.

Ecclesiastes 2:22
For what comes to a person from all his toil and the resolve of his heart with which he toils beneath the sun?
  Ecclesiastes 1:3 — repeated question about gain: Both ask what a person obtains from all the toil undertaken beneath the sun, repeating the book's governing question.

Ecclesiastes 2:23
For all his days are pain, and his distraction is anger; even at night his heart does not sleep. This too is futility.
  Ecclesiastes 5:11 — repeated sleeplessness from anxious possession: The source's toiling heart cannot sleep at night; the later contrast says that abundance of riches prevents sleep even while the worker sleeps sweetly.

Ecclesiastes 2:24
There is no good in a person. What he will eat and drink and let his soul see as good in his toil—I saw that this too is from the hand of God.
  Ecclesiastes 3:13 — repeated divine gift of enjoyment: Both explicitly locate eating, drinking and seeing goodness in one's toil in God's giving, rather than treating enjoyment as self-produced.

Ecclesiastes 2:25
For who will eat, and who will abstain, apart from Him?
  Ecclesiastes 5:18 — dependence on divine enabling: The question about eating apart from God is developed by the statement that God must empower a person to eat from his possessions.

Ecclesiastes 2:26
For to the person who is good before Him He gave wisdom, knowledge and gladness; but to the sinner He gave the distraction of adding and gathering, to give to the one who is good before God. This too is futility and resolve of spirit.
  1 Kings 3:12-13 — parallel divine gift of wisdom and prosperity: The source attributes wisdom, knowledge and gladness to God's giving; Kings depicts God giving Solomon discernment, wealth and glory.

Ecclesiastes 3:1
For all things there is a time, and a season for every matter beneath heaven.
  Daniel 2:21 — parallel ordering of times: The source assigns a time and season to every matter; Daniel explicitly places changes of seasons and times under God's action.

Ecclesiastes 3:2
a time to give birth and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to uproot what is planted,
  1 Samuel 2:6 — related life-and-death pairing: The source places birth and death in paired times; Hannah's prayer pairs the Lord's putting to death and giving life, grounding the contrast in divine action.

Ecclesiastes 3:3
a time to kill and a time to heal, a time to pull down and a time to build,
  Jeremiah 1:10 — shared demolition and building imagery: The source pairs pulling down with building; Jeremiah's commission likewise includes demolishing and building among opposed appointed actions.

Ecclesiastes 3:4
a time to weep and a time to laugh, a time to beat the breast in mourning and a time to dance,
  Romans 12:15 — shared joy-and-grief pairing: The source recognizes distinct times for weeping and laughter; Paul instructs believers to join others in rejoicing and weeping as their circumstances require.

Ecclesiastes 3:7
a time to tear and a time to sew, a time to be silent and a time to speak,
  Amos 5:13 — parallel timely silence: The source distinguishes a time to be silent from a time to speak; Amos provides a particular case in which understanding calls for silence in an evil time.

Ecclesiastes 3:9
What surplus does the worker gain from the things for which he toils?
  Ecclesiastes 1:3 — repeated question about toil: The worker's surplus from toil restates the opening question about a person's surplus from labor beneath the sun.

Ecclesiastes 3:10
I saw the distraction God has given human beings to be distracted by.
  Ecclesiastes 1:13 — repeated God-given distraction: Both passages identify the distracting human occupation being investigated as something God has given to human beings.

Ecclesiastes 3:11
He has made everything beautiful in its time. He has also put the age in their hearts, so that human beings may not find out the work God has done from beginning to end.
  Ecclesiastes 8:17 — repeated limit of discovery: Humans cannot find God's work from beginning to end in the source; the target insists that even a wise person's searching cannot discover it.

Ecclesiastes 3:12
I knew that there is no good for them except to rejoice and do good during one’s life.
  Ecclesiastes 8:15 — parallel commendation of gladness: The source finds good in rejoicing during life; the later passage praises gladness as accompanying one's toil through God-given days.

Ecclesiastes 3:13
Moreover, whenever a person eats and drinks and sees good in all his toil, this is God’s gift.
  1 Timothy 6:17 — parallel enjoyment as divine provision: The source calls eating, drinking and enjoying one's toil God's gift; Paul describes God as richly providing things for enjoyment.

Ecclesiastes 3:14
I knew that everything God has done will endure for ever. Nothing can be added to it, and nothing taken from it; God has done this so that people may fear before Him.
  Deuteronomy 4:2 — analogous completeness: No addition or subtraction can be made to God's enduring work in the source; Deuteronomy forbids adding to or subtracting from God's command, applying completeness to His word.

Ecclesiastes 3:15
What has come to be already is, and whatever is to come has already come to be; God will seek the one who is pursued.
  Ecclesiastes 1:9 — repeated recurrence claim: The temporal claim that what will come has already happened repeats the earlier description of recurring events beneath the sun; the target does not address the pursued person.

Ecclesiastes 3:16
Again I saw beneath the sun: in the place of judgment, the ungodly person was there; in the righteous person’s place, the ungodly person was there.
  Ecclesiastes 5:7 — parallel corruption of justice: Ungodliness occupies the place of judgment in the source; the target explicitly names robbery of judgment and righteousness alongside oppression of the poor.

Ecclesiastes 3:17
I said in my heart: God will judge the righteous and the ungodly, for there is a time for every matter and for every work.
  Ecclesiastes 12:14 — repeated universal judgment: The source expects God to judge righteous and ungodly people; the conclusion likewise brings every deed, good or evil, into God's judgment.

Ecclesiastes 3:18
There I said in my heart concerning human beings’ speech: God will distinguish them and show that they themselves are beasts, even to themselves.
  Psalms 48:13 — shared comparison with beasts: The source exposes human beings as beasts to themselves; the psalm similarly compares the uncomprehending person in honor with senseless beasts.

Ecclesiastes 3:19
For the fate of human beings and the fate of the beast is one fate for them: as one dies, so the other dies, and all have one breath. What advantage has a human being over the beast? None, for everything is futility.
  Psalms 48:13-15 — shared beast and mortality imagery: The source compares human and animal death; the psalm joins comparison with beasts to being placed like sheep in Hades under death's rule.

Ecclesiastes 3:20
All go to one place; all came from dust, and all return to dust.
  Genesis 3:19 — parallel return to earth: Both explicitly say that the human being returns to the earth from which he was taken, grounding mortality in earthly origin.

Ecclesiastes 3:21
Who knows whether the spirit of human beings goes upward, and whether the spirit of the beast goes down into the earth?
  Ecclesiastes 12:7 — later clarification of human spirit: The source questions whether the human spirit goes upward; the closing passage says the spirit returns to God, without resolving the source's separate question about beasts.

Ecclesiastes 3:22
I saw that there is no good except for a person to rejoice in his works, for that is his portion. For who will bring him to see what may happen after him?
  Ecclesiastes 2:10 — repeated portion in joyful work: Both call rejoicing in one's works or toil a person's portion, directing attention to present enjoyment of labor.

Ecclesiastes 4:1
Again I turned and saw all the extortions committed beneath the sun. Behold, the tears of those being extorted, with no one to comfort them! Power was in their extorters’ hands, and they had no one to comfort them.
  James 5:4-6 — parallel oppression of the powerless: The source observes victims' tears and their oppressors' power; James depicts withheld wages crying out and a murdered righteous person offering no resistance.

Ecclesiastes 4:2
I praised the dead, who had already died, above the living, who are alive until now.
  Job 3:11-19 — parallel preference for death amid suffering: The source praises the already dead above the living; Job's lament imagines death as rest beyond present bodily exhaustion and servitude.

Ecclesiastes 4:3
Better than these two is the one who has not yet come to be, who has not seen the evil work done beneath the sun.
  Job 3:16 — parallel preference for never experiencing life: The source prefers someone not yet born who has not seen evil; Job similarly imagines the condition of a miscarriage or infant who never saw light.

Ecclesiastes 4:4
I saw all toil and all courage in work: it is a person’s rivalry with his companion. This too is futility and resolve of spirit.
  James 3:14-16 — parallel critique of rivalry: The source identifies rivalry with one's companion beneath productive toil; James connects jealousy and selfish ambition with disorder rather than wisdom from above.

Ecclesiastes 4:5
The fool folded his hands and ate his own flesh.
  Proverbs 6:10-11 — shared folded-hands warning: The fool's folded hands lead to consuming his own flesh; Proverbs likewise connects clasped hands and inactivity with arriving poverty and want.

Ecclesiastes 4:6
Better a handful of rest than two handfuls of toil and resolve of spirit.
  Proverbs 15:16-17 — parallel preference for modest sufficiency: A handful of rest is preferred to doubled toil; Proverbs similarly prefers a small portion and friendly vegetables to greater stores and hostile feasting.

Ecclesiastes 4:8
There is one person with no second, with neither son nor brother. There is no end to all his toil, and his eye is not filled with wealth. For whom am I toiling and depriving my soul of goodness? This too is futility and a grievous distraction.
  Ecclesiastes 5:9 — repeated unsatisfied wealth-seeking: The isolated worker's eye is not filled with wealth; the later saying likewise states that loving silver never results in being filled with silver.

Ecclesiastes 4:9
Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil.
  Ecclesiastes 4:10-12 — same-chapter development of companionship: The assertion that two receive better reward is developed by specific benefits: lifting a fallen partner, providing warmth and resisting an opponent together.

Ecclesiastes 4:10
For if they fall, one will lift his partner. But woe to the one who falls with no second person to lift him!
  Proverbs 17:17 — parallel aid in adversity: A companion lifts the person who falls; Proverbs likewise describes friends and brothers as useful precisely in times of need.

Ecclesiastes 4:12
If the one gains strength, the two will stand against him; a threefold cord is not quickly torn apart.
  Sirach 6:14 — shared protective companionship: Two can withstand an assailant where one cannot; Sirach describes a faithful friend as a strong shelter, giving a related protective account of friendship.

Ecclesiastes 4:13
Better a poor and wise youth than an older and foolish king who no longer knows how to pay attention,
  Proverbs 17:2 — parallel wisdom overcoming status: The poor wise youth is preferred to the foolish king; Proverbs likewise allows an intelligent servant to rule foolish masters, placing understanding above rank.

Ecclesiastes 4:14
for he will come from the prisoners’ house to reign, though he was born poor in his kingdom.
  Genesis 41:14-41 — narrative parallel of elevation from confinement: The source pictures someone coming from confinement to rule; Joseph is brought from the fortress and, after his discerning counsel, appointed over Egypt. It is a parallel, not an identification.

Ecclesiastes 4:17
Guard your foot when you go to God’s house, and be near to listen. Your sacrifice is better than the fools’ gift, for they do not know how to do evil.
  1 Samuel 15:22 — parallel priority of attentive worship: The source urges approaching God's house to listen while criticizing fools' gifts; Samuel explicitly ranks listening and attentiveness above sacrifice.

Ecclesiastes 5:1
Do not be hasty with your mouth, and do not let your heart hurry to bring out a word before God, for God is in heaven and you are on earth. Therefore let your words be few.
  Matthew 6:7-9 — parallel restraint in prayer: The source calls for few words before God in heaven; Jesus rejects babbling many words and begins the model prayer by addressing the Father in heaven.

Ecclesiastes 5:2
For a dream comes with much distraction, and a fool’s voice with many words.
  Proverbs 10:19 — parallel warning about many words: The source associates a fool's voice with many words; Proverbs warns that much talking does not escape sin and commends restraint of the lips.

Ecclesiastes 5:3
Whenever you vow a vow to God, do not delay paying it, for there is no delight in fools. Pay whatever you vow.
  Deuteronomy 23:22 — parallel vow obligation: Both command someone who vows to God not to delay paying the vow; the Deuteronomy passage explicitly makes failure sinful.

Ecclesiastes 5:4
Better that you do not vow than that you vow and do not pay.
  Deuteronomy 23:23 — parallel permission not to vow: The source prefers abstaining from a vow to failing to pay; Deuteronomy expressly says there is no sin in choosing not to vow.

Ecclesiastes 5:5
Do not let your mouth cause your flesh to sin, and do not say before God, “It was ignorance,” lest God become angry at your voice and destroy the works of your hands.
  Deuteronomy 23:24 — parallel accountability for vowed words: The source refuses an excuse of ignorance for a mouth that incurs sin; Deuteronomy requires doing what one's lips have spoken as a gift vowed to God.

Ecclesiastes 5:6
For with many dreams come futilities and many words; therefore fear God.
  Ecclesiastes 12:13 — repeated fear-of-God imperative: Fear of God answers the source's abundance of futile dreams and words; the same imperative governs the book's concluding summary.

Ecclesiastes 5:7
If you see extortion of the poor and robbery of judgment and righteousness in a region, do not be astonished at the matter, for a high official is above another to keep watch, and higher ones are above them.
  James 5:4-6 — parallel exploitation and corrupted judgment: The source names extortion of the poor and stolen justice; James depicts withheld laborers' wages together with condemnation and murder of a righteous person.

Ecclesiastes 5:9
Whoever loves silver will not be filled with silver; and who has loved a yield in their abundance? This too is futility.
  Proverbs 27:20 — parallel insatiable desire: The silver lover is never filled with silver; Proverbs likewise presents human eyes as insatiable, describing the appetite that possession does not satisfy.

Ecclesiastes 5:10
When goodness increased, those who consumed it multiplied. What strength has its possessor except to look at it with his eyes?
  Ecclesiastes 6:2 — parallel possession without enjoyment: Increasing goods leaves their owner merely looking while others consume; the target likewise describes possessions from which a stranger, rather than the owner, eats.

Ecclesiastes 5:11
Sweet is the slave’s sleep, whether he eats little or much; but being filled with riches does not let him sleep.
  Ecclesiastes 2:23 — repeated disturbance of sleep: Riches prevent their owner's sleep in the source; the earlier account of painful toil likewise leaves the heart awake at night.

Ecclesiastes 5:12
There is a sickness I have seen beneath the sun: wealth kept by its possessor to his harm,
  1 Timothy 6:9-10 — parallel harm from pursuing wealth: Wealth is kept to its owner's harm; Paul describes the desire for riches and love of money leading people into ruin and many pains.

Ecclesiastes 5:13
and that wealth will be lost through a grievous distraction, and he fathered a son with nothing in his hand.
  1 Timothy 6:17 — illustration of wealth's uncertainty: The source depicts wealth disappearing and leaving a son empty-handed; Paul's warning against hoping in uncertain wealth states the principle illustrated by that loss.

Ecclesiastes 5:14
As he came naked from his mother’s womb, he will return to depart as he came. He will take nothing from his toil to carry in his hand.
  Job 1:21 — close verbal parallel: Both describe coming naked from the mother's womb and departing naked, using the same birth-and-departure comparison to deny lasting possession.

Ecclesiastes 5:15
This too is a grievous sickness: just as he came, so he will depart. What surplus has he from toiling for the wind?
  1 Timothy 6:7 — parallel inability to carry wealth away: The source says a person departs as he came despite toil; Paul likewise says people brought nothing into the world and can carry nothing out.

Ecclesiastes 5:16
All his days are in darkness and mourning, with much anger, sickness and wrath.
  Ecclesiastes 2:23 — parallel emotional cost of toil: The source's days of darkness, mourning and anger recall the earlier description of all one's days as pain and one's distraction as anger.

Ecclesiastes 5:17
Behold, what I have seen to be good and fitting is to eat and drink and see goodness in all one’s toil beneath the sun during the numbered days of life God has given, for that is one’s portion.
  Ecclesiastes 3:13 — repeated enjoyment of toil: Both commend eating, drinking and seeing goodness in one's toil within God's provision, giving the same practical response to labor's limits.

Ecclesiastes 5:18
Moreover, every person to whom God has given wealth and possessions, and whom He has empowered to eat from them, take his portion and rejoice in his toil—this is God’s gift.
  1 Timothy 6:17 — parallel divine provision for enjoyment: God both grants possessions and enables their enjoyment in the source; Paul directs the wealthy toward God as the provider of things for enjoyment.

Ecclesiastes 5:19
For he will not often remember the days of his life, because God distracts him with the gladness of his heart.
  Ecclesiastes 8:15 — repeated gladness through life's days: God occupies the person's heart with gladness; the later saying describes rejoicing as accompanying a person through the days God gives.

Ecclesiastes 6:2
a person to whom God will give wealth, possessions and glory, so that his soul lacks nothing it desires, yet God will not empower him to eat from it, for a stranger will eat it. This is futility and a grievous sickness.
  Ecclesiastes 5:18 — explicit contrast in enjoyment of wealth: Both describe God-given possessions, but the source denies the owner power to eat while the earlier passage makes that power a further gift from God.

Ecclesiastes 6:3
If a man fathers a hundred children and lives many years, however numerous the days of his years, but his soul is not filled with goodness and he has no burial, I said a stillborn child is better off than he,
  Job 3:11-16 — parallel preference for a stillborn condition: The source prefers a stillborn child to long life without goodness; Job likewise imagines rest through death at birth or the condition of a miscarriage.

Ecclesiastes 6:5
and though it has not seen the sun or known anything, it has more rest than he.
  Job 3:13-16 — shared rest without seeing light: The source's stillborn child has not seen the sun yet has more rest; Job joins imagined rest in death to infants who never saw light.

Ecclesiastes 6:6
Even if he lived through courses of a thousand years but saw no goodness, do not all go to one place?

  Ecclesiastes 12:7 — same-chapter explanation of breakage imagery: Broken vessels and disrupted mechanisms lead into the following declaration of dust returning to earth and spirit to God, placing the images within mortality without assigning them specific organs.

Ecclesiastes 12:7
and the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it.
  Genesis 2:7 — reversal of creation's dust and breath: The source returns dust to earth and spirit to its giver; Genesis describes God forming the human from earth's dust and imparting life's breath.

Ecclesiastes 12:8
Futility of futilities, said Ecclesiastes; everything is futility.
  Ecclesiastes 1:2 — book framing repetition: The concluding futility declaration repeats the opening formula and its attribution to Ecclesiastes, closing the book's principal frame.

Ecclesiastes 12:9
Besides becoming wise, Ecclesiastes also taught the people knowledge; and an ear will trace out an orderly arrangement of proverbs.
  Proverbs 1:1-6 — parallel purpose of wisdom collections: Ecclesiastes teaches knowledge, and the unit mentions an orderly arrangement of proverbs; Proverbs similarly introduces a collection intended to teach wisdom, understanding and interpretation of sayings.

Ecclesiastes 12:10
Ecclesiastes sought much to find words of delight and something written in uprightness, words of truth.
  Proverbs 25:11 — related value of speech: The search for delightful and upright words compares with Proverbs' precious-ornament image for spoken speech; the comparison concerns valued expression, not identical claims about truth.

Ecclesiastes 12:11
The words of the wise are like goads and like planted nails, which were given from collections, from one shepherd, and something beyond them.
  Proverbs 25:12 — shared fastening imagery for wise speech: Wise words are planted nails in the source; Proverbs pictures a wise word fitting a receptive ear as a precious stone fastened in a golden earring.

Ecclesiastes 12:12
My son, beware of making many books; there is no limit, and much study is weariness of the flesh.
  Ecclesiastes 1:18 — repeated burden of learning: The warning that much study wearies the flesh recalls the opening observation that increasing knowledge increases pain.

Ecclesiastes 12:13
The conclusion of the matter—the whole is heard: fear God and keep His commandments, for this is the whole human being.
  Deuteronomy 10:12-13 — parallel fear and obedience summary: The source summarizes human duty as fearing God and keeping His commandments; Deuteronomy likewise describes God's requirement through fear, service and commandment-keeping.

Ecclesiastes 12:14
For God will bring every deed into judgment, concerning everything overlooked, whether good or evil.
  2 Corinthians 5:10 — parallel judgment of good and evil deeds: Both bring human deeds, whether good or evil, into comprehensive divine judgment; Paul identifies the judgment seat as Christ's.
