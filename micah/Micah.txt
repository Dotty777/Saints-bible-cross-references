Micah 1:1
The word of the Lord came to Michaiah of Morashthi in the days of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, concerning what he saw about Samaria and Jerusalem.
  Jeremiah 33:18-19 — later identification and citation of the prophet: Micah's opening places Michaiah of Morashthi in Hezekiah's reign. Jeremiah identifies Micah the Morathite in that reign and cites the warning found in Micah 3:12. The reign and cited warning support the connection while the supplied name forms remain unchanged.

Micah 1:2
Hear words, you peoples! Let the earth and everyone in it attend! The Lord will be among you as a testimony, the Lord from His holy house.
  Psalms 49:1-6 — earth summoned before divine testimony and judgment: Micah summons the peoples and earth to attend to the Lord's testimony from His holy house. The psalm likewise summons the earth, presents God's manifestation from Zion and calls heaven and earth to His judgment.

Micah 1:3
For look, the Lord is coming out of His place; He will descend and step upon the heights of the earth,
  Isaiah 26:21 — judgment proceeding from God's dwelling to the earth: Micah depicts the Lord coming out of His place and descending upon the earth's heights. Isaiah announces the Lord bringing wrath from the holy place upon earth's inhabitants; both move from God's dwelling to judgment on earth.

Micah 1:4
and the mountains will be shaken beneath Him, and the valleys will melt like wax before fire and like water flowing downhill.
  Psalms 96:4-5 — shaken earth and wax-melting imagery: Micah's mountains are shaken and its valleys melt like wax before fire. The psalm likewise describes the shaken earth, with mountains melting like wax before the Lord. The shared shaking and wax imagery preserve the different landscape features named as melting.

Micah 1:5
All this is because of Jacob’s impiety and the sin of Israel’s house. What is Jacob’s impiety? Is it not Samaria? And what is the sin of Judah’s house? Is it not Jerusalem?
  Hosea 8:5-6 — Samaria as a focus of Israel's religious offense: Micah identifies Samaria with Jacob's impiety. Hosea rebukes Samaria's calf, declares that a craftsman made it and it is no god, and speaks of God's anger. Both locate Israel's offense concretely in Samaria.

Micah 1:6
I will make Samaria a fruit watcher’s hut in a field and a vineyard plantation. I will pull her stones down into a chasm and uncover her foundations.
  Micah 3:12 — same-book parallel fates of the two capitals: Samaria is to become a fruit watcher's hut amid agricultural ground. The later warning similarly makes Jerusalem a fruit watcher's hut and Zion a plowed field, linking the two capitals named in the book's opening through parallel ruin imagery.

Micah 1:7
They will cut up all her carved images and burn all her wages in fire, and I will consign all her idols to obliteration. For she gathered from prostitution wages and amassed from prostitution wages.
  Deuteronomy 7:25 — destruction of idolatrous objects: Micah announces the cutting up of Samaria's carved images and obliteration of her idols. Deuteronomy commands the burning of graven images. The shared connection is destruction of the images; Micah specifically assigns the wages to the fire.

Micah 1:8
Because of this she will lament and mourn; she will go barefoot and naked. She will make lamentation like dragons and mourning like daughters of sirens.
  Job 30:28-31 — lament associated with sirens: Micah compares the city's lament with dragons and daughters of sirens. Job calls himself a brother to sirens while describing crying out and song becoming weeping. Both associate lament with sirens, preserving their differing companion animal names.

Micah 1:9
For her blow has prevailed; it came as far as Judah and touched the gate of My people, as far as Jerusalem.
  2 Kings 18:13-17 — narrative counterpart to a threat reaching Jerusalem: Micah says the blow reaches Judah and Jerusalem. Kings narrates the Assyrian capture of Judah's fortified cities and a great force sent onward to Jerusalem. The connection concerns that advance into Judah toward Jerusalem, without equating every detail of the oracle with the campaign.

Micah 1:10
You in Gath, do not magnify yourselves! You in Akim, do not rebuild from a house for laughter; sprinkle yourselves with earth in keeping with your laughter.
  2 Samuel 1:20 — Gath and rejoicing over Israel's calamity: Micah tells those in Gath not to magnify themselves in the midst of the lament. David likewise forbids reporting Israel's defeat in Gath lest foreign daughters rejoice. Both address Gath's possible exultation over Israel's misfortune.

Micah 1:12
Who began good things for the woman dwelling in pains? For calamities came down from the Lord upon Jerusalem’s gates,
  Ezekiel 5:5-8 — parallel divine judgment against Jerusalem: Micah attributes the calamities at Jerusalem's gates to the Lord. Ezekiel explicitly identifies Jerusalem and declares that the Lord is against her and will execute judgment within her because His ordinances were rejected.

Micah 1:13
the noise of chariots and horsemen. Inhabitant of Lachish, she is a leader of sin to the daughter of Zion, for Israel’s impieties were found in you.
  2 Kings 18:17 — military connection between Lachish and Jerusalem: Micah addresses Lachish amid the noise of chariots and horsemen and relates her to Zion's trouble. Kings records an Assyrian force sent from Lachish against Jerusalem. Both passages connect the two cities in a military setting, without making the historical report explain every charge against Lachish.

Micah 1:15
Until I bring heirs to you, O inheritance dwelling in Lachish, the glory of Israel’s daughter will come as far as Odollam.
  2 Chronicles 11:7 — same place: Micah says the glory of Israel's daughter will come as far as Odollam, and 2 Chronicles 11:7 lists Odollam, with the same spelling, among the cities built up for defence.
  1 Samuel 22:1 — same place: Micah's Odollam is the place David fled to when he escaped and came to 'the cave of Adullam'. The spelling differs (Odollam / Adullam).

Micah 1:16
Shave yourself and shear your hair for your delicate children. Enlarge your widowhood like an eagle, for they have been taken captive away from you.
  Amos 8:10 — hair cutting and mourning for loved ones: Micah commands shaving and shearing in mourning for children taken captive. Amos likewise joins baldness on every head with lamentation and mourning for a beloved one. The shared mourning signs are retained without changing Micah's separate widowhood-and-eagle wording.

Micah 2:1

Micah 2:2

  Isaiah 5:8 — parallel condemnation of land seizure: Micah condemns coveting fields and plundering a person, house and inheritance. Isaiah likewise condemns joining house to house and field to field in order to take from a neighbor.
  1 Kings 20:15-16 — narrative example of seized inheritance: Micah condemns taking a person's field and inheritance. Kings records Ahab going to possess Naboth's vineyard after Naboth's death, following the refusal to surrender his ancestral inheritance in the surrounding account.

Micah 2:3

  Jeremiah 18:11 — parallel announcement of planned calamity: The Lord declares that He is planning calamities against the offending tribe. Jeremiah similarly announces the Lord's shaping evils and devising a design against Judah and Jerusalem, alongside a call to turn from evil practices.

Micah 2:4
On that day a parable will be taken up against you, and a lament will be sung in melody, saying: ‘We have suffered grievous misery! The portion of My people was measured with a cord, and there was no one to prevent him from turning away; our fields were divided.’
  Joel 4:2 — shared loss through division of the people's land: Micah's lament says the people's portion was measured and their fields divided. Joel announces judgment over the nations concerning Israel's scattering and the division of the Lord's land. Both describe dispossession through division of the land.

Micah 2:5
Therefore you will have no one casting a measuring cord by lot in the assembly of the Lord.
  Joshua 18:8-10 — loss contrasted with inheritance allotted by lot: Micah threatens the loss of anyone casting a measuring cord by lot in the Lord's assembly. Joshua's account surveys the land into portions and casts a lot before the Lord, providing the positive allotment practice with which Micah's exclusion contrasts.

Micah 2:7
The one who says, ‘Jacob’s house has provoked the Lord’s Spirit’: Are these his practices? Are not his words good with him, and have they walked uprightly?
  Isaiah 63:10 — provoking God's Spirit: Micah cites the claim that Jacob's house provoked the Lord's Spirit. Isaiah describes the people disobeying and provoking His holy spirit, followed by the Lord turning against them. The connection concerns the stated provocation, not an imported claim that God's patience is short.

Micah 2:8

  Micah 3:2-3 — same-book imagery of flaying the people: Micah 2 describes hostility through the image of flaying skin. Chapter 3 develops the charge against leaders with skins stripped from the people and their flesh treated as meat, repeating the book's bodily imagery of abuse.

Micah 2:9
Therefore the leaders of My people will be cast out of their luxurious houses; because of their evil practices they were expelled. Draw near to everlasting mountains!
  Amos 6:4-7 — luxury followed by captivity: Micah says the people's leaders will be expelled from luxurious houses because of their practices. Amos likewise describes powerful people reclining on ivory beds and enjoying costly food and perfume, then announces captivity.

Micah 2:10
Rise and go, for this is no resting place for you because of uncleanness. You were corrupted with corruption,
  Leviticus 18:24-28 — uncleanness and loss of a place to remain: Micah commands departure because uncleanness has made the place no resting place. Leviticus connects defilement of the land with the Lord driving out its inhabitants and warns Israel against the same defilement.

Micah 2:11
you were pursued with no one pursuing. A spirit established falsehood; it dripped to you into wine and strong drink. And it will be from the drop of this people:
  Leviticus 26:17 — shared pursuit-without-a-pursuer language: Micah describes being pursued with no one pursuing. Leviticus' covenant sanctions include fleeing when no one pursues, alongside defeat before enemies. The paradoxical pursuit language is the specific connection.

Micah 2:12
Jacob will surely be gathered with everyone. I will surely receive Israel’s remaining ones; I will put their turning back together. Like sheep in affliction, like a flock within their resting place, they will leap out from among people.
  Ezekiel 34:13-15 — Israel's gathering portrayed as a flock: Micah promises to gather Israel's remaining ones, compares them with sheep in affliction and a flock within its resting place, and says they will leap out from among people. Ezekiel likewise uses flock language for gathering from countries, then explicitly promises pasture and rest.

Micah 2:13
Through the breach before them they broke through, passed a gate and went out through it. Their king went out before them, and the Lord will lead them.”
  John 10:3-4 — shared flock leadership through an opening: Micah's gathered flock passes through a gate with its king before it and the Lord leading. John describes the opened sheepfold, the shepherd leading his sheep out and going before them. The connection is the shared leadership image, not a claim that the passages narrate the same event.

Micah 3:1

  Deuteronomy 16:18 — leaders' responsibility for justice: Micah asks Israel's authorities whether they ought to know justice. Deuteronomy directs appointed judges and officers to judge the people with righteous judgment, supplying the explicit responsibility challenged by the question.

Micah 3:2
you who hate good things and seek evil things, who snatch their skins from them and their flesh from their bones?”
  Amos 5:14-15 — opposition between corrupt preferences and the call to good: Micah accuses leaders of hating good and seeking evil. Amos calls for seeking good rather than evil and restoring judgment, directly opposing the preferences Micah condemns.

Micah 3:3

  Psalms 13:4 — shared imagery of devouring the people: Micah describes the people's flesh treated as meat for a cooking pot. The psalm similarly accuses lawless people of devouring the people like a meal of bread. Both use eating imagery to portray abuse of the people.

Micah 3:4
so they will cry to the Lord, but He will not listen to them. He will turn His face away from them at that time, because they acted wickedly in their practices against them.
  Isaiah 1:15 — violence followed by rejected prayer: Micah says the Lord will not hear the cry of those who acted wickedly against the people and will turn His face away. Isaiah likewise says He will turn His eyes away and refuse multiplied supplications because their hands are full of blood.

Micah 3:5
Thus says the Lord concerning the prophets who lead My people astray, who bite with their teeth and proclaim peace to him; but when nothing was put into their mouths, they raised war against him:
  Ezekiel 13:19 — corrupt prophetic pronouncements tied to food: Micah's misleading prophets proclaim peace when their mouths are supplied and raise war when they are not. Ezekiel condemns profaning God through vain pronouncements for barley and scraps of bread; both connect deceptive prophetic activity with food received.

Micah 3:6
“Therefore you will have night instead of vision and darkness instead of divination; the sun will set upon the prophets, and the day will darken over them.
  Amos 8:9 — shared judgment imagery of lost daylight: Micah announces night in place of vision and sunset over the prophets. Amos similarly announces the sun setting at midday and light becoming dark during daytime. Micah applies the darkness specifically to the prophets' loss of vision.

Micah 3:7
Those seeing dreams will be ashamed and the diviners mocked; all of them will speak against them, because there will be no one listening to them,
  Zechariah 13:4 — prophets ashamed of their visions: Micah announces shame for dream-seers and mockery for diviners. Zechariah likewise says prophets will be ashamed because of their visions when they prophesy.

Micah 3:8
unless I fill up strength in the Spirit of the Lord and of judgment and might, to declare to Jacob his impieties and to Israel his sins.”
  Isaiah 58:1 — commission to declare Jacob's sins: Micah links strength in the Lord's Spirit with declaring Jacob's impieties and Israel's sins. Isaiah likewise commands a strong public proclamation of the people's sins and Jacob's lawless acts.

Micah 3:9

  Deuteronomy 16:19-20 — perverted justice opposed to the legal requirement: Micah charges leaders with abhorring justice and twisting what is straight. Deuteronomy forbids perverting judgment and commands pursuit of what is just, directly opposing that conduct.

Micah 3:10
you who build Zion with bloodshed and Jerusalem with injustices!
  Habakkuk 2:12 — parallel condemnation of building with bloodshed: Micah condemns building Zion with bloodshed and Jerusalem with injustices. Habakkuk pronounces woe on building and preparing a city with bloodshed and injustices, repeating the distinctive city-building accusation.

Micah 3:11

Micah 3:12
Therefore, because of you, Zion will be plowed like a field, Jerusalem will be like a fruit watcher’s hut, and the mountain of the house like a forest grove.

Joel reverses that direction, ordering farming tools made into weapons while gathering nations for judgment. The same implements serve contrasting scenes in their respective oracles.

Micah 4:4
