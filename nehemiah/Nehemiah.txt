Nehemiah 1:1
The words of Neemias son of Achalia. In the month of Chaseelou, in the twentieth year, I was in Sousan abira,
  Nehemiah 5:14 — Regnal chronology within the memoir: The opening dates the account to the twentieth year; the later retrospective identifies the twentieth year of Arthasastha as the beginning of Nehemiah's governorship. These notices connect the court setting with the dated period of his rule without supplying a modern calendar conversion.

Nehemiah 1:2
and Anani, one of my brothers, came, he and men of Judah. I asked them about those who had been saved, who remained from the captivity, and about Jerusalem.
  Ezra 2:1 — Returned community from captivity: Nehemiah asks about the survivors from captivity and Jerusalem. Ezra introduces the people returning from Babylonian deportation to Jerusalem and Judah, supplying the return setting of the community about which he asks.

Nehemiah 1:3
They said to me, “The remaining people who were left from the captivity there in the country are in great distress and disgrace. Jerusalem’s walls have been pulled down, and its gates have been set ablaze with fire.”
  2 Chronicles 36:19 — Jerusalem's demolished wall and fire: Chronicles records Jerusalem's wall being demolished and its citadels burned. The report to Nehemiah likewise describes the ruined wall and burned gates, connecting the earlier destruction account with the city's need for restoration while preserving the different structures named.

Nehemiah 1:4
When I heard these words, I sat down and wept, and mourned for days. I was fasting and praying before the God of heaven,
  Daniel 9:2-3 — Fasting prayer concerning Jerusalem's reproach: Nehemiah responds to the report of Jerusalem's disgrace by fasting and praying. Daniel likewise turns to prayer and fasting after considering the period of Jerusalem's reproach; both responses seek God concerning the city's condition.

Nehemiah 1:5
and said, “No, I pray, Lord, God of heaven, the Mighty One, the Great and Fearsome, who dost keep the covenant and mercy for those who love Him and keep His commandments:
  Deuteronomy 7:9 — Covenant-language echo: Nehemiah addresses God as the keeper of covenant and mercy for those who love Him and keep His commandments. Deuteronomy describes the faithful God with that same combination of covenant, mercy, love and obedience.
  Daniel 9:4 — Parallel opening of confessional prayer: Both prayers open by naming God great and fearsome and by confessing His covenant mercy toward those who love Him and keep His commands. The shared address introduces each speaker's confession of communal sin.

Nehemiah 1:6
let Thine ear be attentive and Thine eyes opened to hear Thy servant’s prayer, which I pray before Thee today, day and night, for the sons of Israel, Thy servants. I confess concerning the sins of the sons of Israel that we have sinned against Thee. I and my father’s house have sinned.
  2 Chronicles 6:40 — Prayer for attentive ears and open eyes: Nehemiah asks that God's ear be attentive and His eyes open to the servant's prayer. Solomon's prayer likewise asks for open eyes and attentive ears toward supplication, providing a close parallel in the language of petition.
  Ezra 9:6-7 — Corporate and ancestral confession: Nehemiah includes himself and his father's house in Israel's sin. Ezra similarly confesses our offenses and traces the community's guilt from the days of the fathers, joining personal participation to the people's history of wrongdoing.

Nehemiah 1:7
We have utterly broken faith with Thee, and have not kept the commandments, ordinances and judgments that Thou didst command Moses Thy servant.
  Deuteronomy 28:15 — Confession against the covenant command: Nehemiah confesses failure to keep the commandments given through Moses. Deuteronomy states the covenant consequence of failing to hear God and keep His commandments, supplying the legal setting for that confession.

Nehemiah 1:8
Remember, I pray, the word that Thou didst command Moses Thy servant, saying, ‘If you break covenant, I will scatter you among the peoples;
  Deuteronomy 28:64 — Mosaic warning of dispersion: Nehemiah recalls the warning that covenant-breaking brings scattering among the peoples. Deuteronomy explicitly warns that the Lord will scatter Israel among all nations, the consequence appealed to in the prayer.
  Leviticus 26:33 — Covenant warning of scattering and desolation: The remembered warning of scattering among the peoples corresponds to Leviticus's threat to scatter Israel among the nations and leave its land and cities desolate. This also fits the ruined-city report that prompts Nehemiah's prayer.

Nehemiah 1:9
and if you turn back to Me, keep My commandments and do them, even if your dispersed people are at the farthest edge of heaven, I will gather them from there and bring them into the place I chose for My name to dwell there.’
  Deuteronomy 30:2-4 — Appeal to the promised gathering: Nehemiah recalls return to God, obedience and gathering even from the farthest edge of heaven. Deuteronomy places the same conditions before God's mercy and gathering from dispersion at the ends of heaven.
  Deuteronomy 12:5 — The chosen place for God's name: Nehemiah's petition speaks of bringing the dispersed people to the place chosen for God's name to dwell. Deuteronomy directs the people to seek the place God chooses to set His name, giving the worship-place language used in the petition.

Nehemiah 1:10
They are Thy servants and Thy people, whom Thou didst redeem by Thy great power and Thy mighty hand.
  Deuteronomy 9:26 — Intercession for the redeemed people: Nehemiah appeals to God's ownership of the people redeemed by great power and a mighty hand. Moses likewise intercedes for God's people by recalling their redemption through His great strength and mighty hand.

Nehemiah 1:11
No, I pray, Lord! But let Thine ear be attentive to Thy servant’s prayer and to the prayer of Thy servants who wish to fear Thy name. Grant, I pray, Thy servant success today, and give him compassion before this man.” Now I was the king’s cupbearer.
  Nehemiah 2:8 — Petition and subsequent royal grant: Nehemiah asks for success and compassion before the man he serves as cupbearer. The later royal grant is attributed to God's good hand, answering the petition's concern for favor in the royal audience.
  Ezra 7:28 — Divine mercy before the king: Nehemiah asks God to give him compassion before the king. Ezra thanks God for inclining mercy toward him in the eyes of the king and his officials, a closely parallel account of divine favor in a royal mission.

Nehemiah 2:1

  Nehemiah 5:14 — Twentieth-year mission and governorship: The audience is dated to the twentieth year of Arthasastha. Nehemiah later dates the beginning of his governorship to that same regnal year, linking the royal audience to the dated period of his service in Judah.

  Nehemiah 1:3-4 — Reported distress becomes visible sorrow: The king recognizes distress of heart in Nehemiah's face. The earlier report of Jerusalem's disgrace and Nehemiah's resulting weeping and mourning supply the stated narrative cause of that sorrow.

  Nehemiah 1:3 — The ruined-city report repeated to the king: Nehemiah explains his distress by describing the desolate city and burned gates. This repeats the earlier report of Jerusalem's broken walls and burned gates, now making the city's condition the reason for his appeal at court.

Nehemiah 2:4

  Nehemiah 1:11 — Prayer carried into the royal audience: When the king invites a request, Nehemiah prays to the God of heaven. The earlier prayer had specifically asked for success and compassion before this royal audience, connecting the immediate prayer to that stated petition.

Nehemiah 2:6

This verbal correspondence records the requested mission becoming an authorized journey; the duration of the stated time limit is not supplied.

Nehemiah 2:7

Ezra's party delivers the king's ordinance to administrators and governors beyond the river, showing the corresponding use of royal documents in a Jerusalem mission.

Nehemiah 2:8

Nehemiah 2:9

  Ezra 8:22 — Contrast in arrangements for travel protection: The king sends army commanders and horsemen with Nehemiah. Ezra explains that he was ashamed to request a force and horsemen after speaking of God's protecting hand, so the two Jerusalem journeys describe different arrangements for protection.

  Ezra 7:27-28 — God's good hand strengthens the royal mission: Nehemiah recounts God's good hand and the king's words as the builders are strengthened. Ezra likewise joins God's influence on the king with mercy before the officials and his own strengthening under God's good hand.

Nehemiah 2:19

  Nehemiah 6:6-8 — Recurring allegation of revolt: The accusation of revolt voiced by Sanaballat and his companions returns in Sanaballat's later letter, which links wall building with alleged rebellion and kingship. Nehemiah explicitly denies that later allegation as invented.

Nehemiah 2:20

The leaders in Ezra similarly reject the outsiders' proposed participation and affirm that they themselves will build God's house; the projects remain respectively the city wall and the temple.

  Genesis 15:18-21 — Land covenant recalled: The prayer recalls the covenant to give Abram and his offspring the land of named peoples; Genesis explicitly records that covenant and its peoples. Genesis's longer list and boundary description are not reduced to Nehemiah's shorter list.

Nehemiah 9:9

Nehemiah 9:10

  Exodus 15:4-5 — Pursuers sink like a stone: The prayer's image of the pursuers thrown into the deep like a stone closely recalls the sea song's description of Pharaoh's force sinking into the deep as a stone.

Nehemiah 9:12

Nehemiah 9:20

Nehemiah 9:21

“Thou didst multiply their sons like the stars of heaven and bring them into the land that Thou hadst spoken of to their fathers, and they inherited it.
  Genesis 15:5 — Offspring like the stars promised: The prayer's multiplication of the ancestors' sons like heaven's stars recalls God's promise to Abram that his seed would be like the stars he was asked to count.
  Deuteronomy 1:10 — Israel multiplied like the stars: The prayer's star comparison also corresponds to Moses' statement that the Lord had multiplied Israel so that they were as heaven's stars in multitude.

Nehemiah 9:25

Deuteronomy had described that same distinctive set of inherited benefits and warned against forgetting God after being filled.

Nehemiah 9:28

Nehemiah 12:20: Sallai, Kallai, Amouk and Abed are priestly family heads; the other texts concern Gaal son of Abed at Shechem, an unrelated man.
Nehemiah 12:21: The priestly houses Elkia and Jedeiou with heads Asabias and Nathanael are not shown elsewhere; the other texts name a Levite Nathanael and Asabias under Josiah, an assistant of Esdras, and Elkia in the high priest's ancestry, all different men.
Nehemiah 12:25: The fragment 'when I gathered the gatekeepers' records Nehemiah's own act; the other texts describe gatekeeper divisions or duties under David, Solomon or Jehoiada, or use 'gatekeepers' figuratively, none referring to this gathering.
Nehemiah 12:29: The unit explains that the singers had built farmsteads for themselves around Jerusalem; the other texts concern Benjaminite towns, Levitical cities, farmsteads of Judah's settlers or Egypt's plague, none describing the singers' settlements.
Nehemiah 12:32: Osaia and half the rulers of Jouda appear only in this dedication procession. The Osaia of Ezra 8:19 is a Merarite Levite brought back with Ezra, not shown in any processional role, and the other passages only mention the rulers or sons of Jouda in general settlement lists. Nothing identifies this group elsewhere.
Nehemiah 12:33: Azarias, Esdras and Mesoulam are named only as members of this procession. Other texts with Azaria, Esdra(s) or Mesoulam involve different contexts: a covenant-sealing list, a priestly house headed by Mesoulam, the returnee leaders, Ezra's genealogy and a gate repairer. None shows these men in this role, and the names are too common to establish identity.
Nehemiah 12:34: Jouda, Beniamin, Samaia and Jermia are processional participants. The other passages use Jouda and Beniamin as tribal names in settlement lists, not as these individuals, and no text shows the same Samaia or Jermia in this ceremony.
Nehemiah 12:42: The verse lists participants in the dedication and notes that the singers were heard. Similar names elsewhere (Ozi the Levite overseer, Maasia and Melchia in a genealogy, Eleazar and Melchia among the sons of Phoros, Joanan son of Karee) are not shown to be the same people. The praise psalms are only generic calls to shout for joy and do not touch this specific ceremony.
