Psalms 1:1
Blessed is the man who did not walk in the counsel of the ungodly, and did not stand in the way of sinners, and did not sit in the seat of pestilent men;
  Psalms 25:4-5 — Refusal of the company of evildoers: The blessed person refuses the counsel, way and seat of the ungodly. Psalm 25 likewise refuses to sit with an empty council or the ungodly and rejects an assembly of evildoers; the shared concern is chosen association with wrongdoing.

Psalms 1:2
but his will is in the Lord’s law, and on His law he will meditate day and night.
  Joshua 1:8 — Meditation on the law day and night: Both texts describe meditation on the law day and night. Joshua connects this discipline with doing what is written and prospering, while the psalm says the blessed person's will is in the Lord's law.

Psalms 1:3
He will be like the tree planted beside the outlets of waters, which will give its fruit in its season, and whose leaf will not fall off; and everything he does will be prospered.
  Jeremiah 17:7-8 — Watered tree and continuing fruitfulness: The psalm compares the blessed person to a tree beside waters, bearing seasonal fruit without losing its leaf. Jeremiah compares the person trusting the Lord to a tree beside waters that continues bearing fruit through heat and drought.

Psalms 1:4
Not so the ungodly, not so, but like chaff which the wind flings away from the face of the earth.
  Job 21:18 — Wind-scattered plant matter and the wicked: The psalm pictures the ungodly as chaff flung away by wind. In Job's discussion of the wicked, they are compared with straw before wind and dust swept by a storm; the shared image is instability and dispersal, within different arguments about their fate.

Psalms 1:5
Therefore the ungodly will not rise in judgment, nor sinners in the council of the righteous;
  Matthew 13:49-50 — Exclusion of the evil from the righteous in judgment: The psalm says the ungodly will not rise in judgment and excludes sinners from the righteous council. Jesus' explanation of the net separates the evil from the righteous at the age's consummation. This compares exclusion from the righteous company without turning the psalm's wording into a separate claim about resurrection.

Psalms 1:6
for the Lord knows the way of the righteous, and the way of the ungodly will perish.
  Psalms 36:18-20 — Known righteous ways and the perishing of sinners: Psalm 1 contrasts the Lord's knowledge of the righteous way with the perishing of the ungodly way. Psalm 36 says the Lord knows the ways of the blameless and contrasts their enduring inheritance with sinners who perish.

Psalms 2:1
Why did nations rage, and peoples meditate on empty things?
  Acts 4:25 — Quotation of the raging nations: The gathered believers quote the question about raging nations and peoples devising empty things, attributing it to God's speech through David. Their surrounding prayer applies the quoted opposition to the gathering against Jesus.

Psalms 2:2
The kings of the earth stood ready, and the rulers gathered together, against the Lord and against His anointed. Diapsalma.
  Acts 4:26-28 — Quotation applied to opposition against Jesus: Acts quotes the kings and rulers gathering against the Lord and His Christ, then names Herod, Pontius Pilate, Gentiles and Israel's peoples as gathering against God's anointed Servant Jesus. The target itself supplies that application.

Psalms 2:3
“Let us tear apart their bonds and cast their yoke away from us.”
  Jeremiah 5:5 — Rebellion pictured as broken yoke and bonds: The rebellious speakers propose tearing bonds and casting away a yoke. Jeremiah describes those who recognized the Lord's way nevertheless breaking the yoke and tearing the bonds together. The images of rejected restraint match, although Jeremiah addresses a different group.

Psalms 2:4
The One dwelling in heaven will laugh at them, and the Lord will sneer at them.
  Psalms 58:9 — The Lord's laughter at hostile nations: The heavenly Lord laughs and sneers at the rebels in Psalm 2. Psalm 58 likewise says the Lord will laugh them to scorn and reduce nations to nothing, presenting divine contempt for threatening opponents rather than fear of their power.

Psalms 2:5
Then He will speak to them in His anger and trouble them in His fury:
  Psalms 109:5-6 — Divine anger confronting kings and nations: The Lord's angry speech troubles the rulers who oppose Him in Psalm 2. Psalm 109 describes the Lord crushing kings on the day of His anger and judging among nations. The comparison concerns royal opposition subjected to divine anger, without identifying a single narrated battle.

Psalms 2:6
“But I was appointed king by Him on Zion, His holy mountain,
  2 Samuel 5:7-12 — Zion and kingship established by the Lord: The appointed king speaks of Zion as God's holy mountain. The narrative joins David's capture and residence in Zion with his recognition that the Lord prepared him as king. This supplies a concrete Davidic royal setting without identifying the psalm with that precise occasion.

Psalms 2:7
announcing the Lord’s decree: The Lord said to me, ‘You are My son; today I have begotten you.
  Acts 13:32-33 — Explicit citation of the second psalm: Acts explicitly names the second psalm and quotes the decree about the Son begotten today in its announcement that God fulfilled the fathers' promise by raising Jesus. The reference and its application are stated in the target itself.
  Hebrews 1:5 — Sonship decree in the comparison with angels: Hebrews quotes the Lord's declaration of sonship and begetting today while asking which angel ever received such speech. The royal decree is used in the target's argument concerning the Son's distinction from angels.
  Hebrews 5:5 — Sonship decree and the calling of Christ: Hebrews quotes the decree about sonship and begetting today when explaining that Christ did not glorify Himself to become high priest. Its context joins the quoted declaration to divine calling, rather than self-assumed honor.

Psalms 2:8
Ask of Me, and I will give you nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession.
  Daniel 7:14 — Granted authority over nations: The son is promised nations as an inheritance and the earth's ends as a possession. Daniel's vision describes authority given to the figure of the preceding verse and worship by all the earth's nations. Both portray worldwide dominion as granted, without forcing the two passages' figures into an independently established identity.

Psalms 2:9
You will shepherd them with an iron rod; you will crush them like a potter’s vessel.’”
  Isaiah 30:14 — Judgment as the shattering of pottery: The nations are to be crushed like a potter's vessel. Isaiah compares the coming collapse to an earthen vessel shattered beyond useful fragments. The particular comparison is the destructive pottery image; Isaiah's warning concerns its own disobedient audience.

Psalms 2:10
And now, kings, understand; be instructed, all who judge the earth.
  Wisdom of Solomon 6:1-3 — Kings and judges summoned to understand: Both texts directly call kings to understand and the earth's judges to receive instruction. Wisdom explains that their authority comes from the Lord and that He will examine their actions, developing the accountability implicit in the psalm's warning.

Psalms 2:11
Serve the Lord with fear, and exult in Him with trembling.
  Hebrews 12:28-29 — Service to God with reverent fear: The psalm commands service to the Lord with fear and rejoicing with trembling. Hebrews urges acceptable service with reverence and awe, adjoining the warning that God is a consuming fire. Both join worship with serious awareness of divine judgment.

Psalms 2:12
Take hold of instruction, lest the Lord become angry and you perish from a righteous way. When His fury burns quickly, blessed are all who have trusted in Him.
  Proverbs 1:29-33 — Instruction accepted or rejected and contrasting outcomes: The psalm commands taking hold of instruction, warning of anger and perishing while blessing those who trust. Wisdom's speech contrasts refusal of counsel and fear of the Lord with the hopeful, secure dwelling of the one who listens. The connection preserves the psalm's actual instruction wording.

Psalms 3:1

The narrative reports Israel's support for Absalom and David's command to flee Jerusalem, followed by his departure with his household; it directly supplies the setting named by the title.

Psalms 3:2

  2 Samuel 15:12-13 — Increasing opposition in Absalom's conspiracy: The speaker laments multiplied afflicters and many rising against him. In the Absalom setting specified by the title, the narrative expressly says the conspiracy strengthened and the people with Absalom increased, before the report that Israel's hearts followed him.

Psalms 3:3

  2 Samuel 16:7-12 — Hostile interpretation of David's calamity: The psalm reports claims that God offers the speaker no salvation. During David's flight, Shimei interprets his calamity and the transfer of the kingdom to Absalom as the Lord's repayment, while David still hopes for the Lord's favor. This compares the hostile theological accusation without identifying the psalm's many speakers solely with Shimei.

Psalms 3:4
But Thou, Lord, art my helper, my glory and the One who lifts my head.
  Psalms 26:5-6 — Divine help and the raising of the head: Psalm 3 calls the Lord its helper and the one who lifts the speaker's head. Psalm 26 describes God's shelter and the raising of the speaker's head above enemies, making lifted head imagery an expression of protection and restored standing.

Psalms 3:5
With my voice I cried to the Lord, and He heard me from His holy mountain. Diapsalma.
  Psalms 17:7 — The cry heard from the holy dwelling: The speaker's voiced cry is heard from the Lord's holy mountain. Psalm 17 likewise recounts a distressed cry whose voice God hears from His holy temple. Both locate the answering of the afflicted worshipper's voice at God's holy dwelling.

Psalms 3:6
I lay down and slept; I was awakened, for the Lord will help me.
  Psalms 4:9 — Trust expressed through lying down and sleeping: Psalm 3 recounts lying down, sleeping and awakening because of the Lord's help. Psalm 4 resolves to lie down and sleep in peace because the Lord grants hopeful dwelling. Sleep becomes a concrete expression of dependence on divine protection.

Psalms 3:7
I will not fear tens of thousands of people who together attack me all around.
  Psalms 26:3 — Fearlessness before an encircling force: Psalm 3 refuses fear of tens of thousands attacking on every side. Psalm 26 similarly refuses fear even if an army deploys and war rises against the speaker. The shared affirmation specifically concerns military encirclement or attack.

Psalms 3:8
Rise, Lord! Save me, my God! For Thou didst strike all who were my enemies without cause; Thou didst crush sinners’ teeth.
  Psalms 57:7 — God breaking the teeth of enemies: The appeal for rescue recalls God crushing sinners' teeth. Psalm 57 says God shattered their teeth and crushed lions' grinders. Both use broken teeth to represent the disabling of threatening wicked power.

Psalms 3:9
Salvation belongs to the Lord, and Thy blessing is upon Thy people.
  Jonah 2:10 — Deliverance assigned to the Lord: Psalm 3 declares that salvation belongs to the Lord and speaks of His blessing upon His people. Jonah's vow of praise and thanksgiving ends by assigning deliverance to the Lord. The concluding attribution places rescue with God rather than the endangered worshipper.

Psalms 4:2
When I called, the God of my righteousness heard me. In affliction Thou madest room for me. Have compassion on me, and hear my prayer.
  Psalms 117:5 — Room granted in response to a distressed call: The speaker recalls being heard and given room in affliction. Psalm 117 similarly says that a call made in affliction was answered with room to breathe. The repeated contrast of constriction and enlarged space describes answered distress.

Psalms 4:3

  Jonah 2:9 — Vanity and falsehood as misdirected attachment: The psalm challenges love of vanity and pursuit of falsehood. Jonah contrasts those guarding vain and false things with his own thanksgiving, saying they abandoned their mercy. Both reject attachment to vanity and falsehood as a spiritual misdirection.

Psalms 4:5

The psalm's own continuation directs inward speech and remorse on the bed; the applications remain distinct.

Psalms 4:6

Psalm 50 joins the broken and humbled heart with anticipated delight in a sacrifice of righteousness. The comparison concerns rightly offered sacrifice in a setting of inward correction.

Psalms 4:7

The priestly blessing asks that His face shine upon Israel with mercy and be lifted toward them in peace. Both express divine favor through the face's light.

Psalms 4:8

Deuteronomy promises to multiply the people and bless their grain, wine and oil. The same agricultural triad accompanies increase, without resolving the psalm's pronoun or importing a greater-than-harvest comparison.

Psalms 4:9

  Psalms 3:6 — Peaceful sleep grounded in divine help: The speaker will lie down and sleep in peace because of the Lord's hopeful provision. Psalm 3 recounts lying down, sleeping and awakening because the Lord helps. Both connect ordinary vulnerable sleep with trust in God's care.

Psalms 5:4

  Psalms 87:14 — Morning prayer reaching the Lord: Psalm 5 expects the Lord to hear in the morning while the speaker stands and watches. Psalm 87 likewise says that the morning prayer will reach the Lord first. The parallel concerns the deliberate presentation of prayer at morning.

Psalms 5:5

Psalm 14 asks who may sojourn in God's dwelling and answers with blameless conduct, righteousness, truthful speech and refusal of harm. The two formulations give negative and positive accounts of access to the holy dwelling.

Psalms 5:6

Psalms 5:7

Proverbs' description of what the Lord hates includes an unjust tongue, hands shedding righteous blood and a witness kindling falsehoods. The paired speech and bloodshed offenses give the connection its particular content.

Psalms 5:8

Psalms 5:9

Psalms 5:10

Psalms 5:11

Psalms 5:12

Psalm 32 likewise joins the heart's rejoicing in Him with hope in His holy name and a prayer for mercy. The combined name, hope and joy language connects the worshippers' response.

Psalms 6:1

Psalms 6:2

Psalms 6:3

Psalms 7:14

Psalms 8:2

Psalms 8:4

Indeed a person passes through as an image; yet they are troubled in vain. He stores treasure and does not know for whom he will gather it.
  Luke 12:18-21 — uncertain treasure recipient: The person gathers treasure without knowing for whom he gathers it. Jesus' rich fool stores goods for years but is told his life is demanded that very night, facing God's question about whose the prepared possessions will be.

Psalms 38:8
And now, what is my expectation? Is it not the Lord? And my existence comes from Thee.
  Acts 17:25-28 — dependent existence parallel: The confession that the speaker's existence comes from God corresponds to Paul's proclamation that God gives everyone life and breath and that in Him people live, move and exist.

Psalms 38:9
Rescue me from all my lawless deeds; Thou hast given me as a reproach to a fool.
  Psalms 129:8 — release from lawlessness parallel: The personal plea for rescue from all lawless deeds corresponds to the promise that the Lord will redeem Israel from all its lawless deeds. The scope changes from the individual to Israel while retaining comprehensive release from wrongdoing.

Psalms 38:10
I became mute and did not open my mouth, because Thou art the One who made me.
  Psalms 37:14 — silent mouth wording: Becoming mute and not opening the mouth repeats the comparison with a person unable to speak who does not open his mouth. This verse explicitly attributes the restraint to God as maker.

Psalms 38:11
Remove Thy scourges from me; from the strength of Thy hand I wasted away.
  Job 13:21 — withdrawn hand petition: The plea to remove scourges and the wasting force of God's hand corresponds to Job's request that God keep His hand away and not overwhelm him with terror.

Psalms 38:12
With rebukes for lawlessness Thou didst discipline a person and melt his soul like a spider. Yet every person is troubled in vain. Diapsalma.
  Proverbs 3:11-12 — divine correction comparison: The Lord's rebukes and discipline for lawlessness correspond to the instruction about receiving the Lord's discipline and correction. Proverbs explicitly supplies a loving father-and-son framework, while the psalm stresses human frailty.

Psalms 38:13
Hear my prayer, Lord, and give ear to my supplication; do not remain silent at my tears. For I am a sojourner with Thee and a temporary resident like all my fathers.
  1 Chronicles 29:15 — sojourner confession parallel: Both prayers call the worshippers sojourners or resident strangers before God like all their fathers. Chronicles also compares earthly days to a shadow, matching this psalm's context of brief life.

Psalms 38:14
Give me relief, that I may be refreshed, before I depart and no longer exist.”
  Job 10:20-22 — respite before departure: Both speakers ask for a little relief before an irreversible departure from mortal life. The psalm says before I depart and no longer exist; Job speaks of going without return to a dark land.

Psalms 39:3
He brought me up from a pit of misery and from muddy slime; He set my feet upon a rock and directed my steps.
  Psalms 68:3 — mud and footing imagery: Being lifted from muddy slime and set on a rock reverses the situation in Psalm68, where the sufferer is stuck in deep mud without firm ground. The shared image joins mire with the presence or absence of stable footing.

Psalms 39:4
He put a new song into my mouth, a hymn to our God. Many will see and fear and hope in the Lord.
  Psalms 97:1 — new salvation song parallel: God gives a new song after rescue here; Psalm97 calls for a new song because the Lord has done wonders and won salvation. Both connect new praise with saving action.

Psalms 39:5
Blessed is the man whose hope is the Lord’s name and who has not looked toward vanities and false frenzies.
  Jeremiah 17:7 — trust beatitude parallel: Both pronounce blessed the person whose hope or trust is in the Lord. The psalm additionally rejects vanities and false frenzies, while Jeremiah's context contrasts dependence on human power.

Psalms 39:6
Many are the wonders Thou hast done, Lord my God, and in Thy purposes there is no one who can be compared with Thee. I announced and spoke; they multiplied beyond number.
  Job 5:9 — innumerable divine wonders: The speaker cannot number God's many wonders and purposes. Eliphaz likewise describes the Lord's great, extraordinary works as beyond numbering; the connection is to this statement within his speech.

Psalms 39:7
Sacrifice and offering Thou didst not desire, but ears Thou didst prepare for me. Burnt offering and an offering for sin Thou didst not request.
  Hebrews 10:5-6 — quotation with wording difference: Hebrews quotes the rejection of sacrifice, offering, burnt offerings and sin offerings. This Psalms text says ears were prepared, while Hebrews says a body was prepared; both exact wordings are preserved without harmonizing them.

Psalms 39:8
Then I said, “Behold, I have come; in the scroll of the book it is written concerning me.
  Hebrews 10:7 — quotation: Hebrews repeats the declaration of coming and the scroll of the book written concerning the speaker. Those are the distinctive clauses of this psalm unit before the following statement of God's will.

Psalms 39:9
I desired to do Thy will, my God, and Thy law within my belly.”
  Hebrews 10:7-10 — quotation and interpretation: Hebrews quotes coming to do God's will and interprets that will through Jesus Christ's once-for-all bodily offering. The psalm's additional law within my belly wording remains specific to this source.

Psalms 39:10
I proclaimed good news of righteousness in a great assembly; behold, I will certainly not restrain my lips; Lord, Thou knowest.
  Psalms 21:23 — public proclamation parallel: The testimony of proclaiming righteousness in a great assembly corresponds to the pledge to declare God's name among brothers and sing hymns within the assembly. Both locate proclamation in a gathered community, with different past and future wording.

Psalms 39:11
I did not hide Thy righteousness in my heart; I spoke of Thy truth and Thy salvation. I did not hide Thy mercy and Thy truth from a great congregation.
  Acts 20:20-21 — unwithheld public witness: The speaker has not hidden divine righteousness, truth and salvation from the congregation. Paul likewise says he held back nothing beneficial and taught publicly, bearing witness to repentance toward God and faith in Jesus.

Psalms 39:12
But Thou, Lord, do not put Thy compassions far from me; Thy mercy and Thy truth have continually sustained me.
  Exodus 34:6 — divine character correspondence: The plea names God's compassions, mercy and truth as sustaining the speaker. The Lord's self-proclamation to Moses names Him compassionate, merciful, abundant in mercy and true, supplying the same cluster of attributes.

Psalms 39:13
For evils without number surrounded me; my lawless deeds overtook me, and I could not see. They multiplied beyond the hairs of my head, and my heart abandoned me.
  Psalms 37:5 — overwhelming transgression imagery: Lawless deeds overtake and overwhelm the speaker here. Psalm37 similarly describes them rising above his head and weighing like a heavy load, expressing transgression as a burden exceeding his strength.

Psalms 39:14
Be pleased, Lord, to rescue me; Lord, attend to helping me.
  Psalms 69:2 — literary refrain parallel: The plea for the Lord to rescue and attend to helping the speaker closely corresponds to Psalm69's request for the Lord to save and for God to attend to his help.

Psalms 39:15
May those seeking my soul to remove it be ashamed and abashed together; may those desiring evils for me turn backward and be abashed.
  Psalms 69:3 — literary refrain parallel: Both prayers ask that seekers of the speaker's life be ashamed and that those wishing him evil turn backward in disgrace, retaining the same paired enemy groups and reversals.

Psalms 39:16
Let those saying to me, “Well done! Well done!” receive their shame immediately.
  Psalms 69:4 — literary refrain parallel: The enemies' Well done taunt and requested immediate shame correspond to Psalm69's Well, Well taunt and requested turning back immediately in shame.

Psalms 39:17
May all seeking Thee exult and rejoice in Thee, Lord; let those loving Thy salvation always say, “May the Lord be magnified!”
  Psalms 69:5 — literary refrain parallel: Both verses invite all who seek God to rejoice and all who love His salvation to continually declare that God or the Lord be magnified.

Psalms 39:18
But I am poor and needy; the Lord will care for me. Thou art my helper and defender; my God, do not delay.
  Psalms 69:6 — literary refrain parallel: Both speakers identify themselves as poor and needy, call God helper and rescuer or defender, and end with the request not to delay.

Psalms 40:2
Blessed is the one who understands the poor and needy; in an evil day the Lord will rescue him.
  Proverbs 19:17 — care for the poor and divine repayment: The blessing and promised rescue for someone attentive to the poor correspond to Proverbs' assurance that God repays the person showing mercy to the poor.

Psalms 40:3
May the Lord guard him, keep him alive and bless him in the land; may He not hand him over into his enemy’s hands.
  Psalms 36:33 — protection from enemy hands: The request that the Lord not hand the person into enemy hands corresponds to the assurance that the Lord will not abandon the righteous into the sinner's hands when he faces judgment.

Psalms 40:4
May the Lord help him upon his bed of pain; Thou didst turn all his bed in his weakness.
  James 5:14-15 — divine help in sickness: The request for God's help on a bed of pain corresponds to James' instruction to pray over a sick person and his assurance that the Lord will raise that person up.

Psalms 40:5
I said, “Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I sinned against Thee.”
  Psalms 102:3 — forgiveness and healing correspondence: The petition joins mercy and healing of the soul with confession of sin. Psalm102 likewise places God's gracious treatment of lawless deeds beside His healing of diseases.

Psalms 40:6
My enemies spoke evils against me: “When will he die and his name perish?”
  Psalms 108:13-15 — imprecatory wording correspondence: The enemies here wish the sufferer's death and the loss of his name. Psalm 108 likewise voices a wish for a person's name and memorial to be erased. The comparison concerns this imprecatory wording without equating the persons or settling the speaking roles of every imprecation.

Psalms 40:7
If someone entered to see me, he spoke vainly; his heart gathered lawlessness for himself; he went outside and spoke.
  Proverbs 26:24-26 — concealed hostility parallel: The visitor speaks vainly while gathering lawlessness in his heart, then goes outside and speaks. Proverbs exposes an enemy's agreeable lips concealing deceitful purposes in his heart and hidden enmity.

Psalms 40:8
Together all my enemies whispered against me; against me they devised evils for me.
  Matthew 26:3-4 — collective hostile planning: The enemies whisper together and devise evils against the speaker. Matthew gives a concrete parallel of leaders gathering together and taking counsel to seize Jesus by deceit and kill Him.

Psalms 40:10
For even the man of my peace, in whom I hoped, who ate my loaves, magnified treachery against me.
  John 13:18 — quotation with wording difference: Jesus quotes Scripture about the person eating His bread lifting a heel against Him. This psalm describes a trusted companion eating the speaker's loaves and magnifying treachery; John applies the meal-and-betrayal pattern to Jesus' impending betrayal.

Psalms 40:11
But Thou, Lord, have mercy on me and raise me up, and I will repay them.
  Psalms 29:3-4 — restoration petition and testimony: The prayer for mercy and being raised up corresponds to Psalm 29's testimony of crying to the Lord, being healed and having the soul brought up from Hades. The shared restoration theme does not identify the two experiences as one event.

Psalms 40:12
By this I knew that Thou didst desire me: my enemy will certainly not rejoice over me.
  Psalms 29:2 — enemy joy prevented: The speaker recognizes divine favor because his enemy will not rejoice over him. Psalm29 likewise praises God for taking the speaker up without gladdening his enemies over him.

Psalms 40:13
But Thou didst sustain me because of innocence and establish me before Thee for ever.
  Psalms 16:15 — righteousness before God: Being sustained in innocence and established before God corresponds to Psalm16's expectation of appearing before God's face in righteousness. Both join right standing with access to the divine presence.

Psalms 40:14
Blessed be the Lord, Israel’s God, from age to age. May it be! May it be!
  Psalms 71:18-19 — liturgical doxology parallel: Both doxologies bless the Lord as Israel's God, extend the blessing across the ages, and conclude with the doubled May it be response.
  1 Chronicles 16:36 — liturgical doxology parallel: Chronicles repeats the blessing of the Lord, Israel's God, from age to age, followed by the people's Amen. It provides a communal worship counterpart to this psalm's closing doxology.
