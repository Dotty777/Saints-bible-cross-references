Jeremiah 1:1
The word of God that came upon Jeremiah son of Hilkiah, one of the priests, who lived in Anathoth in the land of Benjamin,
  Jeremiah 11:21-23 — historical connection through Anathoth: Jeremiah is introduced as a priest living in Anathoth. The later passage names men of Anathoth who demand that the prophet stop speaking in the Lord's name, connecting his hometown with opposition to his commission.

Jeremiah 1:2
which became God’s word to him in the days of Josiah son of Amos, king of Judah, in the thirteenth year of his reign.
  2 Chronicles 34:3 — regnal chronology of Josiah's reforms and Jeremiah's calling: Jeremiah dates the beginning of God's word to Josiah's thirteenth year. Chronicles dates Josiah's beginning to cleanse Judah and Jerusalem to his twelfth year, providing adjacent regnal chronology without identifying the calling and the purge as the same event.

Jeremiah 1:3
It also came in the days of Jehoiakim son of Josiah, king of Judah, until the eleventh year of Zedekiah son of Josiah, king of Judah, until Jerusalem’s captivity in the fifth month.
  2 Kings 25:2-11 — historical counterpart to Jerusalem's captivity: Jeremiah's heading ends with Zedekiah's eleventh year and Jerusalem's captivity in the fifth month. Kings records the siege lasting to Sedekias's eleventh year and the fifth-month destruction and removal of Jerusalem's population; the royal name forms differ.

Jeremiah 1:5
Before I formed you in the belly, I know you; before you came out of the womb, I have consecrated you. I have appointed you a prophet to nations.
  Isaiah 49:5-6 — parallel formation associated with the womb and commission toward nations: Jeremiah is known and consecrated before birth and appointed a prophet to nations. Isaiah describes one formed from the belly as God's servant and appointed a light for nations, sharing vocation associated with formation and an international scope without identifying the two speakers.

Jeremiah 1:6
I said, O Master Lord! Behold, I do not know how to speak, because I am young.
  Exodus 4:10-12 — parallel objection about inability to speak when called: Jeremiah protests that he does not know how to speak because he is young. Moses similarly pleads weakness of voice and slowness of tongue; the grounds differ, but both objections arise when God commissions a speaker.

Jeremiah 1:7
The Lord said to me, Do not say, I am young, because you will go to everyone to whom I send you, and you will speak according to everything I command you.
  Deuteronomy 18:18 — parallel prophetic obligation to speak God's commands: God tells Jeremiah to go to those to whom he is sent and speak everything commanded. Deuteronomy describes a prophet into whose mouth God puts His word and who speaks all God commands; this compares the commission rather than asserting an exclusive identification.

Jeremiah 1:8
Do not fear them, because I am with you to rescue you, says the Lord.
  Acts 18:9-10 — parallel assurance of divine presence against fear: Jeremiah receives the command not to fear because God is with him to rescue him. The Lord likewise tells Paul not to fear or cease speaking because He is with him, applying the assurance to a different threatened ministry.

Jeremiah 1:9
The Lord stretched out His hand toward me and touched my mouth. The Lord said to me, Behold, I have put My words into your mouth.
  Isaiah 51:16 — shared image of God placing words in a mouth: The Lord touches Jeremiah's mouth and declares that He has put His words there. Isaiah also promises to put divine words in the addressee's mouth; the touching action belongs specifically to Jeremiah's account.

Jeremiah 1:10
Behold, today I have appointed you over nations and kingdoms, to uproot, demolish, destroy, build and plant.
  Jeremiah 18:7-10 — parallel authority to announce destruction and restoration of nations: The commission names uprooting, demolition, destruction, building and planting over nations and kingdoms. Jeremiah 18 explains God's speech about destroying a nation or kingdom and rebuilding and planting it, adding the conditions of repentance and disobedience.

Jeremiah 1:12
The Lord said to me, You have seen well, because I am watchful over My words to perform them.
  Isaiah 55:10-11 — parallel certainty that God's spoken word will be performed: God says He watches over His words to perform them. Isaiah compares the effective divine word to rain that produces growth and says it will not return before God's will is accomplished; it supports the performance promise without explaining the nut-wood vision.

Jeremiah 1:13
The Lord’s word came to me a second time, saying, What do you see? I said, A cauldron heated from beneath, its face away from the north.
  Ezekiel 24:3-6 — parallel cauldron imagery in judgment upon Jerusalem: Jeremiah sees a cauldron heated from beneath, interpreted in the following verses through northern calamity. Ezekiel's cauldron parable explicitly addresses the city of bloodshed; both employ a heated cooking vessel for judgment, while Ezekiel's contents and rust are distinct details.

Jeremiah 1:14
The Lord said to me, From the north calamities will be kindled upon all who inhabit the land.
  Jeremiah 6:1 — parallel announcement of northern calamity: The explanation of Jeremiah's vision says calamities will be kindled from the north upon the land's inhabitants. Jeremiah 6 again announces evils looking out from the north and great crushing, repeating the direction and destructive threat.

Jeremiah 1:15
Because behold, I am summoning all the kingdoms from the north of the land, says the Lord. They will come and each set his throne at the forecourts of Jerusalem’s gates, against all her surrounding walls and against all Judah’s cities.
  Jeremiah 6:22-23 — parallel gathering of northern forces against Zion: God summons northern kingdoms to set their thrones against Jerusalem's gates, walls and Judah's cities. Jeremiah 6 describes northern peoples and nations armed and drawn up for war against Zion, giving a corresponding military description without reproducing the thrones detail.

Jeremiah 1:16
I will speak to them with judgment concerning all their evil, because they abandoned Me, sacrificed to foreign gods and bowed down to the works of their hands.
  Isaiah 2:8 — parallel condemnation of worshiping handmade objects: Jeremiah explains judgment by abandonment of God, sacrifice to foreign gods and bowing to human handiwork. Isaiah likewise condemns a land filled with abominations whose people bow before what their fingers made.

Jeremiah 1:17
But you, gird your waist, rise and tell them everything I command you. Do not fear them or be terrified before them, because I am with you to rescue you, says the Lord.
  Ezekiel 2:6-7 — parallel fearless delivery of God's words despite hostile hearers: Jeremiah must rise, speak everything commanded and not fear his opponents. Ezekiel similarly receives the command not to fear hostile faces or words and to deliver God's words to the embittering house.

Jeremiah 1:18
Behold, on this day I have made you like a fortified city and like a strong bronze wall against all Judah’s kings, its rulers and the people of the land,
  Ezekiel 3:8-9 — parallel strengthening of a prophet against opposition: Jeremiah is made like a fortified city and bronze wall against Judah's authorities and people. Ezekiel's face and contention are strengthened beyond rock against opponents; the comparison concerns divinely strengthened resistance, not identical material imagery.

Jeremiah 1:19
and they will fight you, but they will certainly not prevail against you, because I am with you to rescue you, said the Lord.
  Jeremiah 15:20-21 — repeated promise that opponents will not prevail against the prophet: The Lord promises that those fighting Jeremiah will not prevail because He is present to rescue him. Jeremiah 15 repeats the fight, failure to prevail and saving presence, then specifies rescue from evil and pestilent people.

Jeremiah 2:2
And He said, Thus says the Lord: I remembered the mercy of your youth and the love of your completion, when you followed the Holy One of Israel, says the Lord.
  Hosea 11:1-2 — parallel recollection of Israel's early relationship with God: Jeremiah recalls the mercy of Israel's youth and the love of its completion. Hosea likewise looks back to Israel's infancy and God's love before describing departure and Baal worship; Jeremiah's supplied wording is not replaced with a marriage-in-the-wilderness formulation.

Jeremiah 2:3
Israel is holy. To the Lord he is the first of His produce. All who devour him will offend; calamities will come upon them, declares the Lord.
  Exodus 19:5-6 — parallel designation of Israel as holy to God: Jeremiah calls Israel holy to the Lord. Exodus describes covenant-keeping Israel as God's treasured people and a holy nation, providing a covenant counterpart to that sacred status.
  James 1:18 — shared firstfruits imagery for people belonging to God: Jeremiah calls Israel the first of the Lord's produce. James calls those born through the word of truth a kind of firstfruits of God's creatures; the shared agricultural imagery is clear, while the target does not repeat Jeremiah's warning against devouring Israel.

Jeremiah 2:5
Thus says the Lord: What offense did your fathers find in Me, that they withdrew far from Me, followed futile things and became futile?
  Romans 1:21-23 — parallel futility resulting from departure into false worship: Israel's fathers followed futile things and became futile. Romans similarly describes people who fail to glorify God, become futile in thought and exchange divine glory for images, connecting distorted worship with the worshipers' own futility.

Jeremiah 2:6
They did not say, Where is the Lord who brought us up from Egypt’s land, who guided us in the wilderness, in a boundless and impassable land, a waterless and fruitless land, a land through which nothing passed and where no human being lived?
  Deuteronomy 8:14-16 — parallel remembrance of deliverance and guidance through a waterless wilderness: Jeremiah indicts those who fail to ask after the God who brought them from Egypt through an impassable, waterless wilderness. Deuteronomy warns against forgetting that same deliverance and guidance through a fearful wilderness where there was no water.

Jeremiah 2:7
I brought you into Carmel to eat its fruits and good things, but you entered and polluted My land and made My inheritance an abomination.
  Ezekiel 36:17-19 — parallel defilement of the land by Israel's conduct: Jeremiah contrasts God's gift of fruitful land with the people's pollution of His inheritance. Ezekiel likewise says Israel defiled its land through its way, idols and uncleannesses and describes the resulting judgment and dispersal.

Jeremiah 2:8
The priests did not say, Where is the Lord? Those holding to the law did not know Me; the shepherds acted ungodly toward Me; the prophets prophesied by Baal and followed what is useless.
  Micah 3:9-11 — parallel indictment of corrupt civil and religious leadership: Jeremiah indicts several leadership groups, including law handlers who do not know God and prophets who speak by Baal. Micah similarly indicts leaders, priests and prophets, specifying distorted justice and paid religious service rather than Jeremiah's particular Baal charge.

Jeremiah 2:9
Therefore I will still enter judgment with you, says the Lord, and with your children’s children I will enter judgment.
  Exodus 20:5-6 — related intergenerational judgment in a context of idolatry: Jeremiah announces judgment with the present people and their children's children after describing false worship. Exodus warns that the jealous God repays the sins of fathers upon later generations among those who hate Him; the connection does not assert that descendants are judged independently of their own conduct.

Jeremiah 2:11
Will nations exchange their gods, though these are not gods? But My people exchanged their glory, from which they will not benefit.
  Psalms 105:19-21 — parallel exchange of glory for futile worship: Jeremiah says God's people exchanged their glory and will derive no benefit. The psalm specifies the exchange of their glory for the likeness of a grass-eating calf and the forgetting of their saving God.

Jeremiah 2:12
Heaven was astonished at this and shuddered exceedingly, says the Lord.
  Isaiah 1:2-4 — related heavenly witness to Israel's rejection of God: Jeremiah portrays heaven as astonished and shuddering at the people's exchange. Isaiah summons heaven and earth to hear the Lord's charge that His children rejected and abandoned Him; the cosmic response differs from the summons to witness.

Jeremiah 2:13
For My people did two evil things: they abandoned Me, a spring of water of life, and dug broken cisterns for themselves that will not be able to hold water.
  Jeremiah 17:13 — repeated description of abandoning the divine fountain of life: Jeremiah 2 names the Lord as a spring of water of life abandoned for broken cisterns. Jeremiah 17 again charges people with abandoning the Lord, the fountain of life, and attaches shame to that departure.
  John 4:10-14 — related living-water imagery with a contrasting human water source: Jeremiah contrasts the abandoned divine spring with cisterns unable to hold water. Jesus contrasts ordinary well water with His gift of living water becoming a spring toward eternal life; the target develops the water imagery without reproducing the accusation or broken cisterns.

Jeremiah 2:14
Is Israel a slave, or is he house-born? Why has he become spoil?
  Exodus 4:22-23 — contrast between Israel as God's son and Israel reduced to spoil: Jeremiah questions whether Israel is a slave or house-born servant in view of his becoming spoil. Exodus identifies Israel as God's firstborn son and demands his release for divine service, supplying a contrasting account of Israel's identity rather than a direct explanation of this later calamity.

Jeremiah 2:15
Lions roared over him and raised their voice. They made his land a wilderness, and his cities were demolished so that none inhabited them.
  Jeremiah 4:7 — parallel lion imagery for devastating invasion: Lions roar over Israel, making his land wilderness and cities uninhabited. Jeremiah 4 portrays a lion leaving its enclosure to destroy nations and make the land desolate and cities uninhabited, repeating the animal and the consequences.

Jeremiah 2:17
Has abandoning Me not brought these things upon you? says the Lord your God.
  Judges 2:11-15 — parallel causal link between forsaking God and enemy domination: Jeremiah asks whether abandoning God brought the calamities upon the people. Judges explicitly joins Israel's forsaking the Lord and serving other gods with being delivered to plunderers and surrounding enemies.

Jeremiah 2:18
Now what do you have to do with Egypt’s road, to drink Gihon’s water? And what do you have to do with the Assyrians’ road, to drink river waters?
  Isaiah 30:1-3 — parallel challenge to seeking security through Egypt: Jeremiah challenges the journey on Egypt's road to drink Gihon's water and also mentions the Assyrian road. Isaiah condemns going to Egypt for Pharaoh's help without consulting God; the shared Egyptward reliance does not require renaming Jeremiah's river.

Jeremiah 2:19
Your apostasy will discipline you, and your evil will rebuke you. Know and see that abandoning Me is bitter for you, says the Lord your God; and I took no pleasure in you, says the Lord your God.
  Proverbs 5:21-23 — parallel wrongdoing becoming the wrongdoer's own restraint or punishment: Jeremiah says apostasy will discipline the people and their evil will rebuke them. Proverbs says lawless deeds hunt a person and his own sins bind him, similarly depicting wrongdoing as returning upon its agent.

Jeremiah 2:20
For long ago you shattered your yoke and tore apart your bonds, and said, I will not be enslaved, but I will go upon every high hill and beneath every shady tree; there I will abandon myself to my prostitution.
  Deuteronomy 12:2-3 — cultic counterpart to worship on hills and under trees: Jeremiah describes prostitution upon every high hill and beneath every shady tree after breaking bonds. Deuteronomy commands the destruction of foreign worship places on mountains, hills and under leafy trees, identifying the repeated locations as cultic settings.

Jeremiah 2:21
But I planted you as a fruitful vine, wholly genuine. How have you changed into bitterness, you foreign vine?
  Isaiah 5:1-7 — parallel failure of a carefully planted divine vine: God planted a fruitful, genuine vine that became bitter and foreign. Isaiah's vineyard likewise receives careful planting but produces thorns instead of expected grapes; the images express failed cultivation while preserving each text's different bad outcome.

Jeremiah 2:22
Even if you wash with natron and multiply cleansing herb for yourself, you remain stained in your injustices before Me, says the Lord.
  Job 9:30-31 — related washing imagery that cannot establish cleanness before God: Jeremiah says natron and cleansing herb cannot remove the stain of injustice before God. Job imagines washing with snow and clean hands yet being dipped in filth by God; his complaint and Jeremiah's accusation differ, but both deny that the imagined washing secures cleanness.

Jeremiah 2:25
Turn your foot from a rough road and your throat from thirst. But she said, I will be strong, because she had loved strangers and went after them.
  Hosea 2:7-9 — parallel pursuit of other lovers despite warning or obstruction: Jeremiah's woman answers the warning about her road and thirst with I will be strong, and the text says she loved strangers and followed them. Hosea's mother likewise declares that she will follow lovers, whose pursuit is then obstructed; the eventual return in Hosea is not asserted of Jeremiah's speaker.

Jeremiah 2:27
They said to wood, You are my father, and to stone, You gave birth to me. They turned their backs toward Me, not their faces; and in the time of their calamities they will say, Rise and save us!
  Isaiah 44:17-20 — parallel exposure of religious claims made toward manufactured objects: Jeremiah describes wood called a father and stone credited with birth, while people turn their backs on God. Isaiah describes a wooden remainder made into a god and addressed in prayer; this comparison concerns manufactured objects and avoids assigning Jeremiah's later cry for rescue specifically to the wood or stone.

Jeremiah 2:28
Where are your gods that you made for yourself? Will they rise and save you in the time of your affliction? For your gods were as numerous as your cities, Judah, and according to the number of Jerusalem’s streets they sacrificed to Baal.
  Deuteronomy 32:37-39 — parallel challenge that false gods should rise and rescue their worshipers: Jeremiah asks where Judah's manufactured gods are and whether they can rise and save in affliction. Deuteronomy similarly asks where the trusted gods are, challenges them to rise and help, and contrasts them with God's unrivaled power.

Jeremiah 2:30
In vain I struck your children; you accepted no discipline. A sword devoured your prophets like a destroying lion, and you did not fear.
  Amos 4:9-11 — parallel failure to respond to repeated divine discipline: Jeremiah says blows upon the children were vain because discipline was not accepted. Amos repeatedly recounts blows, sword and destruction followed by failure to turn to God, matching the indictment of unresponsive recipients without equating every punishment.

Jeremiah 2:31
Hear the Lord’s word. Thus says the Lord: Have I become a wilderness to Israel, or a land left barren? Why did My people say, We will not be ruled, and we will come to Thee no more?
  Deuteronomy 32:15-18 — related indictment of rejecting the God who sustained Israel: God asks whether He became a wilderness or barren land to Israel while the people reject His rule. Deuteronomy describes the nourished and satisfied people forsaking their Maker and forgetting the God who nourishes them, supplying the corresponding contrast between divine provision and rejection.

Jeremiah 2:32
Will a bride forget her adornment, or a virgin her breastband? Yet My people forgot Me for days beyond counting.
  Isaiah 49:14-16 — contrast between the people's forgetfulness and God's refusal to forget: Jeremiah compares the people's long forgetfulness of God with the implausibility of a bride or virgin forgetting adornment. Isaiah answers Zion's complaint of being forgotten with God's refusal to forget her; the direction of the forgetting is reversed.

Jeremiah 2:34
Even on your hands was found the blood of innocent lives. I did not find them in break-ins, but at every oak.
  Exodus 22:1-2 — legal contrast distinguishing innocent blood from a fatal break-in: Jeremiah charges bloodshed against innocent lives and explicitly says they were not found in break-ins. Exodus excludes murder in the death of a thief found breaking in and then distinguishes a case after sunrise; this supplies a legal contrast without resolving the target's later pronouns or Jeremiah's oak detail.

Jeremiah 2:35
And you said, I am innocent; let His fury turn away from me. Behold, I enter judgment with you because you say, I did not sin.
  1 John 1:8-10 — parallel rejection of a claim to have no sin: Jeremiah announces judgment precisely because the people say they did not sin. First John likewise rejects claims of having no sin or not having sinned and contrasts them with confession and cleansing.

Jeremiah 2:36
Why did you show such contempt by repeating your ways? You will also be put to shame by Egypt, as you were put to shame by Assyria.
  Isaiah 30:3-5 — parallel shame resulting from reliance on Egypt: Jeremiah predicts that Egypt will put the people to shame as Assyria did. Isaiah likewise says Pharaoh's shelter and the hoped-for Egyptian assistance will become shame and reproach rather than benefit.

Jeremiah 2:37
For from here too you will go out with your hands on your head, because the Lord rejected your hope, and you will not prosper in it.
  2 Samuel 13:19 — shared gesture of departing with hands on the head: Jeremiah predicts departure with hands upon the head after the Lord rejects the people's hope. Tamar likewise leaves with her hands on her head and cries out; the visible gesture is shared, while her victimization must not be treated as the same moral situation as Judah's judgment.

Jeremiah 3:1
If a man sends his wife away, and she leaves him and becomes another man’s, surely she will not return to him again? Would not that woman be utterly polluted? And you prostituted yourself with many shepherds—and were you returning to Me? says the Lord.
  Deuteronomy 24:1-4 — legal background to the divorce and remarriage question: Jeremiah asks whether a dismissed wife who becomes another man's will return to her former husband, before confronting the people's many shepherds. Deuteronomy forbids the former husband from taking such a wife back after the intervening marriage, supplying the stated legal analogy rather than resolving all the prophetic question's implications.

Jeremiah 3:2
Lift your eyes straight ahead and see: where have you not been defiled? You sat for them on the roads like a solitary crow, and polluted the land with your prostitution and your evils.
  Ezekiel 16:24-26 — parallel roadside prostitution imagery for covenant infidelity: Jeremiah portrays the woman seated on roads and polluting the land through prostitution. Ezekiel's address to Jerusalem describes prostitution chambers in broad streets and at road heads, giving a particular counterpart to the public locations without reproducing the solitary-crow image.

Jeremiah 3:3
You had many shepherds as a stumbling block for yourself. You had a prostitute’s appearance; you were shameless toward everyone.
  Proverbs 7:10-13 — parallel prostitute appearance and shameless face: Jeremiah charges the people with a prostitute's appearance and shamelessness. Proverbs describes the seductress with a prostitute's appearance and a shameless face; Jeremiah applies such language collectively rather than narrating the same individual encounter.

Jeremiah 3:4
Did you not call Me a household, a father and the leader of your virginity?
  Malachi 2:10-11 — related appeal to divine fatherhood amid covenant infidelity: Jeremiah recalls calling God a father and leader of virginity amid accusations of faithlessness. Malachi asks whether all have one Father and then indicts covenant profanation and devotion to foreign gods, likewise exposing a gap between the paternal relationship and conduct.

Jeremiah 3:6
The Lord said to me in the days of King Josiah, You saw what the dwelling of Israel did to Me. They went upon every high mountain and beneath every wooded tree and prostituted themselves there.
  Hosea 4:12-13 — parallel prostitution imagery linked with hilltop and tree-shaded worship: Jeremiah describes Israel's prostitution on high mountains and beneath wooded trees. Hosea similarly joins a spirit of prostitution and departure from God with sacrifices on mountaintops, hills and beneath named shade trees.

Jeremiah 3:7
After she prostituted herself in all these ways, I said, Return to Me. But she did not return, and faithless Judah saw her faithlessness.
  Hosea 11:1-2 — parallel divine summons met by departure rather than return: God calls Israel to return, but she does not. Hosea likewise recalls God's summons followed by departure from His presence and sacrifice to Baalim; Jeremiah additionally says Judah observed Israel's faithlessness.

Jeremiah 3:8
I saw that, concerning all the things in which the dwelling of Israel was caught committing adultery, I sent her away and gave a certificate of divorce into her hands. Yet faithless Judah did not fear; she too went and prostituted herself.
  Isaiah 50:1 — related divorce-document imagery for separation caused by unfaithfulness: Jeremiah explicitly says God sent Israel away and gave her a divorce certificate. Isaiah asks about the mother's divorce document and attributes her dismissal to lawless acts; the rhetorical question and Jeremiah's explicit giving of a document are kept distinct.

Jeremiah 3:9
Her prostitution came to be regarded as nothing, and she committed adultery with wood and stone.
  Ezekiel 23:37-39 — parallel association of adultery imagery with idolatry: Jeremiah describes adultery with wood and stone. Ezekiel combines adultery with reflections, child offerings and the explicit mention of idols, placing false worship within the same language of marital unfaithfulness without replacing the target's unusual wording.

Jeremiah 3:10
In all these things faithless Judah did not return to Me with her whole heart, but in falsehood.
  Isaiah 29:13 — parallel contrast between outward religious approach and a distant heart: Judah's return is described as false rather than wholehearted. Isaiah similarly contrasts honoring God with lips and a heart far from Him, providing a precise counterpart to outward response lacking inward fidelity.

Jeremiah 3:11
The Lord said to me, Israel has justified himself in comparison with faithless Judah.
  Ezekiel 16:51-52 — parallel relative justification through another community's greater wrongdoing: Jeremiah says Israel is justified in comparison with faithless Judah. Ezekiel says Jerusalem's greater lawlessness justified her sisters, including Samaria; neither comparison declares the less guilty community absolutely innocent.

Jeremiah 3:12
Go and read these words toward the north: Return to Me, dwelling of Israel, says the Lord. I will not set My face firmly against you, because I am merciful, says the Lord, and I will not be angry with you for ever.
  Psalms 102:8-10 — parallel divine mercy and anger that does not last forever: God invites Israel to return because He is merciful and will not remain angry forever. The psalm likewise calls Him compassionate and merciful and says He does not maintain wrath forever.

Jeremiah 3:13
Only recognize your injustice: you acted ungodly toward the Lord your God and spread your ways to strangers beneath every wooded tree, but you did not obey My voice, says the Lord.
  1 John 1:9 — parallel acknowledgment of wrongdoing as the proper response: Jeremiah demands recognition of injustice, disobedience and departure toward strangers. First John describes confession of sins together with divine forgiveness and cleansing; the shared response is acknowledgment rather than a denial of wrongdoing.

Jeremiah 3:14
Return, children who have withdrawn, says the Lord, because I will rule over you. I will take you, one from a city and two from a clan, and bring you into Zion,
  Isaiah 11:11-12 — parallel gathering of a scattered remnant: God promises to take small numbers from cities and clans and bring them to Zion. Isaiah likewise promises recovery of a remaining remnant and the gathering of Israel's lost and Judah's scattered people, with a wider geographical enumeration rather than Jeremiah's numerical expressions.

Jeremiah 3:15
and I will give you shepherds according to My heart, and they will shepherd you, shepherding with knowledge.
  Ezekiel 34:23-24 — parallel divine provision of shepherds for the restored people: Jeremiah promises shepherds according to God's heart who shepherd with knowledge. Ezekiel promises one shepherd, God's servant David, over the people; the divine provision is common, while the plural shepherds and the single named shepherd are distinct.

Jeremiah 3:17
In those days and at that time they will call Jerusalem the Lord’s throne, and all the nations will gather to her. They will no longer follow the thoughts of their evil heart.
  Isaiah 2:2-3 — parallel future gathering of nations to Jerusalem: Jeremiah says Jerusalem will be called the Lord's throne and nations will gather there. Isaiah likewise describes nations coming to the Lord's mountain and receiving His way, with law and the Lord's word proceeding from Zion and Jerusalem.

Jeremiah 3:18
In those days the house of Judah will join the house of Israel, and together they will come from the northern land and from all the regions to the land I gave their fathers as an inheritance.
  Ezekiel 37:21-24 — parallel reunion of divided Israel in the inherited land: Jeremiah promises that Judah will join Israel and return together from northern and other regions. Ezekiel promises gathering into Israel's land and an end to division into two nations or kingdoms, giving a corresponding account of reunion.

Jeremiah 3:19
And I said, May it be, Lord! For I will place you among children and give you a chosen land, an inheritance of God Almighty of nations. I said, You will call Me Father and will not turn away from Me.
  Isaiah 63:16-18 — parallel acknowledgment of God as Father in relation to inheritance: Jeremiah anticipates the people calling God Father and receiving a chosen inheritance. Isaiah's prayer calls the Lord Father and appeals concerning the tribes of His inheritance and the holy mountain; its petition differs from Jeremiah's promise.

Jeremiah 3:20
But as a woman deals faithlessly with the one living with her, so the house of Israel dealt faithlessly with Me, says the Lord.
  Hosea 2:4-7 — parallel portrayal of the people's infidelity through a broken marriage: Jeremiah compares Israel's faithlessness with a woman's faithlessness toward her partner. Hosea likewise portrays the mother through prostitution and the repudiated wife-husband relationship, using marital language for the people's departure.

Jeremiah 3:21
A voice was heard from lips, the weeping and supplication of Israel’s children, because they acted unjustly in their ways and forgot their holy God.
  Joel 2:12-13 — parallel weeping associated with returning from wrongdoing: Jeremiah hears the weeping and supplication of Israel's children after their unjust ways and forgetting God. Joel calls for return to God with weeping, mourning and a torn heart, giving the corresponding summons to penitential sorrow.

Jeremiah 3:22
Return, returning children, and I will heal your fractures. Behold, we will be Thy servants, because Thou art the Lord our God.
  Hosea 14:2-5 — parallel summons to return followed by a promise of healing: Jeremiah calls the returning children back and promises to heal their fractures. Hosea calls Israel to return with words and later promises to heal their dwellings; the return-and-healing pattern is shared while fractures and dwellings remain different expressions.

Jeremiah 3:23

Their tongue is a wounding missile; the utterances of their mouth are deceitful. One speaks peaceful things to his neighbor, and within himself he holds enmity.
  Psalms 54:22 — gentle speech concealing injury: Jeremiah calls the tongue a wounding missile and contrasts peaceful words with inward enmity. The psalm says words became softer than oil yet are missiles. Both expose speech that sounds reassuring while functioning as a weapon.

Jeremiah 9:8
Shall I not visit for these things? says the Lord. Or will not My soul take vengeance on such a people?
  Jeremiah 5:9 — repeated divine question about vengeance: Both passages ask whether the Lord will visit these offenses and whether His soul will take vengeance on such a nation or people. The repeated rhetorical question links the new indictment of deceit to the earlier judgment refrain.

Jeremiah 9:9
Take up mourning for the mountains and a dirge for the wilderness paths, because they failed for lack of people. They did not hear the sound of possessions; from heaven’s birds to livestock, they were displaced and went away.
  Hosea 4:3 — land and animal life affected by judgment: Jeremiah calls for mourning over emptied wilderness paths as birds and livestock depart. Hosea similarly describes a mourning, diminishing land together with the loss of beasts and birds. Both extend the consequences of human wrongdoing to the inhabited landscape and its animals.

Jeremiah 9:10
And I will give Jerusalem over to deportation and as a dwelling of dragons, and make Judah’s cities disappear, so that they are not inhabited.
  Isaiah 34:13 — abandoned cities inhabited by strange creatures: Jeremiah describes Jerusalem becoming a dwelling of dragons and Judah's cities becoming uninhabited. Isaiah describes another ruined territory whose cities grow thorns and become a dwelling of sirens and ostriches. The parallel is the reversal from human habitation to desolation; the named creatures and territories differ.

Jeremiah 9:11
Who is the discerning person? Let him understand this; and the one to whom the word of the Lord’s mouth comes, let him announce it to you: for what reason did the land perish, kindled like a wilderness so that no one passes through it?
  Deuteronomy 29:23-24 — question about the cause of the land's ruin: Jeremiah asks a discerning hearer to explain why the land has perished and become impassable. Deuteronomy anticipates the nations asking why the Lord treated the land so severely and answers with covenant abandonment. Both frame the ruined land as a question requiring a covenantal explanation.

Jeremiah 9:12
And the Lord said to me: Because they abandoned My law that I set before them and did not hear My voice,
  Deuteronomy 29:24-26 — abandoned covenant law as the cause of judgment: The Lord explains the land's disaster by the people's abandonment of His law and refusal to hear Him. Deuteronomy likewise attributes the land's curses to forsaking the covenant and serving other gods. The passages connect judgment to prior obligations that the people rejected.

Jeremiah 9:13
but walked after the things pleasing to their evil heart and after the idols that their fathers taught them,
  Jeremiah 7:24 — repeated accusation of following the evil heart: Jeremiah 9 describes walking after what pleases the evil heart. Jeremiah 7 similarly says the people did not hear the Lord but walked in the thoughts of their evil heart. The recurrence identifies self-directed desire as a form of refusing divine instruction.

Jeremiah 9:14
therefore thus says the Lord, Israel’s God: Behold, I will feed them distresses and make them drink gall-water,
  Lamentations 3:15 — bitterness and gall as a diet of affliction: Jeremiah depicts the people's punishment as being fed distresses and made to drink gall-water. The lamenter says he was filled with bitterness and made drunk with gall. Both express suffering through bitter food and drink, with a communal subject in Jeremiah and an individual lamenting voice in Lamentations.

Jeremiah 9:15
and I will scatter them among nations that neither they nor their fathers knew, and send the sword upon them until I consume them by it.
  Leviticus 26:33 — covenant warning of scattering and the sword: Jeremiah announces scattering among unfamiliar nations and a sword sent after the people. Leviticus's covenant warning likewise combines scattering among nations with the consuming, advancing sword and a desolated homeland. The paired judgments provide a particularly close connection.

Jeremiah 9:16
Thus says the Lord: Call the women who lament, and let them come; send for the skilled women, and let them speak,
  Amos 5:16 — calling skilled mourners: Jeremiah commands that women skilled in lament be summoned. Amos's announcement of mourning in public places likewise calls people to mourning and to those skilled in wailing. Both portray lament as a communal response involving people practiced in it.

Jeremiah 9:17
and let them take up a dirge over you; let your eyes bring down tears, and let your eyelids flow with water.
  Lamentations 2:18-19 — command to pour out tears for Zion: Jeremiah calls for a dirge that brings water from eyes and eyelids. Lamentations addresses Zion's walls with the command to bring down torrents of tears and pour out the heart like water. Both make abundant weeping the fitting response to the people's catastrophe.

Jeremiah 9:18
For a voice of lament was heard in Zion: How we suffered misery and were greatly ashamed, because we abandoned the land and threw away our dwellings!
  Lamentations 1:1-3 — Zion's lament over displacement: Jeremiah hears Zion's lament over misery, shame and the loss of land and dwellings. Lamentations opens with ruined, weeping Jerusalem and deported Judea dwelling among the nations without rest. The passages share communal grief over the loss of a settled homeland.

Jeremiah 9:19
Hear God’s word now, women, and let your ears receive His mouth’s words; teach your daughters lament, and each woman her neighbor a dirge.
  Jeremiah 9:16-17 — same-chapter extension of the call to lament: After summoning skilled women to begin a dirge, the oracle tells women to teach lament to daughters and neighbors. The reference follows the specific movement from calling practiced mourners to spreading their lament through the community; both passages explicitly concern the women and the dirge.

Jeremiah 9:20
For death climbed through your windows and entered your land, to wipe out little children outside and young men from the squares.
  Lamentations 2:21 — death and devastation in public places: Jeremiah pictures death entering the land and wiping out children outside and young men in the squares. Lamentations describes child and elder lying at the way out, virgins and young men in captivity, and killing by sword and famine. Both portray catastrophe in the community's public spaces without assigning every listed fate to the same group.

Jeremiah 9:21
And people’s dead will become an example on the surface of your land’s plain, and like grass behind one reaping, and there will be no one gathering.
  Jeremiah 8:2 — exposed remains without gathering or burial: Jeremiah 9 depicts people's dead as an example upon the land's surface, left like cut grass without a gatherer. Jeremiah 8 uses the same earth-surface disgrace for exposed bones that will not be mourned or buried. The remains arise in different scenes, but both describe bodies deprived of ordinary care.

Jeremiah 9:22
Thus says the Lord: Let no wise man boast in his wisdom, let no strong man boast in his strength, and let no rich man boast in his wealth,
  1 Corinthians 1:26-29 — rejection of boasting in human advantage: Jeremiah forbids boasting in wisdom, strength and wealth. Paul points to the calling of people without worldly wisdom, power or noble birth and says no flesh may boast before God. Both reject human advantage as a sufficient ground for boasting; their lists are not identical.

Jeremiah 9:23
but let whoever boasts boast in this: understanding and knowing that I am the Lord, doing mercy and judgment and righteousness upon the earth, because in these is My will, says the Lord.
  1 Corinthians 1:30-31 — scriptural echo of boasting in the Lord: Jeremiah directs the boaster to understanding and knowing the Lord who acts in mercy, judgment and righteousness. Paul, after describing Christ as wisdom and righteousness from God, introduces as written the instruction to boast in the Lord. The shared redirection of boasting is explicit, without claiming identical wording throughout.

Jeremiah 9:24
Behold, days are coming, says the Lord, and I will visit all who have been circumcised in their foreskins:
  Romans 2:25-29 — outward circumcision does not avert judgment: Jeremiah announces visitation even upon those circumcised in the foreskin. Romans says circumcision benefits the law's practitioner but does not protect a transgressor, and distinguishes outward flesh from the heart. Both deny that the bodily sign by itself settles the person's standing before God.

Jeremiah 9:25
Egypt and Judea and Edom and the sons of Ammon and the sons of Moab and everyone who clips the hair around his face, those dwelling in the wilderness; because all the nations are uncircumcised in flesh, and all Israel’s house are uncircumcised in their hearts.
  Deuteronomy 10:16 — circumcision of the heart: Jeremiah calls Israel's house uncircumcised in heart despite the surrounding discussion of bodily circumcision. Deuteronomy commands circumcision of the hardness of the heart and an end to the stiffened neck. The connection uses the same bodily image for inward responsiveness.

Jeremiah 10:2
Thus says the Lord: Do not learn according to the nations’ ways, and do not fear heaven’s signs, because they fear them before their faces.
  Deuteronomy 4:19 — heavenly bodies and the practices of the nations: Jeremiah warns Israel not to learn the nations' ways or fear heaven's signs as they do. Deuteronomy warns against being led astray by the sun, moon, stars and heavenly array into serving them. Both distinguish Israel's response to the heavens from the religious practices associated with the nations.

Jeremiah 10:3
For the nations’ customs are futile: it is wood cut from the woodland, a carpenter’s work and a cast object.
  Isaiah 44:13-17 — wood transformed by craftsmen into an idol: Jeremiah reduces the nations' object of devotion to woodland timber and a carpenter's work. Isaiah follows wood from cutting and practical use to the remainder being fashioned into a god and worshipped. Both expose the manufactured origin of a supposedly divine object.

Jeremiah 10:4
They are adorned with silver and gold; with hammers and nails they fastened them, and they will not move.
  Isaiah 46:6-7 — precious metal and an immobile handmade object: Jeremiah describes silver and gold adornment and fastening the object so it does not move. Isaiah describes gold and silver given to a goldsmith and an object that remains where people set it. Both contrast costly workmanship with the object's dependence on human handling.

Jeremiah 10:5
They must be lifted and carried, because they will not take a step. Do not fear them, because they certainly will not do evil, and no good is in them.
  Isaiah 46:7 — idols must be carried and cannot save: Jeremiah says the objects must be lifted and carried because they cannot take a step, and have no power for good or evil. Isaiah likewise describes people carrying an object that never moves on its own and cannot answer or save. Both deny the carried image independent agency.

Jeremiah 10:9
It is worked silver; they will not walk. Applied silver will come from Tarshish, gold from Mophaz, and the goldsmiths’ hand; all are artisans’ works. They will dress them in blue and purple.
  Psalms 113:12-15 — metal idols made by human hands cannot walk: Jeremiah names worked silver, gold and artisans while stressing that the objects will not walk. The psalm describes the nations' silver-and-gold idols as human handiwork and says their feet will not walk. The parallel concerns material manufacture and the absence of living capability, not the particular trading places or clothing.

Jeremiah 10:11
This is what you will say to them: Gods who did not make heaven and earth, let them perish from the earth and from beneath this heaven.
  Psalms 95:4-5 — false gods contrasted with the maker of heaven: Jeremiah pronounces the passing away of gods that did not make heaven and earth. The psalm contrasts the nations' gods with the Lord who made the heavens. Creation marks the decisive difference in both passages.

Jeremiah 10:12
The Lord who made the earth by His strength, who set the inhabited world upright by His wisdom, also stretched out heaven by His understanding,
  Proverbs 3:19-20 — creation established by divine wisdom: Jeremiah joins the making of earth and stretching of heaven to the Lord's strength, wisdom and understanding. Proverbs likewise describes earth founded by wisdom and heaven prepared by good sense. Both attribute the ordered world to divine understanding.
  Jeremiah 28:15 — repeated creation proclamation: The two passages repeat the earth made by strength, the inhabited world established by wisdom and heaven stretched by understanding. This is a close internal recurrence in the edition's native Jeremiah numbering, linking the creator proclamation across the two oracles.

Jeremiah 10:13
and an abundance of water in heaven; and He brought clouds up from the earth’s extremity, made lightning for rain, and brought light out of His treasuries.
  Psalms 134:7 — clouds and lightning for rain with a wording distinction: Both passages speak of clouds brought from the earth's extremity and lightning made for rain. The supplied Jeremiah text then says light comes from the treasuries, while the psalm says winds. The shared weather clauses support the comparison without changing either final noun.
  Jeremiah 28:16 — repeated weather proclamation: Jeremiah 28 repeats the heavenly water, clouds from the earth's end, lightning for rain and light from divine treasuries. In this edition both Jeremiah passages say light. The parallel preserves that wording and follows the repeated account of the creator's activity.

Jeremiah 10:14
Every human became foolish, without knowledge; every goldsmith was put to shame at his carved things, because they cast false things; there is no spirit in them.
  Habakkuk 2:18-19 — cast images are false and breathless: Jeremiah calls the goldsmith's cast works false and says there is no spirit in them. Habakkuk likewise calls the cast object a false appearance and says the plated wood or stone has no breath at all. Both expose the lifelessness of the manufactured object despite its maker's confidence.

Jeremiah 10:15
They are futile, mocked works; at the time of their visitation they will perish.
  Jeremiah 28:18 — repeated verdict on futile works: Both passages call the images futile or vain, mocked works that will perish at the time of visitation. The repeated verdict directly follows the same criticism of their manufacture and absence of spirit.

Jeremiah 10:16
Jacob’s portion is not such as this, because the One who formed everything is himself his inheritance; Lord is His name.
  Jeremiah 28:19 — repeated identification of Jacob's inheritance: Both passages distinguish Jacob's portion from the lifeless works because the One who formed everything is Himself his inheritance and is named Lord. The connection retains the direction of inheritance stated in these texts.

Jeremiah 10:18
For thus says the Lord: Behold, I am tripping those dwelling in this land with affliction, so that your wound may be found.
  Jeremiah 37:12-14 — divine affliction pictured as a wound: Jeremiah 10 announces affliction that reveals the people's wound. Jeremiah 37 likewise has the Lord speak of a fracture and painful wound, then identify His blow as discipline for multiplied wrongdoing. Both describe divine judgment through injury imagery; no slinging action is supplied to Jeremiah 10.

Jeremiah 10:19
Woe for your crushing! Your wound is painful. And I said: Truly this is my wound, and it has seized me.
  Jeremiah 37:12-14 — painful wound and lament under discipline: The lament in Jeremiah 10 names a painful wound and acknowledges it as the speaker's own. Jeremiah 37 repeats the painful-wound language and explains the blow as discipline amid sin and abandonment by friends. The connection is the acknowledged injury of judgment, not an assertion that the voices are identical.

Jeremiah 10:20
My tent suffered misery and perished, and all my tent coverings were torn apart. My sons and my sheep are no more; there is no longer a place for my tent, a place for my coverings.
  Isaiah 54:2-3 — contrast between a ruined tent and an enlarged tent: Jeremiah's tent has perished, its coverings are torn and its sons are gone. Isaiah commands widening the tent and curtains because offspring will spread out and settle deserted cities. Both use dwelling and offspring imagery, with the restoration promise reversing the losses lamented in Jeremiah.

Jeremiah 10:21
For the shepherds acted foolishly and did not seek the Lord; therefore all the pasture did not perceive, and they were scattered.
  Ezekiel 34:2-6 — failed shepherds and a scattered flock: Jeremiah brings together foolish shepherds, failure to seek the Lord and scattering. Ezekiel indicts Israel's shepherds for feeding themselves rather than caring for sheep and explicitly describes the flock scattered without anyone seeking it. Both connect failed leadership with dispersal; Jeremiah's compressed pronouns are not expanded into a new subject.

Jeremiah 10:22
A sound of a report—behold, it comes! And a great shaking from the northern land, to lay Judah’s cities waste and make them a resting place for ostriches.
  Jeremiah 1:14-16 — calamity from the north against Judah's cities: The report of a great shaking from the northern land is directed toward the desolation of Judah's cities. Jeremiah's opening vision likewise locates calamity in the north and describes northern kingdoms arrayed against Jerusalem and Judah's cities. The direction, affected region and announced disaster correspond.

Jeremiah 10:23
I know, Lord, that a human’s way does not belong to him, nor will a man go and make his course straight.
  Proverbs 20:24 — human inability to direct one's own course: Jeremiah confesses that a person's way does not belong to him and that he cannot make his own course straight. Proverbs says human steps are directed by the Lord and asks how a mortal could understand his ways. Both qualify human control or understanding of one's path.

Jeremiah 10:24
Instruct us, Lord, but with judgment and not in fury, so that Thou dost not make us few.
  Psalms 6:2 — request for correction without consuming anger: Jeremiah asks the Lord to instruct with judgment rather than fury so that the people are not diminished. The psalm asks not to be rebuked in fury or disciplined in anger. Both seek a restrained form of divine correction rather than its devastating intensity.

Jeremiah 10:25
Pour Thy fury upon nations who do not know Thee and upon generations who did not call on Thy name, because they devoured Jacob and consumed him and made his pasture desolate.
  Psalms 78:6-7 — shared petition against those who devoured Jacob: Both prayers ask that divine fury be poured on nations that do not know the Lord or call on His name, because they devoured Jacob and desolated his place or pasture. The sequence of petition, description and reason makes the correspondence especially close.

Jeremiah 11:2
Hear the words of this covenant. And you will speak to the men of Judah and those dwelling in Jerusalem,
  Deuteronomy 31:11-13 — public hearing of covenant instruction: Jeremiah is to speak the covenant's words to Judah's men and Jerusalem's inhabitants. Deuteronomy orders public reading of the law before the gathered people so that they hear and learn to obey. Both make covenant instruction something delivered to the community in its hearing.

Jeremiah 11:3
and you will say to them: Thus says the Lord, Israel’s God: Cursed is the person who will not hear the words of this covenant,
  Deuteronomy 27:26 — curse upon failure to heed covenant words: Jeremiah pronounces a curse on the person who will not hear the covenant's words. Deuteronomy pronounces a curse on anyone who does not continue in all the law's words to do them. The shared curse attaches to rejection of the covenant's verbal obligations.

Jeremiah 11:4
which I commanded your fathers on the day I brought them up from Egypt’s land, from the iron furnace, saying: Hear My voice and do everything I command you, and you will be My people and I will be your God,
  Deuteronomy 4:20 — Egypt as an iron furnace: Jeremiah recalls the ancestors brought from Egypt, calling it the iron furnace, into a people-and-God relationship. Deuteronomy likewise names Egypt the iron furnace from which God brought Israel to be His people of inheritance. The unusual image and the exodus-to-belonging movement coincide.
  Leviticus 26:12 — covenant people-and-God formula: Jeremiah recalls the command to obey together with the promise that they will be His people and He their God. Leviticus states the same reciprocal relationship in the covenant promises. This connection concerns the explicit covenant formula rather than only a general mention of obedience.

Jeremiah 11:5
so that I may establish My oath that I swore to your fathers, to give them a land flowing with milk and honey, as on this day. And I answered and said: May it be, Lord.
  Exodus 3:8 — promise of a land flowing with milk and honey: Jeremiah connects the oath's establishment with the gift of a land flowing with milk and honey. Exodus presents the rescue from Egypt as movement into that same kind of good, spacious land. The earlier promised destination supplies the background to Jeremiah's acknowledgment of the land now given.
  Exodus 6:8 — land sworn to the ancestors and given as inheritance: Jeremiah speaks of establishing the oath sworn to the fathers by giving the land. Exodus names Abraham, Isaac and Jacob in the pledge to bring their descendants into the land and give it as inheritance. Both explicitly join ancestral pledge and the gift of land.

Jeremiah 11:6
And the Lord said to me: Read these words in Judah’s cities and outside Jerusalem, saying: Hear the words of this covenant and do them.
  Deuteronomy 31:11-13 — reading the law so that the people do it: The command to read the covenant words in Judah's cities and outside Jerusalem ends with hearing and doing them. Deuteronomy's public law reading likewise aims at hearing, learning and doing all its words. The shared purpose explains why proclamation is more than recitation.

Jeremiah 11:8
And they did not do them.
  Jeremiah 11:6 — same-chapter command and noncompliance: The short native unit says that they did not do them. The preceding command in 11:6 supplies the antecedent: the covenant words were to be heard and done. This reference makes the stated failure intelligible without reconstructing the absent native verse 11:7.

Jeremiah 11:10
They returned to the injustices of their earlier fathers, who would not listen to My words; and behold, they walk after foreign gods to serve them. And the house of Israel and the house of Judah shattered My covenant that I established with their fathers.
  Deuteronomy 29:24-26 — covenant broken through serving foreign gods: Jeremiah describes returning to ancestral wrongdoing, refusing to hear and serving foreign gods, thereby shattering the covenant. Deuteronomy likewise explains the land's judgment through forsaking the covenant and serving gods the people had not known. The correspondence connects religious abandonment with covenant breach.

Jeremiah 11:11
Therefore thus says the Lord: Behold, I am bringing evils upon this people from which they will not be able to escape; and they will cry to Me, and I will not listen to them.
  Proverbs 1:24-28 — refused instruction followed by an unanswered cry: Jeremiah says the people will cry during inescapable calamity but the Lord will not listen. Wisdom's speech in Proverbs similarly connects prior refusal of her calls and counsel with a later call that will not be heard amid affliction. The pattern corresponds while the literary speakers remain distinct.

Jeremiah 11:12

  Jeremiah 2:27-28 — challenge to idols to save in affliction: Jeremiah 11 asks whether the gods receiving incense will save their worshippers in trouble. Jeremiah 2 likewise challenges the gods the people made to rise and save them when affliction comes. Both expose the failure of the alternative objects of worship at the moment help is needed.

Jeremiah 11:13

  Jeremiah 2:28 — repeated comparison of gods and altars to city numbers: Both passages say Judah's gods are as numerous as its cities and connect Jerusalem's streets or exits with sacrifice or incense to Baal. The repeated numerical comparison makes the proliferation of worship explicit; the differing location nouns are retained.

Jeremiah 11:14

  Jeremiah 7:16 — repeated prohibition of intercession: In both passages the Lord tells Jeremiah not to pray or petition for this people because He will not listen. Jeremiah 11 specifies their time of affliction, while Jeremiah 7 also forbids approaching on their behalf. The shared command defines the prophet's restricted intercession.

Jeremiah 11:15
Why has the beloved woman committed an abomination in My house? Will prayers and holy meats remove your evils from you, or will you escape by these?
  Isaiah 1:11-15 — ritual and prayer do not remove persistent evil: Jeremiah asks whether prayers and holy meats can remove the beloved woman's evil committed in God's house. Isaiah rejects sacrifices and multiplied supplication when the people's hands are full of blood. Both deny that religious offerings and prayers neutralize ongoing wrongdoing.

Jeremiah 11:16

  Romans 11:17-22 — olive-tree judgment imagery: Jeremiah describes a once beautiful olive tree whose branches become useless under fiery affliction. Romans describes olive branches broken off because of unbelief and warns the remaining hearer not to boast. Both use damaged or removed olive branches to portray judgment within a relationship of belonging, without equating every detail of the images.

Jeremiah 11:17

  Jeremiah 2:21 — divine planting followed by corrupted response: Jeremiah 11 names the Lord as the planter who now speaks evil against Israel and Judah for provoking Him with Baal worship. Jeremiah 2 contrasts God's genuine, fruitful planting with the vine's turn to bitterness and foreign character. Both connect an initially good divine planting with a corrupted human response.

Jeremiah 11:19

  Isaiah 53:7-8 — lamb led to slaughter and removal from the living: Jeremiah compares himself to a guileless lamb led to slaughter while opponents seek to remove him from the land of the living. Isaiah compares the afflicted figure to a sheep led to slaughter and says his life is taken from the earth. The images and threatened removal correspond without identifying the two figures as the same person.

Jeremiah 11:20

  Psalms 7:9-12 — righteous judge who tests hearts and kidneys: Jeremiah appeals to the Lord who judges justly and tests kidneys and hearts while entrusting his claim to Him. The psalm asks for judgment according to innocence and names God as tester of hearts and kidneys and a righteous judge. Both ground a plea against wrongdoing in divine scrutiny.

Jeremiah 11:21

  Amos 7:12-16 — opposition forbidding a prophet to speak: Anathoth's men forbid Jeremiah to prophesy in the Lord's name and threaten death. Both concern attempts to silence a commissioned prophet; Amos's passage does not contain the same death threat.

Jeremiah 11:22
Behold, I will visit them; their young men will die by the sword, and their sons and daughters will end their lives in famine,
  Jeremiah 18:20-23 — judgment on those plotting against Jeremiah: The Lord announces sword for the young men and famine for the children of Jeremiah's opponents. Jeremiah 18 places the same family losses in the prophet's plea after describing plots against his life. The outcomes and persecution context correspond, while one passage is God's announcement and the other Jeremiah's petition.

Jeremiah 11:23
and no remnant of them will remain, because I will bring evils upon those dwelling in Anathoth in the year of their visitation.
  Jeremiah 11:21-22 — same-chapter identification and culmination of judgment: The announcement that no remnant of these opponents will remain completes the judgment on men who threatened Jeremiah and on their households. The earlier verses identify the threat and specify sword and famine. The reference ties the concluding visitation to its identified recipients and cause without enlarging 'of them' to every resident of Anathoth.

Jeremiah 12:1

  Job 21:7-13 — question about the prosperity of the ungodly: Jeremiah asks why the impious prosper and rejecters flourish while addressing the righteous Lord. Job likewise asks why the ungodly live long in wealth and describes flourishing households and children. Both put the visible success of wrongdoers into a questioning dialogue about justice.

Jeremiah 12:2
Thou didst plant them, and they took root; they produced children and bore fruit. Thou art near their mouth and far from their kidneys.
  Isaiah 29:13 — nearness in speech and distance within: Jeremiah says the Lord is near the people's mouth but far from their kidneys. Isaiah says the people honor Him with lips while their heart is far away. Both contrast outward religious speech with inward distance; each passage's own bodily wording is preserved.

Jeremiah 12:3

  Psalms 138:1-3 — God's testing and knowledge of the speaker: Jeremiah appeals to the Lord's knowledge of him and testing of his heart. The psalm begins by saying that the Lord has tested and known the speaker and understood his movements and reasonings. Both ground the address in God's intimate knowledge rather than outward appearance.

Jeremiah 12:4

  Hosea 4:1-3 — wrongdoing brings mourning to land and animals: Jeremiah connects the mournful earth, dried grass and vanished livestock and birds with the inhabitants' evil. Hosea's indictment of the land's wrongdoing likewise leads to the land mourning and the decline of beasts and birds. Both explicitly join human evil to ecological devastation.

Jeremiah 12:6

  Micah 7:5-6 — betrayal by one's own household: Jeremiah is warned that even brothers and his father's house reject him and that favorable words should not be trusted. Micah warns against trusting intimate associates and says a man's enemies belong to his own house. Both locate danger inside expected bonds of family confidence.

Jeremiah 12:7

  Psalms 77:59-62 — abandoned sanctuary and inheritance handed to enemies: The Lord says He has abandoned His house and inheritance and handed His beloved into enemy hands. The psalm describes rejection of Shiloh's tent, strength handed to captivity and the inheritance disregarded. Both portray the loss of divine protection in terms of dwelling, belonging and enemy control.

Jeremiah 12:8

  Hosea 9:15 — divine rejection of a rebellious people: Jeremiah portrays the inheritance roaring against the Lord like a lion and states that He hated her. Hosea likewise connects the people's evil and disobedient rulers with divine hatred and expulsion from His house. The common point is rejection following rebellion; Hosea does not repeat the lion image.

Jeremiah 12:9
Is My inheritance to Me a hyena’s cave, or a cave around her? Go, gather all the field’s wild beasts, and let them come to eat her!

is your strength. And I will give your treasures as plunder, an exchange for all your sins and in all your borders.

Jeremiah 15:19

  Jeremiah 1:18-19 — repeated promise of a bronze wall and rescue: Both passages make Jeremiah a fortified or strong bronze wall against the people and rulers, say that opponents will fight but not prevail, and ground deliverance in the Lord's presence. Jeremiah 16:1

  Deuteronomy 14:1 — baldness made for the dead: Jeremiah says the people will not shave themselves in mourning. Deuteronomy prohibits making a bald place between the eyes for the dead.
