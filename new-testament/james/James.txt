James 1:1
James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, to the twelve tribes in the dispersion: greetings.
  1 Peter 1:1 — epistolary address: Both letters address people in the dispersion. James names the twelve tribes and calls himself a servant; Peter names chosen resident aliens and particular regions. These different addresses are compared without identifying every recipient.
    1 Peter 1:1 Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, to the chosen resident aliens of the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia,

James 1:2
Count it all joy, my brothers, whenever you encounter trials of various kinds,
  Romans 5:3–5:4 — joy amid testing: James calls for joy amid varied trials; Romans speaks of boasting in afflictions because affliction produces perseverance. The shared response is grounded in the stated endurance/perseverance-producing process, with the different circumstances preserved.
    Romans 5:3 And not only this, but we also boast in afflictions, knowing that affliction produces perseverance,
    Romans 5:4 and perseverance, proven character, and proven character, hope.

James 1:3
knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance.
  Romans 5:3–5:4 — endurance through testing: James says the testing of faith produces endurance. Romans connects affliction, perseverance and proven character in its own sequence; the comparison does not silently exchange testing and proven character.
    Romans 5:3 And not only this, but we also boast in afflictions, knowing that affliction produces perseverance,
    Romans 5:4 and perseverance, proven character, and proven character, hope.

James 1:4
And let endurance have its complete work, so that you may be complete and whole, lacking in nothing.
  Romans 5:4–5:5 — maturing endurance: James seeks endurance completing its work so the readers lack nothing. Romans continues from perseverance to proven character and hope. Both give endurance a further constructive outcome, while describing that outcome differently.
    Romans 5:4 and perseverance, proven character, and proven character, hope.
    Romans 5:5 And hope does not put us to shame, because God’s love has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us.

James 1:5
But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives to all generously and without reproach, and it will be given to him.
  Matthew 7:7–7:11 — asking the giving Father: James directs those lacking wisdom to ask the generous God. Jesus teaches asking and receiving and contrasts human gifts with the Father giving good things. Wisdom is James’s particular requested gift, not the Gospel’s only category.
    Matthew 7:7 Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you.
    Matthew 7:8 For everyone who asks receives, the one who seeks finds, and to the one who knocks it will be opened.
    Matthew 7:9 Or who among you, if his son asks for bread, will give him a stone?
    Matthew 7:10 Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a snake?
    Matthew 7:11 If you, then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in the heavens give good things to those who ask Him!

James 1:6
But let him ask in faith, doubting nothing, for the one who doubts is like a wave of the sea, driven and tossed by the wind.
  Matthew 21:21–21:22 — asking without doubt: Both passages connect believing prayer with the refusal to doubt. James adds the wind-driven wave image; Matthew uses the mountain and fig tree. Neither image is substituted into the other passage.
    Matthew 21:21 Jesus answered them, “Truly I tell you, if you have faith and do not doubt, you will do not only what was done to the fig tree; even if you say to this mountain, ‘Be lifted up and cast into the sea,’ it will happen.
    Matthew 21:22 Everything you ask in prayer, believing, you will receive.”

James 1:7
For that person must not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord,
  James 4:3 — unreceived requests: The doubting requester is told not to expect receipt from the Lord; later James says some requests fail because they aim at pleasures. These are distinct stated obstacles to receiving, not interchangeable accusations against every petitioner.
    James 4:3 You ask and do not receive, because you ask badly, so that you may spend it on your pleasures.

James 1:8
a man of divided mind, unstable in all his ways.
  James 4:8 — divided mind: The description of divided mind recurs when James calls those of divided mind to purify their hearts. The later command supplies a concrete corrective to the instability described here without identifying the same individual.
    James 4:8 Draw near to God, and He will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, sinners, and purify your hearts, you of divided mind.

James 1:9
Let the lowly brother boast in his exaltation,
  Luke 1:52 — exaltation of the lowly: James permits the lowly brother to boast in exaltation; Mary’s song describes the lifting up of the lowly. The comparison concerns the reversal of low status, not an identification of the brother with a person in the song.
    Luke 1:52 He brought rulers down from thrones and raised up the lowly.

James 1:10
and the rich person in his humiliation, because he will pass away like a flower of grass.
  1 Peter 1:24 — grass-flower transience: James compares the passing rich person with a grass flower. Peter’s scriptural grass-and-flower comparison applies to all flesh and its glory. The broader target image supports the source comparison without restricting Peter to wealthy people.
    1 Peter 1:24 For “All flesh is like grass, and all its glory like a flower of grass. The grass withered, and the flower fell,

James 1:11
For the sun rose with its scorching heat and dried the grass, and its flower fell, and the beauty of its appearance perished. So also the rich person will wither in his pursuits.
  1 Peter 1:24–1:25 — withering flower: Both describe grass drying and its flower falling. James applies the image to the rich person’s pursuits; Peter contrasts mortal transience with the enduring word, so the two applications remain distinct.
    1 Peter 1:24 For “All flesh is like grass, and all its glory like a flower of grass. The grass withered, and the flower fell,
    1 Peter 1:25 but the word of the Lord remains forever.” And this is the word that was proclaimed to you as good news.

James 1:12
Blessed is the man who endures a trial, because, having been approved, he will receive the crown of life that He promised to those who love Him.
  James 2:5 — promise to those loving God: Both speak of a divine promise to those who love Him. Here the approved endurance receives the crown of life; later the poor chosen rich in faith inherit the kingdom. The different reward descriptions are preserved.
    James 2:5 Listen, my beloved brothers: has not God chosen those poor in the world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom He promised to those who love Him?

James 1:13
Let no one who is tempted say, “I am being tempted by God.” For God cannot be tempted by evil, and He Himself tempts no one.
  1 Corinthians 10:13 — God and temptation: James denies that God is tempted by evil or tempts anyone. Paul describes God’s faithfulness in limiting temptation and providing an escape for endurance. The target concerns God’s help within temptation, not God enticing people to evil.
    1 Corinthians 10:13 No temptation has seized you except what is common to humanity. But God is faithful: He will not allow you to be tempted beyond what you can bear, but with the temptation will also provide the way out, so that you may be able to endure it.

James 1:14
But each person is tempted when drawn away and enticed by his own desire.
  Mark 7:21–7:23 — evil arising within: James locates enticement in a person’s own desire. Jesus lists evil things coming from within the human heart and defiling a person. The common inward source does not make every desire identical with each listed offense.
    Mark 7:21 For from within, out of people’s hearts, come evil reasonings, acts of sexual immorality, thefts, murders,
    Mark 7:22 adulteries, acts of greed, wickedness, deceit, licentiousness, an evil eye, slander, arrogance, foolishness.
    Mark 7:23 All these evil things come out from within and defile the person.”

James 1:15
Then desire, after conceiving, gives birth to sin; and sin, when fully grown, gives birth to death.
  Romans 6:23 — sin and death: James traces desire conceiving sin and matured sin bringing death. Romans names death as sin’s wages and contrasts God’s gift of life. The target supports the stated sin–death outcome without reproducing James’s birth sequence.
    Romans 6:23 For sin’s wages are death, but God’s gift is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.

James 1:16
Do not be led astray, my beloved brothers.
  James 1:13–1:15 — warning against a false account: The warning not to be led astray follows the denial that God tempts and the explanation of desire, sin and death. This retained passage states the particular account against which the warning must be read.
    James 1:13 Let no one who is tempted say, “I am being tempted by God.” For God cannot be tempted by evil, and He Himself tempts no one.
    James 1:14 But each person is tempted when drawn away and enticed by his own desire.
    James 1:15 Then desire, after conceiving, gives birth to sin; and sin, when fully grown, gives birth to death.

James 1:17
Every good act of giving and every complete gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow from turning.
  Matthew 7:11 — good gifts from the Father: James identifies the Father of lights as the source of good and complete giving. Jesus says the heavenly Father gives good things to those asking. James’s denial of variation adds a claim not stated in the target.
    Matthew 7:11 If you, then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in the heavens give good things to those who ask Him!

James 1:18
By His will He gave us birth through the word of truth, so that we might be a kind of firstfruits of His creatures.
  1 Peter 1:23 — birth through the word: James says He gave us birth through the word of truth by His will; Peter describes being born anew through God’s living, enduring word. The shared word-mediated birth is compared without replacing James’s firstfruits image.
    1 Peter 1:23 having been born anew, not from perishable seed but from imperishable, through the living and enduring word of God.

James 1:19
Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger,
  James 3:2 — restraint in speech: The command to be slow to speak corresponds to the later account of not stumbling in speech and bridling the body. Hearing and anger remain additional commands in the source, rather than claims supplied by the target.
    James 3:2 For we all stumble in many things. If anyone does not stumble in speech, that person is a complete man, able to bridle his whole body also.

James 1:20
for a man’s anger does not accomplish the righteousness of God.
  Ephesians 4:26–4:27 — anger and righteous conduct: James denies that human anger accomplishes God’s righteousness. Ephesians commands anger without sin, a limit on its duration and no opportunity for the devil. These are related restraints, not identical statements that all anger is already sinful.
    Ephesians 4:26 Be angry and do not sin. Do not let the sun go down upon your anger,
    Ephesians 4:27 nor give a place to the devil.

James 1:21
Therefore, putting away every impurity and the abundance of evil, receive with gentleness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls.
  1 Peter 2:1–2:2 — putting away evil and receiving nourishment: Both begin by putting away named evils and then direct readers to receive sustaining instruction or nourishment. James calls the implanted word able to save souls; Peter uses the image of newborn infants desiring milk.
    1 Peter 2:1 Therefore, putting away all malice and all deceit and hypocrisies and envies and all slanders,
    1 Peter 2:2 like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk, so that by it you may grow into salvation,

James 1:22
But become doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves.
  Matthew 7:24–7:27 — hearing and doing: James contrasts doing the word with self-deceiving hearing alone. Jesus contrasts hearers who do His words with hearers who do not, using houses with different foundations. The target supplies an explicit enactment of the same hearing–doing distinction.
    Matthew 7:24 Therefore everyone who hears these words of Mine and puts them into practice will be likened to a prudent man who built his house upon the rock.
    Matthew 7:25 The rain came down, the rivers came, and the winds blew and fell upon that house, and it did not fall, for it had been founded upon the rock.
    Matthew 7:26 And everyone who hears these words of Mine and does not put them into practice will be likened to a foolish man who built his house upon the sand.
    Matthew 7:27 The rain came down, the rivers came, and the winds blew and struck that house, and it fell; and its fall was great.”

James 1:23
For if anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer, he is like a man looking at his natural face in a mirror,
  Luke 6:46–6:49 — the hearer who does not do: James’s mirror example begins with hearing without doing. Luke likewise contrasts hearing and doing with hearing without doing, after the challenge about calling Jesus Lord while not doing what He says. The illustrations remain different.
    Luke 6:46 Why do you call Me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and not do what I say?
    Luke 6:47 Everyone who comes to Me, hears My words and does them—I will show you whom he resembles.
    Luke 6:48 He resembles a man building a house, who dug and went deep and laid a foundation on the rock. When a flood came, the river burst against that house but could not shake it, because it had been well built.
    Luke 6:49 But the one who heard and did not act resembles a man who built a house on the ground without a foundation. The river burst against it, and immediately it collapsed; the ruin of that house was great.”

James 1:24
for he looked at himself and went away and immediately forgot what he was like.
  Luke 6:49 — failure after hearing: The mirror viewer goes away and forgets; Luke’s hearer fails to act and the house falls. The comparison is the ineffective result of an unacted-on encounter, not a claim that Luke contains a mirror or forgetfulness.
    Luke 6:49 But the one who heard and did not act resembles a man who built a house on the ground without a foundation. The river burst against it, and immediately it collapsed; the ruin of that house was great.”

James 1:25
But the one who looks closely into the complete law, the law of freedom, and continues in it—not a forgetful hearer but a doer of the work—will be blessed in what he does.
  John 8:31–8:32 — continuing and freedom: James joins continuing in the law of freedom with doing and blessing. Jesus joins remaining in His word with discipleship, knowing truth and freedom. The target does not rename every occurrence of law as the Gospel saying.
    John 8:31 So Jesus said to the Jews who had believed Him, “If you remain in My word, you are truly My disciples,
    John 8:32 and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.”

James 1:26
If anyone thinks he is religious while not bridling his tongue but deceiving his heart, that person’s religion is futile.
  James 3:9–3:10 — religious speech contradicted: James calls unbridled speech incompatible with worthwhile religion. Later he exposes the same mouth blessing the Lord and cursing people as conduct that ought not occur. The concrete contradiction illustrates the source’s warning about the tongue.
    James 3:9 With it we bless the Lord and Father, and with it we curse human beings who have been made in God’s likeness.
    James 3:10 From the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, these things ought not to happen this way.

James 1:27
Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to care for orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained by the world.
  Isaiah 1:16–1:17 — purity and care for the vulnerable: James combines undefiled religion with care for orphans and widows and freedom from worldly stain. Isaiah combines washing away evil with seeking justice for orphan and widow. The shared ethical pattern includes both cleansing and protection.
    Isaiah 1:16 Wash yourselves, become clean. Remove the evils from your souls, before My eyes. Stop your evil deeds.
    Isaiah 1:17 Learn to do good, seek judgment, rescue the wronged. Give judgment for the orphan and vindicate the widow.

James 2:1
My brothers, do not hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with acts of favoritism.
  Romans 2:11 — refusal of favoritism: James prohibits acts of favoritism within faith in the Lord Jesus Christ. Romans states that there is no partiality with God. The target provides the divine impartiality relevant to the source’s command, not a description of its gathering.
    Romans 2:11 For there is no partiality with God.

James 2:2
For if a man with gold rings and splendid clothing enters your gathering, and a poor person in filthy clothing also enters,
  Luke 14:12–14:14 — wealth and access to a gathering: James contrasts a splendidly dressed rich entrant with a poorly clothed entrant. Jesus contrasts inviting rich neighbors with inviting the poor and disabled. Both expose the treatment of people at gatherings according to material advantage, with different situations.
    Luke 14:12 He also said to the one who had invited Him, “When you give a midday meal or a supper, do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors, lest they also invite you in return and you receive repayment.
    Luke 14:13 But when you give a reception, invite the poor, the disabled, the lame, and the blind.
    Luke 14:14 And you will be blessed, because they have nothing with which to repay you; for you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.”

James 2:3
and you pay attention to the one wearing splendid clothing and say, “You sit here in a good place,” but say to the poor person, “You stand, or sit there beneath my footstool,”
  Leviticus 19:15 — partiality toward rich or poor: James describes honored seating for the splendidly dressed and humiliating directions to the poor. Leviticus forbids partiality to poor or powerful in judgment. The source’s seating example is a particular discriminatory practice, not Leviticus’s identical setting.
    Leviticus 19:15 You shall do no injustice in judgment; you shall not favour the person of the poor, neither shall you admire the person of the mighty; you shall judge your neighbour in righteousness.

James 2:4
have you not made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?
  Leviticus 19:15 — unjust discrimination: James calls the discriminators judges with evil thoughts. Leviticus commands righteous judgment without partiality to poor or powerful. This directly compares the condemned kind of judgment with the prescribed impartial judgment.
    Leviticus 19:15 You shall do no injustice in judgment; you shall not favour the person of the poor, neither shall you admire the person of the mighty; you shall judge your neighbour in righteousness.

James 2:5
Listen, my beloved brothers: has not God chosen those poor in the world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom He promised to those who love Him?
  Luke 6:20 — the poor and the kingdom: James speaks of God choosing the poor to be rich in faith and heirs of the promised kingdom. Jesus pronounces the poor blessed because the kingdom of God is theirs. James’s added faith and love language is retained as its own formulation.
    Luke 6:20 Lifting His eyes toward His disciples, He said, “Blessed are you who are poor, because yours is God’s kingdom.

James 2:6
But you have dishonored the poor person. Is it not the rich who oppress you and drag you into courts?
  James 5:4–5:6 — rich oppressors: The accusation that the rich oppress and drag people into courts is complemented by James’s later charges of withheld wages, condemnation and murder. The later passage supplies concrete abuses without identifying every wealthy person with one named defendant.
    James 5:4 Look, the wages of the laborers who reaped your fields, withheld by you, cry out; and the cries of the harvesters have entered the ears of the Lord Sabaoth.
    James 5:5 You lived luxuriously on the earth and indulged yourselves; you fattened your hearts on a day of slaughter.
    James 5:6 You condemned; you murdered the righteous one. He does not oppose you.

James 2:7
Do they not blaspheme the good name called over you?
  Romans 2:24 — the name blasphemed: James accuses the rich oppressors of blaspheming the good name called over the readers. Romans describes God’s name blasphemed among nations because of others’ conduct. Both connect a divine name with dishonoring behavior, but assign the roles differently.
    Romans 2:24 For “the name of God is blasphemed among the nations because of you,” just as it is written.

James 2:8
If, however, you fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,” you do well.
  Leviticus 19:18 — scriptural quotation: James quotes the command to love one’s neighbor as oneself as the royal law. Leviticus supplies that command in its own context of rejecting vengeance and resentment. The quotation does not turn favoritism into acceptable neighbor-love.
    Leviticus 19:18 And your hand shall not take vengeance, neither shall you remain angry with the sons of your people; and you shall love your neighbour as yourself; I am the Lord.

James 2:9
But if you show favoritism, you commit sin and are convicted by the law as transgressors.
  Leviticus 19:15 — favoritism as transgression: James says favoritism is sin exposed by the law. Leviticus expressly prohibits partiality and requires righteous judgment. The retained prohibition provides concrete legal content rather than a merely general reference to wrongdoing.
    Leviticus 19:15 You shall do no injustice in judgment; you shall not favour the person of the poor, neither shall you admire the person of the mighty; you shall judge your neighbour in righteousness.

James 2:10
For whoever keeps the whole law but stumbles in one point has become accountable for all of it.
  Galatians 3:10 — the obligation to the whole law: James reasons that stumbling at one point makes the keeper accountable for all. Galatians cites the requirement to continue in all things written in the law. Their particular arguments differ, while both reject selective treatment of its obligations.
    Galatians 3:10 For all who are of works of law are under a curse, for it is written, “Accursed is everyone who does not abide by all the things written in the book of the law, to do them.”

James 2:11
For He who said, “Do not commit adultery,” also said, “Do not murder.” Now if you do not commit adultery but do murder, you have become a transgressor of the law.
  Romans 13:9 — adultery and murder commands: Both passages expressly list the prohibitions of adultery and murder. James uses their common speaker to explain transgression despite selective obedience; Romans gathers commandments under neighbor-love. The shared commands support comparison without merging those arguments.
    Romans 13:9 For “You shall not commit adultery,” “You shall not murder,” “You shall not steal,” “You shall not desire,” and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this word: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

James 2:12
So speak and so act as those who are going to be judged through a law of freedom.
  James 1:25 — law of freedom: The command to speak and act as persons judged through a law of freedom recalls the earlier law of freedom whose continuing doer is blessed. The two passages connect this law with conduct rather than bare hearing.
    James 1:25 But the one who looks closely into the complete law, the law of freedom, and continues in it—not a forgetful hearer but a doer of the work—will be blessed in what he does.

James 2:13
For judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy; mercy triumphs over judgment.
  Matthew 6:14–6:15 — mercy and judgment: James warns of judgment without mercy for the person who showed none. Jesus links receiving forgiveness with forgiving others. Mercy and forgiveness are compared as treatment of others bearing on one’s own judgment, without treating the terms as identical.
    Matthew 6:14 For if you forgive people their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you.
    Matthew 6:15 But if you do not forgive people, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.

James 2:14
What good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him?
  Matthew 7:21 — profession without action: James questions whether a claimed faith lacking works can save. Jesus distinguishes saying Lord from doing the Father’s will when speaking of entering the kingdom. Both challenge an unaccompanied profession, without erasing their different formulations.
    Matthew 7:21 Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of the heavens, but the one who does the will of My Father in the heavens.

James 2:15
If a brother or sister is without clothing and lacks daily food,
  1 John 3:17 — a fellow believer in material need: James names a brother or sister lacking clothing and daily food. John describes seeing a brother in need while possessing material resources. The target makes explicit the obligation presented by such a concrete need, not a different spiritualized lack.
    1 John 3:17 But whoever has this world’s goods and sees his brother in need and closes his heart against him, how does the love of God abide in him?

James 2:16
and one of you says to them, “Go in peace; be warmed and filled,” but you do not give them what the body needs, what good is it?
  1 John 3:17–3:18 — words without material help: James rejects a warm farewell without supplying bodily necessities. John rejects closing compassion to a needy brother and calls for love in deed and truth rather than speech alone. Both expose words failing to meet material need.
    1 John 3:17 But whoever has this world’s goods and sees his brother in need and closes his heart against him, how does the love of God abide in him?
    1 John 3:18 Little children, let us not love in word or with the tongue, but in deed and truth.

James 2:17
So also faith, if it does not have works, is dead by itself.
  Galatians 5:6 — faith expressed in action: James calls faith without works dead by itself. Galatians describes faith working through love. The target offers an explicitly active description of faith, while the differing circumcision setting and James’s dead-faith judgment are not silently equated.
    Galatians 5:6 For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any strength, but faith working through love.

James 2:18
But someone will say, “You have faith, and I have works.” Show me your faith apart from works, and I will show you faith by my works.
  Matthew 7:16–7:20 — visible evidence of an inward claim: James challenges the display of faith apart from works and offers to show it by works. Jesus uses fruit to recognize trees and false prophets. The comparison concerns evidence in conduct, not an assertion that the target uses James’s faith terminology.
    Matthew 7:16 You will recognize them by their fruits. Do people gather grapes from thornbushes, or figs from thistles?
    Matthew 7:17 In the same way, every good tree bears good fruit, but the rotten tree bears evil fruit.
    Matthew 7:18 A good tree cannot bear evil fruit, nor can a rotten tree bear good fruit.
    Matthew 7:19 Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into fire.
    Matthew 7:20 So then, you will recognize them by their fruits.

James 2:19
You believe that God is one. You do well. Even the demons believe—and shudder.
  Deuteronomy 6:4 — confession of one God: James’s affirmation that God is one recalls the confession concerning the Lord in Deuteronomy. James adds that even demons believe and shudder; that added warning is not represented as part of Deuteronomy’s words.
    Deuteronomy 6:4 And these are the ordinances and judgments which the Lord commanded the sons of Israel in the wilderness, when they came forth out of the land of Egypt: Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one.

James 2:20
But do you want to know, empty man, that faith apart from works is idle?
  Ephesians 2:8–2:10 — faith and the place of works: James calls faith apart from works idle. Ephesians distinguishes salvation as God’s gift through faith from boasting in works, then describes creation in Christ for good works. The comparison preserves this ordering rather than claiming both sentences say the same thing.
    Ephesians 2:8 For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not from yourselves; it is the gift of God,
    Ephesians 2:9 not from works, so that no one may boast.
    Ephesians 2:10 For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, so that we should walk in them.

James 2:21
Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered Isaac his son upon the altar?
  Genesis 22:9–22:12 — Abraham offering Isaac: James refers to Abraham offering Isaac on the altar. Genesis narrates the altar, the binding and Abraham’s action before the divine interruption. James’s justification language is its interpretation, not wording inserted into Genesis.
    Genesis 22:9 and came to the place of which God had spoken to him. And Abraham built an altar there, and laid the wood upon it, and bound together the feet of Isaac his son, and laid him upon the altar, upon the wood.
    Genesis 22:10 And Abraham stretched out his hand to take the knife, to slaughter his son.
    Genesis 22:11 And an angel of the Lord called to him out of the heaven, and said to him, “Abraham, Abraham.” And he said, “Behold, here am I.”
    Genesis 22:12 And he said, “Do not lay your hand upon the lad or do anything to him; for now I know that you fear God, and have not spared your beloved son for my sake.”

James 2:22
You see that faith was working together with his works, and by the works faith was brought to completion,
  Hebrews 11:17–11:19 — faith acting in the offering: James says Abraham’s faith worked with his works and was completed through them. Hebrews explicitly describes offering Isaac by faith and Abraham’s reasoning about resurrection. These are distinct interpretations of the same offering, with neither reduced to the other.
    Hebrews 11:17 By faith Abraham, when tested, offered Isaac; and the one who had welcomed the promises was offering his only son,
    Hebrews 11:18 to whom it had been said, “In Isaac offspring will be named for you.”
    Hebrews 11:19 He reasoned that God is able even to raise the dead; from there he also received him back, figuratively speaking.

James 2:23
and the Scripture was fulfilled that says, “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness,” and he was called a friend of God.
  Genesis 15:6 — scriptural quotation: James repeats Abraham’s believing God and its reckoning as righteousness. Genesis names him Abram and contains that statement in the promise narrative. James’s additional friend-of-God title is not falsely included in the retained Genesis verse.
    Genesis 15:6 And Abram believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness.

James 2:24
You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone.
  Romans 3:28 — different formulations of faith and works: James denies justification by faith alone and speaks of works; Romans speaks of justification by faith apart from works of law. The comparison preserves both formulations and their different qualifications rather than silently removing alone or of law.
    Romans 3:28 For we reckon that a person is justified by faith apart from works of law.

James 2:25
And likewise was not Rahab the prostitute also justified by works, when she welcomed the messengers and sent them out by another way?
  Joshua 2:15–2:16 — Rahab enabling escape: James cites Rahab welcoming the messengers and sending them by another way. James 5:16
Therefore confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, so that you may be healed. A righteous person’s petition has great strength when it is at work.
  Psalms 31:5 — confession and forgiveness: James commands mutual confession and prayer for healing. The Psalm describes acknowledging sin to the Lord and forgiveness. The confession–forgiveness connection is retained while the Psalm’s address to God is not rewritten as mutual confession between people.
    Psalms 31:5 I made my sin known, and I did not cover my lawlessness. I said, “I will confess my lawlessness against myself to the Lord,” and Thou forgavest the impiety of my sin. Diapsalma.

James 5:17
Elijah was a human being with experiences like ours, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain; and it did not rain upon the earth for three years and six months.
  Luke 4:25 — Elijah’s three-and-a-half-year drought: James explicitly gives three years and six months without rain after Elijah’s prayer. Jesus likewise names Elijah’s days and heaven shut for three years and six months. Luke’s statement supports the duration without supplying every detail of the prayer.
    Luke 4:25 But in truth I tell you, there were many widows in Israel in Elijah’s days, when heaven was shut for three years and six months and a great famine came over the whole land.

James 5:18
And he prayed again, and heaven gave rain, and the earth brought forth its fruit.
  1 Kings 18:42–18:45 — rain after Elijah’s appeal: James says Elijah prayed again and heaven gave rain. Kings narrates Elijah bowing, repeated reports from his servant, a cloud and abundant rain. The narrative supports the rain’s return without inventing a printed verbatim prayer.
    1 Kings 18:42 Ahab went up to eat and drink. Elijah went up onto Carmel, bent down to the ground, and put his face between his knees.
    1 Kings 18:43 He said to his young attendant, “Go up and look toward the sea.” The young attendant looked and said, “There is nothing.” Elijah said, “Go back seven times.” The young attendant went back seven times.

James 5:19
My brothers, if anyone among you is led astray from the truth and someone turns him back,
  Galatians 6:1 — restoring the straying person: James considers someone led astray from truth and another turning him back. Galatians directs restoring a person caught in a transgression with gentleness and self-watchfulness. The common restorative act does not erase the differing descriptions of the offense.
    Galatians 6:1 Brothers, even if a person is overtaken in some transgression, you who are spiritual are to restore such a person in a spirit of gentleness, watching yourself, lest you too be tempted.

James 5:20
let him know that the one who turns a sinner back from the error of his way will save his soul from death and cover a multitude of sins.
  1 Peter 4:8 — covering a multitude of sins: James associates turning back a sinner with saving his soul from death and covering many sins. Peter says love covers a multitude of sins. The shared covering phrase is retained without deciding an unprinted identity for the possessive his or equating every context.
    1 Peter 4:8 Above all, maintain earnest love for one another, because love covers a multitude of sins.
