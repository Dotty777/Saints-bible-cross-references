Philemon 1:1
Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy the brother, to Philemon, our beloved fellow worker,
  Colossians 1:1 — thematic parallel: Both openings name Paul and Timothy, with Timothy described as the brother. Philemon calls Paul a prisoner of Christ Jesus and addresses the beloved fellow worker; Colossians calls him an apostle through God's will. The different titles and receiving communities remain distinct.
    Colossians 1:1 Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus through the will of God, and Timothy our brother,

Philemon 1:2
and to Apphia the sister, and Archippus our fellow soldier, and the Church in your house:
  Colossians 4:17 — thematic parallel: Both name Archippus in a ministry relationship. Philemon addresses him as fellow soldier alongside Apphia and a house Church; Colossians urges him to fulfill service received in the Lord. Neither a particular office nor ownership of the house or Apphia's family relationship is supplied by this comparison.
    Colossians 4:17 And say to Archippus, “Attend to the service which you received in the Lord, so that you may fulfill it.”

Philemon 1:3
Grace to you all and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.
  2 Corinthians 1:2 — thematic parallel: Both greet the recipients with grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Philemon explicitly says you all; Corinthians gives its own greeting to its wider named community. The repeated formula retains the different address.
    2 Corinthians 1:2 Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Philemon 1:4
I thank my God always, remembering you in my prayers,
  1 Thessalonians 1:2 — thematic parallel: Both join ongoing thanks to God with mentioning the recipients in prayer. Philemon uses I, my God and my prayers; Thessalonians uses we, all of you and our prayers. The shared thanksgiving-prayer formula does not merge the speakers or communities.
    1 Thessalonians 1:2 We always give thanks to God for all of you, mentioning you in our prayers, unceasingly

Philemon 1:5
hearing of your love and the faith that you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,
  Colossians 1:4 — thematic parallel: Both report hearing of love and faith with the Lord Jesus or Christ Jesus and all the saints named. Colossians explicitly distributes faith in Christ and love toward saints; Philemon has its own paired love/faith and toward phrases. Its different order and syntax are not forced into an exclusive distribution.
    Colossians 1:4 having heard of your faith in Christ Jesus and the love you have toward all the saints,

Philemon 1:6
that the fellowship of your faith may become effective through recognition of every good thing among us for Christ.
  Galatians 5:6 — thematic parallel: Both describe active or effective faith in relation to Christ. Galatians names faith working through love; Philemon prays that faith's fellowship become effective through recognition of every good thing among us for Christ. The stated means differ, and the actual among-us wording remains intact.
    Galatians 5:6 For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any strength, but faith working through love.

Philemon 1:7
For I had great joy and encouragement because of your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you, brother.
  2 Corinthians 7:13 — thematic parallel: Both connect joy and encouragement or comfort with refreshment received through the addressees. Corinthians names Titus's joy and refreshed spirit; Philemon names the saints' refreshed hearts and the writer's joy because of the recipient's love. The beneficiaries and heart/spirit wording remain distinct.
    2 Corinthians 7:13 For this reason we have been comforted. And beyond our comfort, we rejoiced much more at the joy of Titus, because his spirit has been refreshed by all of you.

Philemon 1:8
Therefore, although I have great boldness in Christ to command you to do what is fitting,
  Philemon 1:9 — internal thematic parallel: The although-command clause leads into the next unit's appeal instead because of love. That internal contrast explains the writer's chosen manner of asking; the next unit also calls Paul an old man and prisoner. The stated capacity to command is not turned into a report of a compulsory order actually imposed.
    Philemon 1:9 I appeal instead because of love, being as I am, Paul, an old man and now also a prisoner of Christ Jesus—

Philemon 1:9
I appeal instead because of love, being as I am, Paul, an old man and now also a prisoner of Christ Jesus—
  2 Corinthians 8:8 — thematic parallel: Both frame an appeal or statement through love rather than issuing a command. Corinthians describes testing love's genuineness through others' diligence; Philemon appeals because of love after mentioning boldness to command. Its old-man and prisoner descriptions and the different matters being requested remain its own.
    2 Corinthians 8:8 I do not speak as a command, but through the diligence of others I also test the genuineness of your love.

Philemon 1:10
I appeal to you for my child Onesimus, whose father I became while in chains,
  Colossians 4:9 — thematic parallel: Both explicitly name Onesimus in relation to the writer's ministry. Philemon calls him my child and says the writer became his father while in chains; Colossians calls him faithful and beloved brother and one of the recipients. The named figure is connected without supplying an unprinted route, conversion scene or dispatch date.
    Colossians 4:9 with Onesimus, the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They will make known to you all the things here.

Philemon 1:11
who was once useless to you, but is now useful both to you and to me.
  Philemon 1:10 — internal thematic parallel: The preceding named Onesimus supplies the antecedent of who was once useless and is now useful. That unit identifies the person through the writer's child/father relationship in chains; the present unit describes changed usefulness. The internal antecedent link does not reconstruct past wrongdoing or assert an unverified name etymology.
    Philemon 1:10 I appeal to you for my child Onesimus, whose father I became while in chains,

Philemon 1:12
I have sent him back to you—him, that is, my very heart.
  Philemon 1:17 — internal thematic parallel: Calling the man being sent back my very heart relates to the later request to welcome him as the writer himself. Both give the same letter's personal identification of Onesimus with Paul's concern or standing. The dispatch statement and reception request remain different acts rather than proof a later welcome occurred.
    Philemon 1:17 So if you regard me as a partner, welcome him as you would me.

Philemon 1:13
I wanted to keep him with me, so that in your place he might serve me in my chains for the gospel.
  Philippians 2:25 — thematic parallel: Both describe service to the writer on the recipients' behalf. Philippians calls Epaphroditus their messenger and minister to Paul's need; Philemon says he wanted Onesimus kept so he might serve in the recipient's place during chains for the gospel. The named people and described role versus proposed service are kept distinct.
    Philippians 2:25 But I considered it necessary to send to you Epaphroditus, my brother and fellow worker and fellow soldier, and your messenger and minister to my need,

Philemon 1:14
But without your consent I did not want to do anything, so that your good deed might be voluntary, rather than as though compelled.
  2 Corinthians 9:7 — thematic parallel: Both reject compulsion in a good act of assistance. Corinthians tells each to give as decided in the heart, not from sorrow or compulsion; Philemon says he did not want to act without consent so the good deed could be voluntary. The contribution and consent concerning Onesimus remain different situations.
    2 Corinthians 9:7 Let each give as he has decided in his heart, not out of sorrow or compulsion; for God loves a cheerful giver.

Philemon 1:15
For perhaps he was separated from you for a little while for this reason: that you might have him back forever,
  Philemon 1:16 — internal thematic parallel: The next clause specifies the manner of the proposed lasting reception: no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother. It continues the perhaps-purpose statement about temporary separation and having him back forever. The tentative perhaps remains, without an asserted reason for departure or a completed legal emancipation.
    Philemon 1:16 no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother, especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord.

Philemon 1:16
no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother, especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord.
  Colossians 4:9 — thematic parallel: The beloved-brother description of Onesimus has an explicit named counterpart in Colossians. Colossians also calls him faithful and one of the readers; Philemon places brotherhood beyond the slave relation and names flesh and Lord. The additional relational and service descriptions are retained separately.
    Colossians 4:9 with Onesimus, the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They will make known to you all the things here.

Philemon 1:17
So if you regard me as a partner, welcome him as you would me.
  Matthew 10:40 — thematic parallel: Both relate receiving someone to receiving the person represented. Matthew has Jesus say receiving the messengers receives Him and His sender; Philemon conditionally asks a partner to welcome Onesimus as Paul himself. The parties and authority relationships are different, not an identification of Onesimus with Jesus.
    Matthew 10:40 Whoever receives you receives Me, and whoever receives Me receives the One who sent Me.

Philemon 1:18
And if he has wronged you in anything, or owes you anything, charge that to my account.
  Philemon 1:19 — internal thematic parallel: The following own-hand promise to repay supplies the immediate undertaking behind charge that to my account. This internal debt-and-payment connection preserves the source's if he wronged or owes anything; it does not establish an actual theft, debt amount or offense.
    Philemon 1:19 I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it—not to mention that you owe me even your own self.

Philemon 1:19
I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it—not to mention that you owe me even your own self.
  Luke 10:35 — thematic parallel: Both include a personal undertaking to repay on another person's behalf. Luke's caregiver promises the innkeeper repayment for further expenditure at his return; Paul writes his repayment promise and adds the recipient's owing him his own self. The expense, conditional debt and self-owing reminder are not made the same transaction or proof of a particular conversion event.
    Luke 10:35 On the next day he took out two denarii, gave them to the innkeeper, and said, ‘Care for him, and whatever more you spend I will repay you when I return.’

Philemon 1:20
Yes, brother, let me have this benefit from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in Christ.
  Philemon 1:7 — internal thematic parallel: The request to refresh my heart in Christ deliberately recalls the earlier report that the saints' hearts had been refreshed through the recipient. This is an internal refreshment callback, with the beneficiary now the writer himself. The past report and present request remain distinct.
    Philemon 1:7 For I had great joy and encouragement because of your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you, brother.

Philemon 1:21
Confident of your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say.
  2 Thessalonians 3:4 — thematic parallel: Both express confidence in the readers' obedience. Thessalonians says confidence in the Lord that they do and will do the commands; Philemon writes in confidence and expects even more than he says. The present/future forms and the unspecified more are preserved without assigning it a particular further act.
    2 Thessalonians 3:4 And we are confident in the Lord about you, that you both do and will do what we command.

Philemon 1:22
At the same time, prepare lodging for me also, for I hope that through your prayers I will be graciously given to you.
  Romans 15:30–15:32 — thematic parallel: Both link hoped-for presence with the recipients to their prayers and divine provision. Romans requests prayer for rescue and acceptable service toward coming through God's will and finding rest with them; Philemon requests lodging and hopes to be graciously given to them through prayer. No fulfilled release, shared itinerary or date is asserted.
    Romans 15:30 And I urge you, brothers, through our Lord Jesus Christ and through the love of the Spirit, to struggle together with me in prayers to God for me,
    Romans 15:31 so that I may be rescued from those who disobey in Judea, and my service for Jerusalem may become acceptable to the saints,
    Romans 15:32 so that, coming to you in joy through God’s will, I may find rest together with you.

Philemon 1:23
Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you,
  Colossians 4:12 — thematic parallel: Both name Epaphras greeting the readers in relation to Christ Jesus. Philemon calls him fellow prisoner; Colossians calls him a slave of Christ Jesus and describes his prayers for the recipients. The different roles are not swapped with Aristarchus or the differently named Epaphroditus.
    Colossians 4:12 Epaphras, who is one of you, a slave of Christ Jesus, greets you, always struggling for you in his prayers, so that you may stand mature and fully assured in all the will of God.

Philemon 1:24
as do Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, my fellow workers.
  Colossians 4:10–4:14 — thematic parallel: The closing lists share Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke. Philemon calls these fellow workers; Colossians gives particular descriptions, including Aristarchus as fellow prisoner, Mark as Barnabas's cousin and Luke as physician, with other people named between. Those titles and the circumcision-group statement are not indiscriminately applied to all four.
    Colossians 4:10 Aristarchus, my fellow prisoner, greets you, and Mark, the cousin of Barnabas, concerning whom you received instructions—if he comes to you, welcome him—
    Colossians 4:11 and Jesus who is called Justus. From among those of the circumcision, these alone are fellow workers for the kingdom of God; they have become a comfort to me.
    Colossians 4:12 Epaphras, who is one of you, a slave of Christ Jesus, greets you, always struggling for you in his prayers, so that you may stand mature and fully assured in all the will of God.
    Colossians 4:13 For I testify about him that he takes great pains for you and those in Laodicea and those in Hierapolis.
    Colossians 4:14 Luke, the beloved physician, greets you, and Demas.

Philemon 1:25
The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.
  Philippians 4:23 — thematic parallel: Both close with the same grace-of-the-Lord-Jesus-Christ blessing directed to the recipients' spirit. The repeated closing formula is preserved without adding Amen or changing it to a different printed grace formulation.
    Philippians 4:23 The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.
