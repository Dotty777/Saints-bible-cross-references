1 Peter 1:1
Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, to the chosen resident aliens of the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia,
  James 1:1 — thematic parallel: Both letters address people of the dispersion. Peter specifies chosen resident aliens and five regions; James addresses the twelve tribes. Their forms of address are comparable without equating the two recipient lists.
    James 1:1 James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, to the twelve tribes in the dispersion: greetings.

1 Peter 1:2
according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in sanctification by the Spirit, for obedience and sprinkling with the blood of Jesus Christ: May grace and peace be multiplied to you.
  Hebrews 12:24 — thematic parallel: Peter joins obedience with sprinkling by Jesus Christ’s blood; Hebrews names Jesus as mediator and a sprinkled blood that speaks. The passages connect Jesus and blood-sprinkling without supplying the same full description of its recipients.
    Hebrews 12:24 and Jesus the mediator of a new covenant, and sprinkled blood that speaks better than Abel.

1 Peter 1:3
Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who, according to His great mercy, has given us new birth into a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,
  Romans 6:4 — thematic parallel: Peter grounds new birth and living hope in Jesus Christ’s resurrection. Romans connects Christ’s being raised with believers walking in newness of life; its baptismal burial image remains distinct from Peter’s birth image.
    Romans 6:4 Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the Father’s glory, so we also might walk in newness of life.

1 Peter 1:4
into an inheritance imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you,
  Ephesians 1:13–1:14 — thematic parallel: Peter describes an imperishable inheritance kept in heaven. Ephesians speaks of the Spirit as the pledge of our inheritance, relating the promised inheritance to present assurance without using Peter’s three descriptive adjectives.
    Ephesians 1:13 In Him you also, having heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and having believed in Him, were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit,
    Ephesians 1:14 who is the pledge of our inheritance, toward the redemption of the possession, to the praise of His glory.

1 Peter 1:5
who are guarded by the power of God through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time.
  1 Peter 1:9 — thematic parallel: The opening assurance of God’s guarding through faith toward a salvation ready for revelation connects with the stated outcome of faith, salvation of souls. The later clause names the outcome without repeating every condition or timing expression.
    1 Peter 1:9 receiving the outcome of your faith, the salvation of your souls.

1 Peter 1:6
In this you rejoice, though now, for a little while, if necessary, you have been grieved by various trials,
  James 1:2–1:3 — thematic parallel: Both connect joy with various trials rather than treating trials as pleasant in themselves. Peter allows present grief for a little while; James directs joy in trials and explains faith’s testing and endurance.
    James 1:2 Count it all joy, my brothers, whenever you encounter trials of various kinds,
    James 1:3 knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance.

1 Peter 1:7
so that the tested genuineness of your faith—more precious than gold that perishes, though tested by fire—may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ.
  James 1:3 — thematic parallel: Both explicitly speak of the testing of faith. James identifies endurance as its result; Peter compares tested faith with perishable gold and looks toward praise, glory and honor at Jesus Christ’s revelation.
    James 1:3 knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance.

1 Peter 1:8
Though you have not seen Him, you love Him; though you do not see Him now, yet believing in Him, you rejoice with joy inexpressible and full of glory,
  John 20:29 — thematic parallel: Peter describes loving and believing in the presently unseen Christ. Jesus calls those who have not seen and yet believe blessed; the Gospel’s saying does not repeat Peter’s love and inexpressible joy clauses.
    John 20:29 Jesus says to him, “Because you have seen Me, have you believed? Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed.”

1 Peter 1:9
receiving the outcome of your faith, the salvation of your souls.
  Hebrews 10:39 — thematic parallel: Peter names salvation of souls as faith’s outcome. Hebrews contrasts shrinking back toward destruction with faith toward preservation of the soul, giving a closely related faith-and-soul outcome without identifying identical circumstances.
    Hebrews 10:39 But we are not people who draw back to destruction, but people of faith for the preservation of the soul.

1 Peter 1:10
Concerning this salvation, the prophets who prophesied about the grace for you searched and investigated,
  Luke 24:25–24:27 — thematic parallel: Peter describes prophets investigating the salvation and grace they announced. Jesus’ explanation from Moses and the prophets concerns Himself and rebukes slow belief; it illustrates prophetic testimony without narrating each prophet’s investigation.
    Luke 24:25 And He said to them, “O foolish ones, and slow in heart to believe all that the prophets spoke!
    Luke 24:26 Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and enter into His glory?”
    Luke 24:27 And beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, He interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning Himself.

1 Peter 1:11
investigating what time or what kind of time the Spirit of Christ within them was indicating, as He testified beforehand to the sufferings destined for Christ and the glories after them.
  Luke 24:26–24:27 — thematic parallel: Both join the Christ’s sufferings with subsequent glory and prophetic witness. Luke states the necessity of suffering and entering glory; Peter describes the Spirit of Christ indicating sufferings and glories beforehand.
    Luke 24:26 Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and enter into His glory?”
    Luke 24:27 And beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, He interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning Himself.

1 Peter 1:12
It was revealed to them that they were serving these things for you, and not for themselves—things now announced to you through those who proclaimed the good news to you by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven, things into which angels long to look.
  Ephesians 3:10 — thematic parallel: Peter says angels long to look into the announced good news. Ephesians describes God’s varied wisdom being made known through the church to heavenly rulers and authorities; their heavenly audiences and exact activities remain distinct.
    Ephesians 3:10 so that now, through the Church, the richly varied wisdom of God might be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places,

1 Peter 1:13
Therefore, gird up the loins of your mind; be sober, and set your hope fully on the grace being brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ.
  Luke 12:35–12:36 — thematic parallel: Both use girding as readiness language. Jesus’ servants have girded loins and lamps while waiting for their master; Peter expressly applies girding to the mind and directs sober hope toward Christ’s revelation.
    Luke 12:35 Let your loins be girded and your lamps burning,
    Luke 12:36 and be like people waiting for their own master to return from the wedding feast, so that when he comes and knocks, they may open to him at once.

1 Peter 1:14
As children of obedience, do not conform yourselves to the former desires of your ignorance,
  Romans 12:2 — thematic parallel: Both prohibit conformity in directing renewed conduct. Peter contrasts obedience with former ignorant desires; Romans contrasts this age’s pattern with transformation by renewal of the mind and discernment of God’s will.
    Romans 12:2 And do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of the mind, so that you may discern what God’s will is: the good and pleasing and perfect.

1 Peter 1:15
but, in accordance with the Holy One who called you, become holy yourselves in all your conduct,
  Leviticus 19:2 — thematic parallel: Leviticus commands Israel’s congregation to be holy because the Lord their God is holy. Peter similarly grounds holiness of conduct in the Holy One who called his readers, applying that holiness rationale to their calling.
    Leviticus 19:2 Speak unto the congregation of the sons of Israel, and you shall say unto them: You shall be holy, for I am holy, the Lord your God.

1 Peter 1:16
because it is written, “You shall be holy, because I am holy.”
  Leviticus 11:44–11:45 — scriptural quotation: Peter’s written command to be holy because God is holy corresponds to the repeated holiness command in Leviticus. The retained target includes its food-creature setting and exodus rationale, which Peter’s brief citation does not reproduce.
    Leviticus 11:44 for I am the Lord your God; and you shall be sanctified and you shall be holy, for I am holy, the Lord your God. And you shall not defile your souls through any creeping things that move upon the earth;
    Leviticus 11:45 for I am the Lord who brought you up out of the land of Egypt to be your God; and you shall be holy, for I am holy, the Lord.

1 Peter 1:17
And if you call upon as Father the One who judges impartially according to each person’s work, conduct yourselves in fear during the time of your sojourn,
  Romans 2:6–2:11 — thematic parallel: Both connect judgment according to work with God’s impartiality. Peter addresses those invoking the Judge as Father and urges fearful conduct during their sojourn; Romans describes differing outcomes without partiality.
    Romans 2:6 who will repay each according to his works:
    Romans 2:7 to those who, through perseverance in good work, seek glory and honor and incorruptibility, eternal life;
    Romans 2:8 but to those acting from selfish ambition, who disobey the truth but obey unrighteousness, wrath and fury,
    Romans 2:9 affliction and distress upon every human soul that does evil—the Jew first and also the Greek;
    Romans 2:10 but glory and honor and peace to everyone who does good, to the Jew first and also to the Greek.
    Romans 2:11 For there is no partiality with God.

1 Peter 1:18
knowing that you were ransomed from the futile conduct handed down from your ancestors, not with perishable things, silver or gold,
  Mark 10:45 — thematic parallel: Peter’s ransom language says release from inherited futile conduct was not purchased by silver or gold. Jesus speaks of giving His life as a ransom for many; the target supplies the life-giving action rather than Peter’s rejected payment materials.
    Mark 10:45 For even the Son of Man did not come to be served but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom in exchange for many.”

1 Peter 1:19
but with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot—the blood of Christ,
  Exodus 12:5 — thematic parallel: Peter compares Christ’s precious blood with a lamb without blemish and spot. Exodus requires an unblemished animal for the Passover preparation; the ritual requirement supports the comparison without itself naming Christ.
    Exodus 12:5 Your sheep shall be without blemish, a male a year old; you shall take it from the lambs and from the young goats.

1 Peter 1:20
who was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was manifested at the end of the times for your sake.
  Ephesians 1:4 — thematic parallel: Both locate a divine purpose before the world’s foundation. Peter says Christ was foreknown and later manifested for the readers; Ephesians says God chose us in Him. The subjects and verbs are preserved rather than made interchangeable.
    Ephesians 1:4 just as He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world, so that we should be holy and without blemish before Him in love,

1 Peter 1:21
Through Him you are believers in God, who raised Him from the dead and gave Him glory, so that your faith and hope are in God.
  Romans 4:24 — thematic parallel: Both describe believing in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. Peter also speaks of God giving Him glory and believers’ hope in God; Romans discusses the reckoning of righteousness to those who believe.
    Romans 4:24 but also for our sake, to whom it is to be reckoned, who believe in the One who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead,

1 Peter 1:22
Having purified your souls by obedience to the truth for sincere brotherly love, love one another earnestly from a pure heart,
  John 13:34 — thematic parallel: Peter urges earnest mutual love after purification through obedience to truth. Jesus commands His disciples to love one another as He loved them; the common mutual-love demand is specific, while Peter’s purification wording remains additional.
    John 13:34 I give you a new commandment: that you love one another. Just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another.

1 Peter 1:23
having been born anew, not from perishable seed but from imperishable, through the living and enduring word of God.
  James 1:18 — thematic parallel: Peter describes new birth through God’s living, enduring word. James describes God bringing us forth by the word of truth according to His will; both connect birth language with God’s word without equating all their seed and firstfruits images.
    James 1:18 By His will He gave us birth through the word of truth, so that we might be a kind of firstfruits of His creatures.

1 Peter 1:24
For “All flesh is like grass, and all its glory like a flower of grass. The grass withered, and the flower fell,
  Isaiah 40:6–40:8 — scriptural quotation: Peter’s comparison of flesh and its glory with grass and a flower recalls Isaiah’s grass-and-flower proclamation. Isaiah says all the glory of man is as the flower of grass, then describes grass withering and the flower falling; Peter preserves these images within his own wording.
    Isaiah 40:6 A voice says, “Cry out!” And I said, “What shall I cry?” All flesh is grass, and all the glory of man is as the flower of grass.
    Isaiah 40:7 The grass has withered, and the flower has fallen;
    Isaiah 40:8 but the word of our God remains for ever.

1 Peter 1:25
but the word of the Lord remains forever.” And this is the word that was proclaimed to you as good news.
  Isaiah 40:8 — scriptural quotation: Peter’s enduring word of the Lord corresponds to Isaiah’s enduring word of our God. Peter additionally identifies this word as the good news proclaimed to the readers; Isaiah’s unit does not make that added identification.
    Isaiah 40:8 but the word of our God remains for ever.

1 Peter 2:1
Therefore, putting away all malice and all deceit and hypocrisies and envies and all slanders,
  Ephesians 4:31 — thematic parallel: Both instruct believers to remove malice and harmful speech. Ephesians names bitterness, rage, anger, shouting and slander with all malice; Peter’s list additionally includes deceit, hypocrisies and envies, without merging the two lists.
    Ephesians 4:31 Let all bitterness and rage and anger and shouting and slander be taken away from you, with all malice.

1 Peter 2:2
like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk, so that by it you may grow into salvation,
  Hebrews 5:12–5:14 — thematic parallel: Both use milk to describe nourishment associated with spiritual development. Peter positively urges newborn-like longing for pure milk toward growth; Hebrews rebukes a continuing need for milk rather than solid food. The evaluations differ.
    Hebrews 5:12 For although by this time you ought to be teachers, you again need someone to teach you the first elements of God’s oracles; you have come to need milk and not solid food.
    Hebrews 5:13 For everyone who partakes of milk is inexperienced in the word of righteousness, for he is an infant.
    Hebrews 5:14 But solid food belongs to the mature, whose faculties have been trained by practice to distinguish both good and evil.

1 Peter 2:3
if you have tasted that the Lord is good.
  Psalms 33:9 — scriptural allusion: Peter’s tasting that the Lord is good recalls the Psalm’s invitation to taste and see that the Lord is kind. The Psalm adds blessedness for the person who hopes in Him; Peter uses the experience to motivate longing for nourishment.
    Psalms 33:9 Taste and see that the Lord is kind; blessed is the man who hopes in Him.

1 Peter 2:4
Coming to Him, a living stone rejected by human beings but chosen and precious before God,
  Psalms 117:22–117:23 — thematic parallel: Peter’s living stone is rejected by human beings but chosen and precious before God. The Psalm’s rejected builders’ stone becomes the head of the corner through the Lord; the target establishes the rejection-and-divine-exaltation pattern without using living-stone language.
    Psalms 117:22 The stone the builders rejected— this has become the head of the corner.
    Psalms 117:23 This has come from the Lord, and is marvelous in our eyes.

1 Peter 2:5
you yourselves, like living stones, are also being built into a spiritual house, for a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.
  Ephesians 2:21–2:22 — thematic parallel: Both portray believers being built together into God’s dwelling. Ephesians describes a holy sanctuary and dwelling in the Spirit; Peter describes a spiritual house and holy priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices through Jesus Christ.
    Ephesians 2:21 in whom the whole building, fitted together, grows into a holy sanctuary in the Lord,
    Ephesians 2:22 in whom you also are being built together into a dwelling place of God in the Spirit.

1 Peter 2:6
For it stands in Scripture: “Look, I am laying in Zion a stone, a chosen, precious cornerstone, and the one who believes in Him will certainly not be put to shame.”
  Isaiah 28:16 — scriptural quotation: Peter’s chosen, precious cornerstone laid in Zion corresponds to Isaiah’s costly, chosen, precious cornerstone in Zion’s foundations. Peter says the one believing in Him will not be put to shame; Isaiah says whoever trusts in it will not be ashamed.
    Isaiah 28:16 therefore thus says the Lord: “Behold, I will place in Zion’s foundations a costly, chosen, precious cornerstone, in her foundations, and whoever trusts in it will certainly not be ashamed.

1 Peter 2:7
Therefore, the honor is for you who believe; but for those who do not believe, “The stone that the builders rejected has become the head of the corner,”
  Psalms 117:22 — scriptural quotation: Peter directly cites the builders’ rejected stone becoming the head of the corner. The source places the citation within a contrast between believers and unbelievers; the Psalm unit supplies the stone saying itself.
    Psalms 117:22 The stone the builders rejected— this has become the head of the corner.

1 Peter 2:8
and “a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense.” They stumble, disobeying the word; to this they were also appointed.
  Isaiah 8:14 — scriptural quotation: Peter’s stone of stumbling and rock of offense draws on Isaiah’s stumbling-stone and falling-rock imagery. Isaiah conditionally promises sanctuary to the trusting person, then names Jacob’s house and Jerusalem’s inhabitants; Peter adds disobedience to the word in his application.
    Isaiah 8:14 If you trust in Him, He will become a sanctuary for you, and you will not encounter Him as the stumbling of a stone or as the falling of a rock. But Jacob’s house is in a trap, and those settled in Jerusalem in a pit.

1 Peter 2:9
But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God’s possession, so that you may proclaim the excellences of Him who called you out of darkness into His marvelous light.
  Exodus 19:5–19:6 — scriptural allusion: Peter’s royal priesthood and holy nation recall the covenant address in Exodus concerning a royal priesthood and holy nation. Peter also names a chosen race and proclamation after darkness-to-light calling, which are not all stated in the retained Exodus units.
    Exodus 19:5 Now therefore, if you diligently listen to My voice, and keep My covenant, you shall be to Me a treasured people from among all the nations; for all the earth is Mine.
    Exodus 19:6 And you shall be to Me a royal priesthood and a holy nation.’ These are the words which you shall speak to the sons of Israel.”

1 Peter 2:10
Once you were not a people, but now you are God’s people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy.
  Hosea 2:25 — scriptural allusion: Peter’s reversal from not-a-people and no-mercy to God’s people and received mercy corresponds to Hosea’s renamed Not My People and Not Shown Mercy. Hosea’s exact Greek-based location is 2:25, and its final response to God is additional.
    Hosea 2:25 I will sow her for Myself upon the land, show mercy to Not Shown Mercy and say to Not My People, ‘You are My people.’ He will say, ‘Thou art the Lord my God!’”

1 Peter 2:11
Beloved, I urge you, as aliens and sojourners, to abstain from fleshly desires that wage war against the soul,
  Galatians 5:17 — thematic parallel: Both portray fleshly desire as conflict rather than harmless appetite. Peter says such desires wage war against the soul; Galatians contrasts the flesh’s desire with the Spirit’s desire, preserving the different terms for the opposed party.
    Galatians 5:17 For the flesh desires what is against the Spirit, and the Spirit what is against the flesh; for these oppose one another, so that you do not do the things you want.

1 Peter 2:12
keeping your conduct good among the Gentiles, so that, in the very matter for which they slander you as evildoers, they may observe your good works and glorify God on the day of visitation.
  Matthew 5:16 — thematic parallel: Both connect visible good works with observers glorifying God. Peter addresses Gentile slander and the day of visitation; Jesus speaks of shining light before people and glory to the Father in heaven, without that stated slander setting.
    Matthew 5:16 So let your light shine before people, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father who is in the heavens.

1 Peter 2:13
Submit yourselves to every human institution for the Lord’s sake, whether to a king as supreme,
  Romans 13:1 — thematic parallel: Both instruct submission in relation to governing authority. Peter specifies every human institution for the Lord’s sake and a king as supreme; Romans speaks of governing authorities and their derivation from God.
    Romans 13:1 Let every soul submit to the higher authorities, for there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been appointed by God.

1 Peter 2:14
or to governors as sent by him for the punishment of evildoers and the praise of those who do good.
  Romans 13:3–13:4 — thematic parallel: Peter describes governors punishing evildoers and praising those doing good. Romans similarly contrasts a ruler’s relation to good and evil works, including praise and punitive service, without identifying Peter’s individual governors.
    Romans 13:3 For rulers are not a terror to good work, but to evil. Do you want to be free from fear of the authority? Do the good, and you will have praise from it;
    Romans 13:4 for it is God’s servant to you for good. But if you do evil, be afraid; for it does not bear the sword without purpose. For it is God’s servant, an avenger who brings wrath on the one who practices evil.

1 Peter 2:15
For this is the will of God: that by doing good you should silence the ignorance of foolish people.
  Titus 2:7–2:8 — thematic parallel: Both connect good conduct with depriving opponents of a valid accusation. Peter speaks of silencing foolish ignorance by doing good as God’s will; Titus directs exemplary works and sound speech so an opponent has nothing bad to say.
    Titus 2:7 in everything. Present yourself as a model of good works, showing integrity in teaching, dignity,
    Titus 2:8 and sound speech that cannot be condemned, so that the opponent may be put to shame, having nothing bad to say about us.

1 Peter 2:16
Live as free people, yet without using freedom as a covering for evil, but as slaves of God.
  Galatians 5:13 — thematic parallel: Both deny that freedom licenses wrongdoing. Peter forbids using freedom as a covering for evil and calls readers God’s slaves; Galatians forbids making freedom a base of operations for the flesh and directs loving service to one another as slaves.
    Galatians 5:13 For you were called to freedom, brothers. Only do not make freedom a base of operations for the flesh, but through love serve one another as slaves.

1 Peter 2:17
Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the king.
  Romans 13:7 — thematic parallel: Peter’s commands to honor everyone and the king connect with rendering honor and fear where due in Romans. Peter separately names love for the brotherhood and fear of God, preserving the distinct recipients of these obligations.
    Romans 13:7 Pay everyone what is owed: tax to whom tax is due, duty to whom duty is due, fear to whom fear is due, honor to whom honor is due.

1 Peter 2:18
Household slaves, submit yourselves to your masters with all fear, not only to the good and considerate but also to the crooked.
  Ephesians 6:5 — thematic parallel: Both address slaves’ submission to earthly masters with fear. Peter explicitly includes crooked as well as good masters; Ephesians directs obedience with sincerity as to Christ, without stating Peter’s contrast between types of masters.
    Ephesians 6:5 Slaves, obey your masters according to the flesh with fear and trembling, in the sincerity of your heart, as you would Christ,

1 Peter 2:19
For this is grace: if, out of awareness of God, someone bears sorrows while suffering unjustly.
  Matthew 5:10 — thematic parallel: Peter calls endurance of unjust suffering out of awareness of God grace. Jesus blesses those persecuted for righteousness. Both regard undeserved suffering in relation to God positively, while their explanatory terms and promised outcome differ.
    Matthew 5:10 Blessed are those who have been persecuted for righteousness’ sake, for theirs is the kingdom of the heavens.

1 Peter 2:20
For what credit is there if, when you sin and are beaten, you endure? But if, when you do good and suffer, you endure, this is grace before God.
  1 Peter 3:17 — thematic parallel: The later statement that suffering while doing good is better than suffering while doing evil restates the contrast here between endurance after sin and endurance after good conduct. It adds the condition concerning God’s will rather than supplying praise for wrongdoing.
    1 Peter 3:17 For it is better, if the will of God should will it, to suffer while doing good than while doing evil.

1 Peter 2:21
For to this you were called, because Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you might follow in His footsteps.
  Philippians 2:5–2:8 — thematic parallel: Peter presents Christ’s suffering as an example for following His footsteps. Philippians directs Christ’s mindset and describes His humility and obedience to death; the example is shared without equating Peter’s footsteps image with every detail of the hymn.
    Philippians 2:5 Have this mind among yourselves which was also in Christ Jesus,
    Philippians 2:6 who, existing in the form of God, did not regard being equal with God as something to exploit,
    Philippians 2:7 but emptied Himself, taking the form of a slave, coming to be in the likeness of human beings; and being found in appearance as a human being,
    Philippians 2:8 He humbled Himself, becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross.

1 Peter 2:22
He committed no sin, nor was deceit found in His mouth.
  Isaiah 53:9 — scriptural quotation: Peter’s declaration of Christ’s sinlessness and absence of deceit corresponds to Isaiah’s statement of no iniquity and no deceit in his mouth. The target’s burial clauses remain additional, and its iniquity wording is preserved.
    Isaiah 53:9 And I will give the wicked in exchange for his burial, and the rich in exchange for his death; for he committed no iniquity, nor was deceit found in his mouth.

1 Peter 2:23
When He was reviled, He did not revile in return; when He suffered, He did not threaten, but entrusted His cause to the One who judges justly.
  Isaiah 53:7 — thematic parallel: Peter describes nonretaliation under reviling and suffering. Isaiah depicts the afflicted one not opening His mouth, like a sheep led to slaughter; Peter additionally describes entrusting His cause to the just Judge.
    Isaiah 53:7 And because he has been afflicted, he does not open his mouth. Like a sheep he was led to slaughter, and as a lamb is silent before its shearer, so he does not open his mouth.

1 Peter 2:24
He Himself bore our sins in His body upon the tree, so that, having died to sins, we might live to righteousness. By His wound you were healed.
  Isaiah 53:4–53:5 — scriptural allusion: Peter’s bearing of sins and healing through His wound correspond to Isaiah’s bearing our sins, wounding for our iniquities and healing by his bruise. Peter adds the tree and the purpose of dying to sins and living to righteousness.
    Isaiah 53:4 He bears our sins, and suffers pain for us; yet we reckoned him to be in toil and affliction and distress.
    Isaiah 53:5 But he was wounded for our iniquities, and has been weakened for our sins. The chastisement of our peace was upon him; by his bruise we were healed.

1 Peter 2:25
For you were wandering like sheep, but now you have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls.
  Isaiah 53:6 — thematic parallel: Peter’s readers formerly wandering like sheep recall Isaiah’s description of all straying like sheep. Peter then describes return to the Shepherd and Overseer; the retained Isaiah unit instead continues with the Lord’s delivering him over to our sins.
    Isaiah 53:6 We all, like sheep, have gone astray; each person has strayed in his own way. And the Lord delivered him over to our sins.

1 Peter 3:1
Likewise, wives, submit yourselves to your own husbands, so that even if some disobey the word, they may be won without a word through the conduct of their wives,
  Ephesians 5:22–5:24 — thematic parallel: Both direct wives’ submission to their own husbands. Peter focuses on the possibility of winning husbands who disobey the word through conduct without a word; Ephesians develops a comparison with Christ and the Church instead.
    Ephesians 5:22 wives, to your own husbands as to the Lord,
    Ephesians 5:23 because a husband is head of the wife as Christ also is head of the Church, He Himself being Savior of the body.
    Ephesians 5:24 But as the Church submits to Christ, so also wives to their husbands in everything.

1 Peter 3:2
when they observe your pure conduct in fear.
  Titus 2:5 — thematic parallel: Peter says husbands may observe wives’ pure conduct in fear. Titus likewise directs purity and submission in household conduct, with the purpose that God’s word not be reviled; its stated purpose differs from Peter’s winning language.
    Titus 2:5 to be self-controlled, pure, working at home, good, subject to their own husbands, so that God’s word may not be reviled.

1 Peter 3:3
Let your adornment not be the outward kind—braiding hair, putting on gold ornaments, or dressing in garments—
  1 Timothy 2:9–2:10 — thematic parallel: Both reject outward adornment defined by braided hair, gold and clothing as the governing standard. Timothy contrasts it with good works suitable to professed reverence for God; Peter’s contrast continues with the hidden person of the heart.
    1 Timothy 2:9 Likewise also women are to adorn themselves in orderly dress, with modesty and self-restraint, not with braids and gold or pearls or costly clothing,
    1 Timothy 2:10 but through good works, as is fitting for women who profess reverence for God.

1 Peter 3:4
but the hidden person of the heart, in the imperishable beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit, which is very precious before God.
  1 Timothy 2:9–2:10 — thematic parallel: Peter values the hidden heart-person’s gentle, quiet spirit above outward adornment.
