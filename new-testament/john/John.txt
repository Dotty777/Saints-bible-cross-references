John 1:1
In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.
  Genesis 1:1–1:3 — allusion: John's beginning, divine Word, creation context and light recall Genesis' beginning with God making heaven and earth, speaking, and bringing light. This is a creation-pattern comparison, not a claim that Genesis explicitly states John's full account of the Word being with God and being God.
    Genesis 1:1 In the beginning God made heaven and earth.
    Genesis 1:2 Now the earth was unseen and unformed, and darkness was over the deep; and the Spirit of God was moving over the water.
    Genesis 1:3 And God said, “Let there be light.” And there was light.

John 1:2
He was in the beginning with God.
  1 John 1:1–1:2 — teaching parallel: The Word's being with God in the beginning is compared with the eternal life that was with the Father and was manifested to the witnesses. Both passages join prior divine fellowship with subsequent revelation, while Word, life, God and Father retain the actual expressions used in their respective contexts.
    1 John 1:1 What was from the beginning, what we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, what we looked upon and our hands touched, concerning the word of life—
    1 John 1:2 and the life was manifested, and we have seen and bear witness and announce to you the eternal life that was with the Father and was manifested to us—

John 1:3
All things came into being through Him, and without Him not one thing came into being. What has come into being
  Colossians 1:16–1:17 — teaching parallel: John says all things came into being through Him and excludes any exception; Colossians says all things, heavenly and earthly, were created through and for Him and that He is before all things. The comparison concerns Christ's comprehensive relation to creation, without changing John's sentence division where the final clause continues into 1:4.
    Colossians 1:16 because in Him all things were created, in the heavens and upon the earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities. All things have been created through Him and for Him.
    Colossians 1:17 And He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together.

John 1:4
in Him was life, and the life was the light of humankind.
  John 8:12 — teaching parallel: The prologue joins life in Him with humanity's light; Jesus later identifies Himself as the world's Light and promises the follower the light of life. This is an explicit recurring life-and-light connection, while the earlier syntax remains continuous with the end of 1:3.
    John 8:12 Then Jesus spoke to them again, saying, “I am the Light of the world. The one who follows Me will certainly not walk in the darkness, but will have the light of life.”

John 1:5
And the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not overcome it.
  John 12:35–12:36 — teaching parallel: The light shines in darkness that did not overcome it; the later saying urges people to walk and believe while the Light is present so darkness may not overtake them. The contrast preserves unconquered light versus a warning about people being overtaken, rather than transferring the first statement into an unconditional guarantee about every person.
    John 12:35 Then Jesus said to them, “For a little time longer the Light is among you. Walk while you have the Light, so that darkness may not overtake you. The one walking in the darkness does not know where he is going.
    John 12:36 While you have the Light, believe in the Light, so that you may become sons of light.” Jesus spoke these things, and went away and hid Himself from them.

John 1:6
There came a man sent from God; his name was John.
  Luke 3:2–3:3 — narrative parallel: John is introduced as a man sent from God; Luke names John son of Zechariah as the recipient of God's word in the wilderness and describes his ensuing baptismal proclamation. Both establish the Baptist's divine commission, with Luke supplying its particular setting and activity rather than a duplicate of John's prologue.
    Luke 3:2 during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas, God’s word came to John, Zechariah’s son, in the wilderness.
    Luke 3:3 He went into all the region around the Jordan, proclaiming a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins,

John 1:7
He came as a witness, to bear witness concerning the Light, so that all might believe through him.
  John 5:33–5:35 — teaching parallel: The prologue says John's witness serves others' belief; Jesus later recalls John's testimony to truth and explains that He mentions it so His hearers may be saved. Both passages describe John's testimony as directed toward others' response, and the later passage calls him a shining lamp rather than making him the ultimate Light.
    John 5:33 You have sent to John, and he has testified to the truth.
    John 5:34 But I do not receive testimony from a person; rather, I say these things so that you may be saved.
    John 5:35 He was the lamp that burned and shone, and you were willing to rejoice for an hour in his light.

John 1:8
He was not the Light, but came to bear witness concerning the Light.
  John 5:35–5:36 — teaching parallel: John is distinguished from the Light to whom he bears witness; Jesus later calls John a burning, shining lamp but describes a greater testimony in His own Father-given works. The distinction permits the Baptist's figurative light as a lamp without identifying him as the true Light of the prologue.
    John 5:35 He was the lamp that burned and shone, and you were willing to rejoice for an hour in his light.
    John 5:36 But I have testimony greater than John’s: for the works the Father has given Me to complete, the very works I do, bear witness concerning Me that the Father has sent Me.

John 1:9
The true Light, which gives light to every person, was coming into the world.
  John 8:12 — teaching parallel: The prologue announces the true Light coming into the world and illuminating people; Jesus later calls Himself the Light of the world and connects following Him with life rather than darkness. The shared worldwide light language does not silently equate being illuminated with every person's believing response.
    John 8:12 Then Jesus spoke to them again, saying, “I am the Light of the world. The one who follows Me will certainly not walk in the darkness, but will have the light of life.”

John 1:10
He was in the world, and the world came into being through Him, and the world did not know Him.
  John 3:19 — teaching parallel: The world fails to recognize the One present in it; the later discourse explains judgment in terms of the Light coming into the world while people prefer darkness because their deeds are evil. This compares failed recognition with the later account of adverse response to the same light motif; the creation clause remains additional in the prologue.
    John 3:19 And this is the judgment: the Light has come into the world, and people loved the darkness more than the Light, for their deeds were evil.

John 1:11
He came to what was His own, and His own did not receive Him.
  Luke 4:24–4:30 — narrative parallel: John speaks of failure to receive Him among His own; Luke narrates Jesus' saying about a prophet's unacceptability at home and the resulting hostile hometown response. Luke provides a concrete rejection scene, while John's broader expression His own is not reduced exclusively to Nazareth or made a claim that every member of a people rejects Him.
    Luke 4:24 He said, “Amen, I tell you, no prophet is acceptable in his homeland.
    Luke 4:25 But in truth I tell you, there were many widows in Israel in Elijah’s days, when heaven was shut for three years and six months and a great famine came over the whole land.
    Luke 4:26 Yet Elijah was sent to none of them except to a widow at Zarephath in Sidon’s territory.
    Luke 4:27 And there were many leprous people in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet, but none of them was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian.”
    Luke 4:28 Everyone in the synagogue was filled with anger when they heard these things.
    Luke 4:29 They rose, threw Him out of the city, and led Him to the brow of the hill on which their city was built, intending to hurl Him down.
    Luke 4:30 But He passed through their midst and went on His way.

John 1:12
But as many as received Him, He gave them authority to become children of God—to those who believe in His name,
  Galatians 3:26 — teaching parallel: John connects receiving Christ and believing in His name with becoming God's children; Galatians calls its hearers God's sons through faith in Christ Jesus. Both relate membership in God's family to faith in Christ, using authority to become children and being sons as their respective expressions.
    Galatians 3:26 For you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus.

John 1:13
who were born not from bloods, nor from the will of flesh, nor from the will of a man, but from God.
  1 Peter 1:23 — teaching parallel: John contrasts divine birth with bloods and human or fleshly will; Peter describes birth anew from imperishable rather than perishable seed through God's living, enduring word. Both distinguish a God-given birth from ordinary mortal derivation, without replacing John's stated negatives or turning the different birth images into identical wording.
    1 Peter 1:23 having been born anew, not from perishable seed but from imperishable, through the living and enduring word of God.

John 1:14
And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory, glory as of the Only-begotten from the Father, full of grace and truth.
  Philippians 2:6–2:8 — teaching parallel: John says the Word became flesh and dwelt among witnesses who saw His glory; Philippians describes Christ in God's form taking a slave's form and human likeness, then humbling Himself to death. The comparison concerns divine Christ entering human existence, with John's glory-and-revelation emphasis and Philippians' humility-and-obedience emphasis preserved.
    Philippians 2:6 who, existing in the form of God, did not regard being equal with God as something to exploit,
    Philippians 2:7 but emptied Himself, taking the form of a slave, coming to be in the likeness of human beings; and being found in appearance as a human being,
    Philippians 2:8 He humbled Himself, becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross.

John 1:15
John bears witness concerning Him and has cried out, saying, “This was He of whom I said, ‘The One coming after me has come before me, because He was before me.’”
  John 1:30 — narrative parallel: The Baptist's testimony about the One coming after him who has come before him because He was before him is explicitly repeated when he identifies Jesus. This is an internal recurrence of the priority saying, not another incident independently establishing a different birth order.
    John 1:30 This is He of whom I said, ‘After me comes a man who has come before me, because He was before me.’

John 1:16
For from His fullness we all received, and grace in place of grace.
  Ephesians 4:7 — teaching parallel: John's community receives from Christ's fullness and grace in place of grace; Ephesians says grace was given to each according to Christ's gift. Both describe received grace originating in Christ, while Ephesians' distribution language does not resolve or replace John's particular grace-in-place-of-grace expression.
    Ephesians 4:7 But grace was given to each one of us according to the measure of Christ’s gift.

John 1:17
For the Law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ.
  Exodus 24:12 — thematic parallel: John identifies Moses as the intermediary of the Law; Exodus records God's promise to give Moses the written stone tablets, law and commandments. This supplies a concrete scriptural basis for Moses' law-receiving role, while the grace-and-truth statement about Jesus is additional in John.
    Exodus 24:12 And the Lord said to Moses, “Come up to Me into the mountain, and remain there; and I will give you the tablets of stone, the law and the commandments which I have written to give them as law.”
  Romans 5:20–5:21 — teaching parallel: John places the Law given through Moses beside grace and truth coming through Jesus Christ; Romans places the law's arrival alongside abounding grace reigning toward eternal life through Jesus Christ. This is a specific law-and-grace comparison, without making the two authors' explanations or the phrase grace and truth identical.
    Romans 5:20 But law came in alongside, so that the trespass might increase; but where sin increased, grace abounded beyond measure,
    Romans 5:21 so that, just as sin reigned in death, so also grace might reign through righteousness for eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

John 1:18
No one has ever seen God; the only-begotten God, who is in the bosom of the Father, He has made Him known.
  Matthew 11:27 — teaching parallel: John presents the only-begotten God in the Father's bosom as the One who makes God known; Matthew presents the Son's unique knowledge of the Father and His ability to reveal Him. Both give the Son a distinctive revelatory relation to the Father. John's supplied wording only-begotten God is preserved rather than replaced with another reading.
    Matthew 11:27 All things have been handed over to Me by My Father. No one knows the Son except the Father, nor does anyone know the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son wishes to reveal Him.

John 1:19
And this is the testimony of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to him, to ask him, “Who are you?”
  John 5:33 — narrative parallel: The priests-and-Levites delegation asks John for his identity; Jesus later explicitly recalls people having sent to John and his testimony to truth. The later retrospective notice is compared with this narrated inquiry, without adding names of envoys or claiming that every detail of the delegation is repeated.
    John 5:33 You have sent to John, and he has testified to the truth.

John 1:20
And he confessed and did not deny, and confessed, “I am not the Christ.”
  John 3:28 — narrative parallel: John's denial I am not the Christ is explicitly recalled later when he asks his hearers to witness that he had said this and had been sent before Him. This is a deliberate internal repetition of the Baptist's stated role, rather than an independent competing claim to the title.
    John 3:28 You yourselves bear witness for me that I said, ‘I am not the Christ, but I have been sent before Him.’

John 1:21
They asked him, “What, then? Are you Elijah?” And he said, “I am not.” “Are you the Prophet?” And he answered, “No.”
  Luke 1:17 — thematic parallel: John denies being Elijah when questioned; Luke's angel describes his going before the Lord in Elijah's spirit and power. The comparison preserves both the denial and the stated likeness of mission, without rewriting either as a simple assertion that John is the same historical Elijah or denying John's prophetic activity.
    Luke 1:17 He himself will go before Him in Elijah’s spirit and power, to turn fathers’ hearts toward children and the disobedient to the understanding of the righteous, to prepare for the Lord a people made ready.”
  Deuteronomy 18:15–18:18 — allusion: The envoys' question about the Prophet is compared with Moses' promise that God will raise a prophet like him from the people and put His word in that prophet's mouth. This supplies scriptural background for the expected prophetic figure, not proof of every assumption behind the envoys' question or a denial that John functions as a prophet in other passages.
    Deuteronomy 18:15 The Lord your God shall raise up unto you a prophet from among your brethren, like unto me; you shall hearken unto him,
    Deuteronomy 18:16 according to all that you asked of the Lord your God in Horeb, in the day of the assembly, saying: We will no more hear the voice of the Lord our God, neither will we see this great fire any more, lest we die.
    Deuteronomy 18:17 And the Lord said unto me: All that they have spoken is right.
    Deuteronomy 18:18 I will raise up for them a prophet from among their brethren, like unto you, and will put My word in his mouth; and he shall speak unto them all that I command him.

John 1:23
He said, “I am a voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make straight the way of the Lord,’ just as Isaiah the prophet said.”
  Isaiah 40:3 — quotation: John explicitly identifies himself with Isaiah's wilderness voice and the command to straighten the Lord's way. The actual Isaiah unit speaks of preparing the Lord's way and making straight the paths of our God. The named citation is retained with the differing wording of the supplied passages, rather than silently making their formulations identical.
    Isaiah 40:3 A voice of one crying in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord; make straight the paths of our God.

John 1:25
And they questioned him and said to him, “Why, then, do you baptize if you are not the Christ, nor Elijah, nor the Prophet?”
  Mark 11:28–11:33 — narrative parallel: The envoys challenge John's authority to baptize in the absence of the titles they propose; Mark's authority dispute asks whether John's baptism came from heaven or humans. Both concern the authorization of John's baptism, while Mark's later question is posed by Jesus to temple authorities and is not the same interrogation of John.
    Mark 11:28 and said to Him, “By what authority art Thou doing these things? Or who gave Thee this authority to do them?”
    Mark 11:29 Jesus said to them, “I will ask you one question. Answer Me, and I will tell you by what authority I do these things.
    Mark 11:30 Was John’s baptism from heaven or from humans? Answer Me.”
    Mark 11:31 They reasoned with one another, saying, “If we say, ‘From heaven,’ He will say, ‘Why then did you not believe him?’
    Mark 11:32 But should we say, ‘From humans’?” They feared the crowd, for everyone held that John truly was a prophet.
    Mark 11:33 They answered Jesus, “We do not know.” Jesus said to them, “Neither do I tell you by what authority I do these things.”

John 1:26
John answered them, saying, “I baptize in water. Among you stands One whom you do not know,
  Mark 1:8 — narrative parallel: John states that he baptizes in water and distinguishes the One standing among his questioners; Mark similarly contrasts his water baptism with the coming One's baptism in the Holy Spirit. John's notice that the hearers do not know that One remains particular to this exchange, with the Spirit identification developed later in 1:33.
    Mark 1:8 I baptized you with water, but He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”

John 1:27
the One coming after me, whose sandal strap I am not worthy to untie.”
  Mark 1:7 — narrative parallel: Both accounts give the Baptist's coming-after-me saying and his unworthiness to untie the stronger One's sandal strap. Mark adds stooping and explicitly says stronger; John places the saying within the delegation's inquiry. The close saying parallel does not combine their narrative order.
    Mark 1:7 He proclaimed, saying, “The One stronger than I is coming after me. I am not worthy to stoop down and untie the strap of His sandals.

John 1:28
These things happened in Bethany beyond the Jordan, where John was baptizing.
  John 10:40 — narrative parallel: The earlier scene is located beyond the Jordan where John baptizes; the later narrative explicitly returns beyond the Jordan to the place where John was first baptizing. This is a retrospective location link within John, not a settled modern site identification or an equation with the Bethany of Lazarus's household.
    John 10:40 And He went away again beyond the Jordan, to the place where John was first baptizing, and remained there.

John 1:29
The next day he saw Jesus coming toward him and said, “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!
  1 Peter 1:18–1:19 — thematic parallel: John identifies Jesus as God's Lamb taking away the world's sin; Peter describes ransom through Christ's precious blood as that of an unblemished, spotless lamb. Both use lamb imagery for Christ's saving work. Peter specifies blood and ransom, while John's world's-sin wording is not narrowed or replaced by those expressions.
    1 Peter 1:18 knowing that you were ransomed from the futile conduct handed down from your ancestors, not with perishable things, silver or gold,
    1 Peter 1:19 but with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot—the blood of Christ,

John 1:30
This is He of whom I said, ‘After me comes a man who has come before me, because He was before me.’
  John 1:15 — narrative parallel: The Baptist's identification of Jesus repeats the earlier coming-after-yet-before-me testimony with the same because-He-was-before-me explanation. This is an explicit internal cross-reference to the witness already announced, not an independently dated second account of their births.
    John 1:15 John bears witness concerning Him and has cried out, saying, “This was He of whom I said, ‘The One coming after me has come before me, because He was before me.’”

John 1:31
And I did not know Him, but I came baptizing in water for this reason: so that He might be revealed to Israel.”
  Luke 1:76–1:77 — thematic parallel: John explains his water-baptizing mission as revealing Jesus to Israel; Zechariah's prophecy assigns his child the role of preceding the Lord and giving His people knowledge of salvation. Both give John's ministry a preparatory, revelatory purpose. Luke does not explain John's personal statement I did not know Him or settle the sequence of recognition in other accounts.
    Luke 1:76 And you also, child, will be called prophet of the Most High, for you will go before the Lord to prepare His ways,
    Luke 1:77 to give His people knowledge of salvation in the forgiveness of their sins,

John 1:32
And John testified, saying, “I have beheld the Spirit descending like a dove from heaven, and He remained upon Him.
  Mark 1:10 — narrative parallel: Both passages describe the Spirit's dove-like descent at Jesus: Mark narrates His seeing the heavens torn open and the Spirit descending into Him, while John reports the Baptist's testimony that the Spirit descended from heaven and remained upon Him. Observer, preposition and remaining-language retain their actual differences.
    Mark 1:10 Immediately as He came up out of the water, He saw the heavens being torn open and the Spirit descending like a dove into Him.

John 1:33
And I did not know Him, but the One who sent me to baptize in water said to me, ‘The One upon whom you see the Spirit descending and remaining—this is the One who baptizes in the Holy Spirit.’
  Matthew 3:11 — narrative parallel: John's commissioned sign identifies the One who baptizes in the Holy Spirit, distinct from his own water baptism; Matthew likewise contrasts John's water baptism with the stronger One's baptism in the Holy Spirit and fire. Matthew does not supply John's recognition-sign wording, and its fire clause is not inserted into John.
    Matthew 3:11 I baptize you in water for repentance, but the One coming after me is stronger than I, whose sandals I am not fit to carry. He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire.

John 1:34
And I have seen and have testified that this is the Son of God.”
  Matthew 3:17 — narrative parallel: The Baptist testifies that Jesus is God's Son; Matthew records the heavenly voice identifying Jesus as the beloved Son in whom God is pleased. Both concern Jesus' sonship at the baptismal setting, while a human witness and a voice from heaven remain distinct speakers and forms of testimony.
    Matthew 3:17 And behold, a voice from the heavens said, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”

John 1:36
and looking at Jesus as He walked, he said, “Behold, the Lamb of God!”
  John 1:29 — narrative parallel: The Baptist repeats the designation Lamb of God while looking at Jesus; the preceding day's fuller identification adds the taking away of the world's sin. This is a repeated identifying title within the narrative, with the extra explanatory words retained in 1:29 rather than added to the shorter saying.
    John 1:29 The next day he saw Jesus coming toward him and said, “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!

John 1:37
The two disciples heard him speaking and followed Jesus.
  John 1:40 — narrative parallel: The two hear the Baptist and follow Jesus; the later unit explicitly identifies Andrew as one of those two who heard from John and followed. This is an internal clarification of one participant in the narrated action. It does not name the second disciple or equate the pair with a differently named calling scene.
    John 1:40 Andrew, Simon Peter’s brother, was one of the two who heard from John and followed Him.

John 1:38
Jesus turned and, seeing them following, said to them, “What do you seek?” They said to Him, “Rabbi”—which, translated, means Teacher—“where art Thou staying?”
  John 20:16 — thematic parallel: The disciples address Jesus as Rabbi, which the narrator translates Teacher; Mary later addresses the risen Jesus as Rabbouni, likewise glossed Teacher. The link concerns the expressly translated forms of address. Mary's resurrection encounter does not reproduce the earlier disciples' question about where Jesus is staying.
    John 20:16 Jesus says to her, “Mary.” Turning, she says to Him in Hebrew, “Rabbouni!”—which means Teacher.

John 1:39
He said to them, “Come, and you will see.” So they came and saw where He was staying, and stayed with Him that day. It was about the tenth hour.
  John 1:46 — narrative parallel: Jesus' invitation Come, and you will see is echoed by Philip's invitation Come and see to Nathanael. Both move an inquirer toward personal encounter with Jesus, with Jesus and Philip as different inviters. Philip's later words do not establish the location or tenth-hour timing of the first disciples' stay.
    John 1:46 Nathanael said to him, “Can anything good come from Nazareth?” Philip said to him, “Come and see.”

John 1:40
Andrew, Simon Peter’s brother, was one of the two who heard from John and followed Him.
  Matthew 4:18–4:20 — narrative parallel: Both passages identify Andrew as Simon Peter's brother and describe his following Jesus. John places Andrew among the Baptist's two hearers; Matthew places the brothers at their fishing work when Jesus calls them. These are related discipleship accounts with different settings and participants, not a merged chronology or an identification of John's unnamed second disciple.
    Matthew 4:18 As He walked beside the Sea of Galilee, He saw two brothers, Simon called Peter and Andrew his brother, casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen.
    Matthew 4:19 He said to them, “Come after Me, and I will make you fishers of people.”
    Matthew 4:20 Immediately they left their nets and followed Him.

John 1:41
He first found his own brother Simon and said to him, “We have found the Messiah”—which, translated, is Christ.
  Matthew 16:16 — narrative parallel: Andrew announces to Simon that Jesus is the Messiah, explicitly translated Christ; Simon Peter later confesses Jesus as the Christ and Son of the living God. The matching Christ identification is compared across the brothers' distinct speeches, without adding Peter's fuller sonship language to Andrew's announcement.
    Matthew 16:16 Simon Peter answered, “Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God.”

John 1:42
He brought him to Jesus. Jesus looked at him and said, “You are Simon, the son of John; you shall be called Cephas”—which is translated Peter.
  Matthew 16:17–16:18 — narrative parallel: Both scenes present Jesus addressing Simon in connection with the name Peter. John gives Cephas translated Peter and calls him son of John; Matthew addresses Simon Bar-Jonah and expands the rock-and-Church saying. The patronymic forms, wording and occasions remain distinct rather than being silently harmonized.
    Matthew 16:17 Jesus answered him, “Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah, because flesh and blood did not reveal this to you, but My Father in the heavens.
    Matthew 16:18 And I tell you that you are Peter, and upon this rock I will build My Church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it.

John 1:43
The next day He wanted to go out into Galilee. And He found Philip, and Jesus said to him, “Follow Me.”
  Mark 2:14 — narrative parallel: Jesus finds Philip and commands Follow Me; in Mark He addresses Levi at the tax station with the same command, and Levi follows. The concrete link is Jesus' individual summons to discipleship. Philip and Levi, their locations and the narrated responses are different and are not treated as one calling.
    Mark 2:14 As He passed by, He saw Levi son of Alphaeus sitting at the tax station. He said to him, “Follow Me.” He arose and followed Him.

John 1:44
Now Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter.
  John 12:21 — narrative parallel: John later again identifies Philip as being from Bethsaida of Galilee when Greek visitors approach him about seeing Jesus. That repeats this unit's particular hometown identification for Philip. It does not separately confirm this unit's additional statement about Andrew and Peter or name a modern site.
    John 12:21 These approached Philip, who was from Bethsaida of Galilee, and asked him, saying, “Sir, we want to see Jesus.”

John 1:45
Philip found Nathanael and said to him, “We have found the One of whom Moses wrote in the Law, and the prophets also: Jesus, the son of Joseph, from Nazareth.”
  Luke 24:27 — teaching parallel: Philip says Moses and the prophets wrote of the One he identifies as Jesus; Luke describes the risen Jesus interpreting the things concerning Himself beginning from Moses and all the prophets. Both concern Jesus as the subject of Scripture. Philip's additional reported description son of Joseph from Nazareth remains his speech, not a new biological-paternity conclusion derived from Luke's passage.
    Luke 24:27 And beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, He interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning Himself.

John 1:46
Nathanael said to him, “Can anything good come from Nazareth?” Philip said to him, “Come and see.”
  John 7:41–7:42 — narrative parallel: Nathanael questions whether anything good can come from Nazareth; the later crowd questions whether the Christ can come from Galilee and invokes Davidic Bethlehem. Both scenes contain objections based on perceived geographic origin. The characters' assumptions are reported rather than endorsed, and Nathanael is not identified with the later crowd.
    John 7:41 Others said, “This is the Christ.” But some said, “Surely the Christ does not come from Galilee?
    John 7:42 Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the offspring of David and from Bethlehem, the village where David was?”

John 1:47
Jesus saw Nathanael coming toward Him and said concerning him, “Behold, truly an Israelite in whom there is no deceit!”
  Psalms 31:2 — thematic parallel: Jesus commends an Israelite in whom there is no deceit; the psalm blesses a man whose sin is not reckoned and in whose mouth there is no deceit. This compares the specific no-deceit commendation, keeping the psalm's mouth wording and forgiveness context. It does not make Nathanael's description a statement of absolute sinlessness.
    Psalms 31:2 Blessed is the man whose sin the Lord will certainly not reckon, and in whose mouth there is no deceit.

John 1:48
Nathanael said to Him, “How dost Thou know me?” Jesus answered him, “Before Philip called you, while you were under the fig tree, I saw you.”
  John 2:24–2:25 — narrative parallel: Jesus tells Nathanael He saw him under the fig tree before Philip called; the later narrator describes Jesus' knowledge of people without needing human testimony. This compares the reported prior recognition with the explicit knowledge theme, while the fig-tree location stays particular and no distance or mechanism of seeing is added.
    John 2:24 But Jesus Himself did not entrust Himself to them, because He knew everyone
    John 2:25 and because He had no need for anyone to testify concerning humanity, for He Himself knew what was within a person.

John 1:49
Nathanael answered Him, “Rabbi, Thou art the Son of God; Thou art the King of Israel.”
  Psalms 2:6–2:7 — thematic parallel: Nathanael joins the titles God's Son and Israel's King; the psalm joins the appointed king on Zion with the declaration You are My son. John 5:44
How can you believe, receiving glory from one another, and not seeking the glory that comes from the only God?
  John 12:42–12:43 — teaching or imagery comparison: Both connect preference for human glory over God's glory with impaired reception of Jesus. John5 asks how such hearers can believe; John12 explicitly says some rulers believed but withheld confession because of that preference and fear. The difference between the question and the narrated hidden belief is preserved.
    John 12:42 Nevertheless, many even from the rulers believed in Him, but because of the Pharisees they did not confess Him, so that they might not be put out of the synagogue,
    John 12:43 for they loved the glory of people more than the glory of God.

John 5:45
Do not think that I will accuse you before the Father. There is one who accuses you: Moses, in whom you have set your hope.
  Deuteronomy 31:26–31:27 — scriptural background comparison: Deuteronomy places Moses' law book as a witness against the people and recalls their rebellion. This is concrete Mosaic accusation background for John's naming Moses as accuser. It does not narrate Moses speaking before the Father or turn the two scenes into one event.
    Deuteronomy 31:26 Take this book of the law, and lay it beside the ark of the covenant of the Lord your God; and it shall be there for a witness against you.
    Deuteronomy 31:27 For I know your provocation and your stiff neck; for while I yet live with you this day, you have been provoking God: how much more after my death!

John 5:46
For if you believed Moses, you would believe Me, for he wrote concerning Me.
  Acts 3:20–3:23 — scriptural background comparison: Acts explicitly places Jesus the appointed Christ beside Moses' promised prophet and the command to hear him. It provides a specific Mosaic-prophecy comparison for Jesus' saying that Moses wrote concerning Him. John5 does not name this as the only Mosaic text intended.
    Acts 3:20 so that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord, and He may send the Christ appointed for you, Jesus,
    Acts 3:21 whom heaven must receive until the times of restoration of all things of which God spoke through the mouth of His holy prophets from long ago.
    Acts 3:22 Moses said, ‘The Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers. You shall listen to him in everything he says to you.
    Acts 3:23 Every person who does not listen to that prophet will be destroyed from among the people.’

John 5:47
But if you do not believe his writings, how will you believe My words?”
  Luke 16:29–16:31 — teaching or imagery comparison: Both sayings connect refusal of Moses' witness with failure to receive a further disclosure. John5 contrasts Moses' writings and Jesus' words; Luke's Abraham contrasts hearing Moses/Prophets with persuasion by someone risen. The parable's speaker and resurrection scenario remain distinct.
    Luke 16:29 But Abraham said, ‘They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them.’
    Luke 16:30 But he said, ‘No, father Abraham; but if someone goes to them from the dead, they will repent.’
    Luke 16:31 And he said to him, ‘If they do not hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they be persuaded if someone rises from the dead.’”
