Luke 1:1
Since many have undertaken to compile an account of the events fulfilled among us,
  Acts 1:1 — thematic parallel: Both opening notices refer to composing an account. Luke mentions multiple prior undertakings concerning fulfilled events; Acts addresses its own prior account of Jesus' deeds and teaching. This is a literary-preface comparison, not a claim that Acts identifies every account Luke mentions.
    Acts 1:1 In my first account, Theophilus, I wrote about all that Jesus began both to do and to teach

Luke 1:2
just as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and servants of the word handed them down to us,
  John 15:27 — thematic parallel: Both connect testimony with people present from the beginning. Luke describes transmitted eyewitness testimony and service of the word; John records Jesus telling the disciples that they testify because they have been with Him from the beginning.
    John 15:27 And you also bear witness, because you have been with Me from the beginning.

Luke 1:3
it seemed good to me also, having followed everything carefully from the beginning, to write an orderly account for you, most excellent Theophilus,
  Acts 1:1 — thematic parallel: Both address Theophilus in connection with a written account. Luke describes careful investigation and an orderly account; Acts refers to the first account about Jesus. The connection uses the linked addressee-and-account context rather than a loose match of a personal name alone.
    Acts 1:1 In my first account, Theophilus, I wrote about all that Jesus began both to do and to teach

Luke 1:4
so that you may know the certainty of the matters in which you have been instructed.
  1 John 5:13 — thematic parallel: Both writers explain their writing as enabling readers to know something with confidence. Luke concerns certainty about received instruction; John concerns believers knowing they have eternal life. The subject matter and intended assurance are not treated as identical.
    1 John 5:13 I have written these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, so that you may know that you have eternal life.

Luke 1:5
In the days of Herod, king of Judea, there was a priest named Zechariah, of the division of Abijah. He had a wife from the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth.
  1 Chronicles 24:10 — allusion: Luke identifies Zechariah with the priestly division of Abijah; Chronicles lists Abia as the eighth priestly allotment in its surrounding division list. The name forms remain as supplied, and this does not identify Zechariah with an unrelated prophet of the same name.
    1 Chronicles 24:10 the seventh for Kos, the eighth for Abia,

Luke 1:6
Both were righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the Lord’s commandments and ordinances.
  Philippians 3:6 — thematic parallel: Both describe blamelessness in relation to observance of the law. Luke praises the couple's conduct in the Lord's commandments and ordinances; Paul describes his own former standing concerning righteousness in law. The persons and the surrounding arguments differ.
    Philippians 3:6 with respect to zeal, persecuting the Church; with respect to righteousness in law, having become blameless.

Luke 1:7
They had no child, because Elizabeth was barren, and both were advanced in years.
  Genesis 11:30 — narrative parallel: Both describe a marriage without children because the wife is barren. Genesis names Sara; Luke names Elizabeth and additionally specifies the couple's advanced age. The two couples are not identified as one.
    Genesis 11:30 And Sara was barren, and bore no children.

Luke 1:8
While he was serving as priest before God in the order of his division,
  1 Chronicles 24:19 — allusion: Luke's priest serving in his division corresponds to the ordered priestly ministry entering the Lord's house described in Chronicles. Chronicles supplies institutional background, not a report of Zechariah's particular service.
    1 Chronicles 24:19 This was their inspection according to their ministry, to enter the Lord’s house according to their ordinance through Aaron their father, as the Lord, the God of Israel, had commanded.

Luke 1:9
according to the priesthood’s custom, he was chosen by lot to enter the Lord’s sanctuary and burn incense.
  Exodus 30:7–30:8 — allusion: Exodus prescribes the priestly burning of incense before the Lord morning and evening. Luke narrates Zechariah's incense duty in the sanctuary; the Exodus passage does not establish Luke's separate lot-selection detail.
    Exodus 30:7 Aaron shall burn finely ground compounded incense upon it morning by morning, when he tends the lamps.
    Exodus 30:8 When Aaron lights the lamps in the evening, he shall burn incense upon it, a continual incense before the Lord throughout their generations.

Luke 1:10
The whole multitude of the people was praying outside at the hour of incense.
  Psalms 140:2 — thematic parallel: Both associate prayer with incense before God. Luke describes people actually praying during the incense hour; the psalm likens prayer to incense and raised hands to evening sacrifice. It does not describe Luke's crowd or fix its hour.
    Psalms 140:2 Let my prayer be directed as incense before Thee, the lifting of my hands as an evening sacrifice.

Luke 1:11
An angel of the Lord appeared to him, standing at the right of the altar of incense.
  Judges 13:3 — narrative parallel: Both birth-announcement settings begin with an angel of the Lord appearing before the promise of a child. Judges addresses a barren woman; Luke addresses the priest Zechariah by the incense altar. Judges does not supply the altar or its right-hand position.
    Judges 13:3 And the messenger of the Lord appeared to the woman and said to her, “Behold, you are barren and have borne no child; but you shall conceive and bear a son.

Luke 1:12
Zechariah was troubled when he saw him, and fear fell upon him.
  Luke 2:9 — narrative parallel: This same-book comparison links fear at an angelic appearance in two infancy scenes. Zechariah fears when he sees the angel; the shepherds fear at the angel and surrounding glory. The people and scenes remain distinct.
    Luke 2:9 An angel of the Lord stood beside them, and the Lord’s glory shone around them; they feared with a great fear.

Luke 1:13
But the angel said to him, “Do not fear, Zechariah, for your petition has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall name him John.
  Genesis 17:19 — narrative parallel: Both announce that a named wife will bear a son and give the child's name in advance. God names Isaac for Abraham and Sarah; Luke's angel names John for Zechariah and Elizabeth. The promises and children are not conflated.
    Genesis 17:19 And God said to Abraham, “Yes; behold, Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac. And I will establish My covenant with him as an everlasting covenant, and with his seed after him.

Luke 1:14
You will have joy and gladness, and many will rejoice at his birth,
  Luke 1:58 — promise and fulfillment: This same-chapter promise of rejoicing at John's birth corresponds to the later account of Elizabeth's neighbors and relatives rejoicing with her. It is an internal promise-and-narrative link, not independent attestation of the birth.
    Luke 1:58 Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had magnified His mercy toward her, and they rejoiced with her.

Luke 1:15
for he will be great before the Lord. He will certainly drink neither wine nor strong drink, and he will be filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother’s womb.
  1 Samuel 1:11 — allusion: John's prohibition on wine and strong drink recalls Hannah's vow that her promised son will drink neither wine nor intoxicating drink in the supplied Saints Samuel text. Samuel also mentions no iron on the head; Luke does not add that restriction or explicitly call John a Nazirite. Luke's Holy-Spirit statement is additional.
    1 Samuel 1:11 She vowed a vow to the Lord, saying, “Adonai, Lord, Eloai Sabaoth, if Thou wilt indeed look upon Thy handmaid’s humiliation and remember me, and give Thy handmaid male offspring, then I will give him before Thee as a gift until the day of his death. He shall drink neither wine nor intoxicating drink, and no iron shall come upon his head.”

Luke 1:16
He will turn many of the sons of Israel to the Lord their God.
  Acts 13:24 — promise and fulfillment: Luke predicts John's turning many Israelites toward the Lord; Acts recalls John's proclamation of a baptism of repentance to all Israel before Jesus' arrival. The later ministry notice supplies a concrete counterpart to the preparatory mission, without quoting every word of the prediction.
    Acts 13:24 after John had proclaimed, before His coming, a baptism of repentance to all the people of Israel.

Luke 1:17
He himself will go before Him in Elijah’s spirit and power, to turn fathers’ hearts toward children and the disobedient to the understanding of the righteous, to prepare for the Lord a people made ready.”
  Malachi 3:22–3:23 — allusion: Luke describes John going in Elijah's spirit and power and turning fathers' hearts toward children. Saints Malachi announces Elijah and restoration of a father's heart toward a son and a person's heart toward a neighbor. The different second relationships are preserved, and John is not declared literally the historical Elijah.
    Malachi 3:22 “And look, I am sending you Elijah the Tishbite before the coming of the Lord’s great and manifest day,
    Malachi 3:23 who will restore a father’s heart toward a son and a person’s heart toward his neighbor, lest I come and strike the land utterly.”

Luke 1:18
Zechariah said to the angel, “How will I know this? For I am an old man, and my wife is advanced in years.”
  Genesis 17:17 — narrative parallel: Both men respond to a promised birth by raising the problem of advanced parental age. Abraham laughs and names his and Sarah's ages; Zechariah asks how he will know and describes himself and his wife as old. Their reactions and the later divine responses are not made identical.
    Genesis 17:17 And Abraham fell upon his face and laughed, and said in his mind, “Shall a child be born to a man a hundred years old? And shall Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?”

Luke 1:19
The angel answered him, “I am Gabriel, who stands before God, and I was sent to speak to you and announce this good news to you.
  Daniel 8:16 — allusion: Both name Gabriel in a divine communication. Daniel hears a voice commissioning Gabriel to explain a vision; Luke has Gabriel identify himself and his mission to announce good news. Daniel's verse does not state Luke's description of standing before God.
    Daniel 8:16 I heard a human voice in the midst of Ulai, calling, “Gabriel, give that one understanding of the vision.” The man cried out and said, “The vision concerns that command.”

Luke 1:20
And behold, you will be silent and unable to speak until the day these things happen, because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled at their appointed time.”
  Ezekiel 3:26–3:27 — thematic parallel: Both describe a divinely governed period of inability to speak followed by an opening of the mouth. Luke makes Zechariah's silence a response to unbelief until the announced events occur; Ezekiel's silence and later speech concern a different prophetic commission and an embittering house.
    Ezekiel 3:26 I will bind your tongue, and you will become mute; you will not be a man rebuking them, for it is an embittering house.
    Ezekiel 3:27 But when I speak to you, I will open your mouth, and you will say to them, ‘Thus says the Lord: Let the one who hears hear, and let the one who disobeys disobey,’ for it is an embittering house.”

Luke 1:22
When he came out, he could not speak to them, and they realized that he had seen a vision in the sanctuary. He kept making signs to them and remained mute.
  Daniel 10:15–10:16 — narrative parallel: Both vision settings involve silence and subsequent communication. Luke explicitly says Zechariah cannot speak and makes signs; Daniel falls silent and then opens his mouth after a touch to his lips. The comparison does not assign the same cause or duration to their silence.
    Daniel 10:15 As he spoke these commands with me, I set my face toward the earth and fell silent.
    Daniel 10:16 Something like a human hand touched my lips. I opened my mouth, spoke, and said to the one standing opposite me, “My lord, something like a vision turned upon my side, upon me, and there was no strength in me.

Luke 1:24
After those days his wife Elizabeth conceived, and she kept herself secluded for five months, saying,
  Genesis 21:1–21:2 — narrative parallel: Both narrate conception in fulfillment of a preceding promise concerning an elderly couple. Genesis describes the Lord's promised visitation to Sarah and her conception; Luke describes Elizabeth conceiving and adds five months of seclusion, which Genesis does not reproduce.
    Genesis 21:1 And the Lord visited Sarah as He had said, and the Lord did to Sarah as He had spoken.
    Genesis 21:2 And Sarah conceived, and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the season of which the Lord had spoken to him.

Luke 1:25
“This is what the Lord has done for me in these days, when He looked upon me to take away my disgrace among people.”
  Genesis 30:23 — allusion: Elizabeth's statement about God removing disgrace recalls Rachel's declaration that God has taken away her reproach after conceiving and bearing a son. The women and births are distinct, and the supplied disgrace/reproach wording is retained.
    Genesis 30:23 And she conceived and bore Jacob a son. And Rachel said, “God has taken away my reproach.”

Luke 1:26
In the sixth month, the angel Gabriel was sent from God to a city of Galilee named Nazareth,
  Luke 1:19 — narrative parallel: This same-chapter connection identifies Gabriel as the envoy previously introduced to Zechariah. The later sending to Nazareth begins a distinct announcement to Mary; the earlier self-introduction does not independently establish the sixth month or destination.
    Luke 1:19 The angel answered him, “I am Gabriel, who stands before God, and I was sent to speak to you and announce this good news to you.

Luke 1:27
to a virgin betrothed to a man named Joseph, of David’s house. The virgin’s name was Mary.
  Matthew 1:18–1:20 — narrative parallel: Both connect Mary with betrothal to Joseph, and Matthew's angel addresses Joseph as son of David alongside Luke's identification of his Davidic house. Matthew narrates an already discovered pregnancy; Luke introduces the virgin before the announcement. The different narrative stages remain distinct.
    Matthew 1:18 The origin of Jesus Christ was like this. When His mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph, before they came together she was found to be with child from the Holy Spirit.
    Matthew 1:19 Joseph her husband, being righteous and unwilling to expose her to public disgrace, decided to divorce her quietly.
    Matthew 1:20 As he considered these things, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream and said, “Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary your wife home, for what has been conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.

Luke 1:28
And he entered and said to her, “Rejoice, you who have been favoured! The Lord is with you.”
  Judges 6:12 — allusion: Both angelic greetings assure the person addressed that the Lord is with them. Luke greets Mary as favored; Judges addresses Gideon as a mighty man of strength. The recipients and commissions are different.
    Judges 6:12 The Lord’s messenger appeared to him and said, “The Lord is with you, mighty man of strength.”

Luke 1:29
She was greatly troubled at the saying and considered what sort of greeting this might be.
  Luke 1:12 — narrative parallel: This same-chapter comparison links the troubled responses to the two angelic appearances. Mary is troubled by the saying and considers the greeting; Zechariah is troubled on seeing the angel and fears. The distinct causes of the reactions are preserved.
    Luke 1:12 Zechariah was troubled when he saw him, and fear fell upon him.

Luke 1:30
The angel said to her, “Do not fear, Mary, for you have found grace with God.
  Luke 1:13 — narrative parallel: Both announcements begin with the angel telling the named recipient not to fear. Mary's assurance concerns grace found with God; Zechariah's concerns his heard petition and Elizabeth's promised son. This is a repeated same-chapter annunciation pattern, not one announcement.
    Luke 1:13 But the angel said to him, “Do not fear, Zechariah, for your petition has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall name him John.

Luke 1:31
And behold, you will conceive in your womb and bear a Son, and you shall name Him Jesus.
  Matthew 1:21 — narrative parallel: Both announce Mary's bearing a Son and direct that He be named Jesus. Luke addresses Mary before conception; Matthew's angel addresses Joseph and adds salvation from sins as the naming explanation.
    Matthew 1:21 She shall bear a Son, and you shall call His name Jesus, for He shall save His people from their sins.”

Luke 1:32
He will be great and will be called Son of the Most High. The Lord God will give Him the throne of His father David,
  2 Samuel 7:12–7:13 — promise and fulfillment: Luke's promise of David's throne recalls God's promise to David concerning his seed, the prepared kingdom and a throne established forever. The Samuel verses supply the royal promise, not Luke's exact title Son of the Most High.
    2 Samuel 7:12 When your days are fulfilled and you sleep with your fathers, I will raise up your seed after you, who will be from your own body, and prepare his kingdom.
    2 Samuel 7:13 He will build Me a house for My name, and I will set his throne upright for ever.

Luke 1:33
and He will reign over Jacob’s house forever, and His kingdom will have no end.”
  2 Samuel 7:16 — promise and fulfillment: Both promise an enduring royal house or kingdom and throne. Luke applies an unending reign to Mary's Son over Jacob's house; Samuel speaks of the royal house and kingdom in the Davidic promise. The named houses are not silently made the same expression.
    2 Samuel 7:16 His house and his kingdom will be made sure for ever before Me, and his throne will be set upright for ever.’”

Luke 1:34
Mary said to the angel, “How will this be, since I do not know a man?”
  Matthew 1:18 — narrative parallel: Both identify conception in the absence of the prior marital relations at issue. Mary asks prospectively because she does not know a man; Matthew narrates pregnancy before Mary and Joseph came together. The question and the narrated discovery occur at different stages.
    Matthew 1:18 The origin of Jesus Christ was like this. When His mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph, before they came together she was found to be with child from the Holy Spirit.

Luke 1:35
The angel answered her, “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; and so the holy One to be born will be called Son of God.
  Matthew 1:20 — narrative parallel: Both attribute Mary's conception to the Holy Spirit. Luke's angel explains it to Mary with overshadowing and Son-of-God language; Matthew's angel explains the already conceived child to Joseph without repeating those additional phrases.
    Matthew 1:20 As he considered these things, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream and said, “Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary your wife home, for what has been conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.

Luke 1:36
And behold, your relative Elizabeth has also conceived a son in her old age, and this is the sixth month for her who was called barren,
  Genesis 21:2 — narrative parallel: Both describe a son conceived or born in an elderly couple's fulfillment of promise. Luke names Elizabeth as Mary's relative and specifies the sixth month; Genesis names Sarah and Abraham and does not supply those relational or timing details.
    Genesis 21:2 And Sarah conceived, and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the season of which the Lord had spoken to him.

Luke 1:37
for no word from God will be powerless.”
  Genesis 18:14 — allusion: Both insist on the efficacy of God's word in the setting of a surprising promised birth. Genesis asks whether any word is impossible with God and names Sarah's future son; Luke says no word from God will be powerless. The births and question/assertion forms differ.
    Genesis 18:14 Is any word impossible with God? At this season I will return to you, when the time comes round; and Sarah shall have a son.”

Luke 1:38

  2 Samuel 7:25 — thematic parallel: Both speakers accept God's promise in the language of His servant and ask for the spoken word to be made effective. Mary consents to the announcement about her Son; David asks confirmation of the word concerning his servant and house. These are different responses to different promises.
    2 Samuel 7:25 Now, my Lord, Lord, confirm for ever the word Thou hast spoken concerning Thy servant and his house, Lord Almighty, God of Israel. And now, as Thou hast spoken,

Luke 1:41
When Elizabeth heard Mary’s greeting, the infant leaped in her womb, and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit.
  Genesis 25:22 — narrative parallel: Both describe movement of children in a mother's womb. Rebekah's children leap within her and prompt her troubled question; Elizabeth's infant leaps at Mary's greeting, with Elizabeth later interpreting the movement as joy. The pregnancies and meanings of the movements differ.
    Genesis 25:22 And the children leaped within her; and she said, “If it is to be this way with me, why is this happening to me?” And she went to inquire of the Lord.

Luke 1:42
She cried out with a loud shout and said, “Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!
  Judges 5:24 — allusion: Elizabeth's blessing of Mary among women recalls the blessing of Jael among women in Deborah's song. Luke additionally blesses the fruit of Mary's womb; Judges praises Jael in a different setting and does not assign her Mary's maternal role.
    Judges 5:24 Blessed among women be Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite; among women in a tent may she be blessed.

Luke 1:43
And how is it that the mother of my Lord should come to me?
  2 Samuel 6:9 — allusion: Elizabeth asks how the mother of her Lord should come to her, recalling David's question about how the Lord's ark will come to him. This is a possible verbal visitation allusion across distinct narratives; it does not explicitly identify Mary as the ark or equate the surrounding events.
    2 Samuel 6:9 David feared the Lord that day, saying, “How can the ark of the Lord come to me?”

Luke 1:44
For behold, when the sound of your greeting entered my ears, the infant in my womb leaped with gladness.
  Luke 1:41 — narrative parallel: This internal narrative-and-speech connection links the narrated leap at Mary's greeting with Elizabeth's explanation that the infant leaped with gladness when she heard it. The second account adds the expressed interpretation of joy; it is not an independent witness report.
    Luke 1:41 When Elizabeth heard Mary’s greeting, the infant leaped in her womb, and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit.

Luke 1:45
And blessed is she who believed that there would be a fulfillment of the things spoken to her from the Lord.”
  Hebrews 11:11 — thematic parallel: Both associate believing God's promise with a remarkable conception. Elizabeth blesses Mary's belief in fulfillment of the Lord's words; Hebrews describes Sarah's faith in the faithful One who promised. The women, promises and circumstances are distinct.
    Hebrews 11:11 By faith even Sarah herself, though barren, received power to conceive offspring even beyond the proper age, since she considered the One who promised faithful.

Luke 1:46
Mary said, “My soul magnifies the Lord,
  Psalms 33:4 — allusion: Mary's soul magnifying the Lord recalls the psalm's call to magnify the Lord and exalt His name together. The psalm invites shared praise; Mary begins her own song. This is shared worship language, not an explicit quotation attribution.
    Psalms 33:4 Magnify the Lord with me; let us exalt His name together.

Luke 1:47
and my spirit rejoiced in God my Savior,
  1 Samuel 2:1 — allusion: Mary's rejoicing in God her Savior recalls Hannah's rejoicing in the Lord's salvation. Both maternal songs praise God, while the speakers and the occasions of their songs differ.
    1 Samuel 2:1 She said, “My heart is established in the Lord; my horn is exalted in my God. My mouth is opened wide against enemies; I rejoice in Thy salvation.

Luke 1:48

  1 Samuel 1:11 — allusion: Mary's praise that God looked upon His servant's lowly condition recalls Hannah's request that the Lord look upon His servant's humiliation. Hannah petitions for a child; Mary praises grace already announced and adds future generations calling her blessed.
    1 Samuel 1:11 She vowed a vow to the Lord, saying, “Adonai, Lord, Eloai Sabaoth, if Thou wilt indeed look upon Thy handmaid’s humiliation and remember me, and give Thy handmaid male offspring, then I will give him before Thee as a gift until the day of his death. He shall drink neither wine nor intoxicating drink, and no iron shall come upon his head.”

Luke 1:49
because the Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is His name.
  Psalms 110:9 — allusion: Both praise God's holy name in connection with His mighty or saving action. Mary names the Mighty One's great deeds for her; the psalm names redemption for His people and His commanded covenant. It does not describe Mary's personal experience.
    Psalms 110:9 He sent redemption to His people; He commanded His covenant for ever; holy and fearsome is His name.

Luke 1:50
His mercy is for generations and generations upon those who fear Him.
  Psalms 102:17 — allusion: Both speak of the Lord's enduring mercy toward those who fear Him across generations. Luke says generations and generations; the psalm says from eternity to eternity and additionally speaks of righteousness for children's children.
    Psalms 102:17 But the Lord’s mercy is from eternity to eternity toward those who fear Him, and His righteousness toward children’s children,

Luke 1:51
He has performed a mighty deed with His arm; He scattered the proud in the thought of their heart.
  Psalms 88:11 — allusion: Both join the Lord's mighty arm with the defeat or scattering of opposing pride. Luke speaks of scattering the proud in their heart's thought; the psalm describes humbling a proud one and scattering enemies with the arm of power. The objects and imagery are not silently made identical.
    Psalms 88:11 Thou didst humble a proud one like a wounded man, and with the arm of Thy power Thou didst scatter Thine enemies.

Luke 1:52
He brought rulers down from thrones and raised up the lowly.
  1 Samuel 2:7–2:8 — allusion: Both songs describe the Lord lowering and raising people in a reversal of status. Luke mentions rulers brought from thrones and lowly people raised; Hannah's song mentions humbling, exalting and raising the poor to sit with rulers on a throne. The examples differ.
    1 Samuel 2:7 The Lord makes poor and makes rich; He humbles and raises high.
    1 Samuel 2:8 He raises the needy from the earth and lifts the poor from the dung heap, to seat them with rulers of peoples and grant them a throne of glory as their inheritance,

Luke 1:53
He filled the hungry with good things and sent the rich away empty.
  Psalms 106:9 — allusion: Both praise God for filling hungry people with good things. Luke pairs that action with the rich being sent away empty; the psalm's paired description concerns an empty soul and a hungry soul, not Luke's rich.
    Psalms 106:9 for He filled an empty soul and filled a hungry soul with good things.

Luke 1:54
He helped Israel His servant, remembering mercy,
  Psalms 97:3 — allusion: Both connect remembered divine mercy with Israel. Luke describes helping Israel His servant; the psalm describes the Lord remembering mercy to Jacob and truth to Israel's house and adds the earth seeing His salvation.
    Psalms 97:3 He remembered His mercy to Jacob and His truth to the house of Israel. All the ends of the earth saw the salvation of our God.

Luke 1:55
as He spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and his offspring forever.”
  Micah 7:20 — allusion: Both connect God's mercy or promise to Abraham with what was spoken or sworn to the fathers. Luke mentions Abraham and his offspring forever; Micah names mercy to Abraham, truth to Jacob and the ancient oath. The differing clauses remain distinct.
    Micah 7:20 Thou wilt grant truth to Jacob, mercy to Abraham, just as Thou didst swear to our fathers in the former days.

Luke 1:57

    Luke 1:13 But the angel said to him, “Do not fear, Zechariah, for your petition has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall name him John.

Luke 1:58

  Luke 1:14 — promise and fulfillment: The relatives' and neighbors' rejoicing with Elizabeth provides the same-chapter narrative counterpart to the promise that many would rejoice at John's birth. Luke also interprets the event as the Lord magnifying mercy toward Elizabeth.
    Luke 1:14 You will have joy and gladness, and many will rejoice at his birth,

Luke 1:59

  Leviticus 12:3 — allusion: Luke's eighth-day circumcision corresponds to the eighth-day circumcision prescribed in Leviticus. The law does not prescribe the proposed name Zechariah or narrate this particular naming gathering.
    Leviticus 12:3 And on the eighth day he shall circumcise the flesh of his foreskin.

Luke 1:60

  Luke 1:13 — promise and fulfillment: Elizabeth's insistence that the child be called John agrees with the angel's prior command to give that name. This internal link does not assert how Elizabeth learned the command, which the cited verses do not explain.
    Luke 1:13 But the angel said to him, “Do not fear, Zechariah, for your petition has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall name him John.

Luke 1:62

  Luke 1:22 — narrative parallel: Both parts of the same narrative use signs during Zechariah's inability to speak. Earlier Zechariah makes signs to the people; at the naming others make signs to him. The gestures alone are not treated as proof that he was deaf.
    Luke 1:22 When he came out, he could not speak to them, and they realized that he had seen a vision in the sanctuary. He kept making signs to them and remained mute.

Luke 1:63

  Luke 1:13 — promise and fulfillment: Zechariah's written declaration that the child's name is John follows the name prescribed by the angel earlier in the same chapter. It is an internal naming-fulfillment connection, with the later wonder of the observers additional.
    Luke 1:13 But the angel said to him, “Do not fear, Zechariah, for your petition has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall name him John.

Luke 1:64

  Luke 1:20 — promise and fulfillment: Zechariah's restored speech at the naming resolves the same-chapter announcement that he would be unable to speak until the promised events occurred. The later verse adds his blessing God rather than repeating the angel's rebuke.
    Luke 1:20 And behold, you will be silent and unable to speak until the day these things happen, because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled at their appointed time.”

Luke 1:65
Fear came upon all who lived around them, and all these matters were discussed throughout the whole hill country of Judea.

    Isaiah 58:7 Break your bread for the hungry and bring homeless poor people into your house. If you see someone naked, clothe him; do not overlook those belonging to your own family.

    Luke 7:29 When all the people and the tax collectors heard, they acknowledged God’s righteousness, having been baptized with John’s baptism.

    Luke 19:8 But Zacchaeus stood and said to the Lord, “Behold, Lord, I give half of my possessions to the poor, and if I have extorted anything from anyone, I repay it fourfold.”

Luke 3:14
