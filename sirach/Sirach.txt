Sirach 1:1
All wisdom is from the Lord and is with Him for ever.
  Proverbs 2:6 — Wisdom teaching parallel: Sirach locates all wisdom with the Lord; Proverbs likewise names the Lord as giver of wisdom and the source of knowledge and understanding.

Sirach 1:2
Who can count the sand of the seas, the drops of rain, and the days of eternity?
  Job 38:37 — Rhetorical image parallel: Sirach asks who can count rain and other quantities beyond human reckoning; God's question in Job asks who counts clouds with wisdom. Both challenge human mastery of the heavens' quantities.

Sirach 1:3
Who can trace the height of heaven, the breadth of the earth, the abyss, and wisdom?
  Job 11:7-9 — Rhetorical image parallel: Sirach links inaccessible cosmic dimensions with wisdom. The speech in Job likewise challenges human understanding through heaven's height, depths, and the earth and sea's extent; its accusation against Job is not endorsed here.

Sirach 1:4
Wisdom was created before all things, and discerning understanding from eternity.
  Proverbs 8:22-25 — Personified wisdom parallel: Wisdom's creation before all things corresponds to personified Wisdom's account of being created before earth, depths and mountains in Proverbs. The shared claim concerns wisdom's priority in creation.

Sirach 1:6
To whom was the root of wisdom revealed? Who knew her skillful works?
  Job 28:20-23 — Wisdom teaching parallel: Sirach asks to whom wisdom's root was revealed; Job asks where wisdom is found, says it escapes human sight, and contrasts this with God's knowledge of its place.

Sirach 1:8
There is One who is wise, greatly to be feared, seated upon His throne,
  Romans 16:27 — Divine attribute parallel: Sirach identifies One who is wise, subsequently named as the Lord; Romans' doxology praises the only wise God. The connection concerns divine wisdom, without adding throne imagery to Romans.

Sirach 1:9
the Lord Himself. He created her, saw her, counted her out, and poured her upon all His works,
  Job 28:27 — Personified wisdom parallel: Sirach describes God seeing and ordering wisdom before pouring her upon His works; Job likewise describes Him seeing, preparing and tracing out wisdom within His ordering of creation.

Sirach 1:10
with all flesh according to His giving; He supplied her to those who love Him.
  James 1:5 — Wisdom as divine gift: Wisdom is supplied according to God's giving in Sirach; James directs those lacking wisdom to the God who gives generously. Both locate the gift's availability in God.

Sirach 1:11
The fear of the Lord is glory and a boast, gladness and a crown of rejoicing.
  Psalms 127:1-2 — Reverence and gladness: Sirach associates fear of the Lord with rejoicing; the psalm calls those fearing the Lord happy and promises their well-being. Reverence and happiness are joined in both.

Sirach 1:12
The fear of the Lord will delight the heart and give gladness, joy, and length of days.
  Proverbs 10:27 — Reverence and longevity: Sirach promises length of days through fear of the Lord; Proverbs explicitly says that fear of the Lord adds days. The longevity promise closely corresponds.

Sirach 1:13
It will go well at the end with the one who fears the Lord, and on the day of his death he will be blessed.
  Ecclesiastes 8:12 — Reverence and well-being: Sirach says the Lord-fearing person's end will be good; Ecclesiastes affirms that it will be good for those fearing God despite the sinner's apparent prolongation.

Sirach 1:14
To fear the Lord is the beginning of wisdom; she was created together with the faithful in the womb.
  Proverbs 9:10 — Verbal wisdom parallel: Both verses explicitly identify fear of the Lord as the beginning of wisdom. Sirach's additional womb imagery is not attributed to Proverbs.

Sirach 1:15
Among human beings she nested on an eternal foundation, and among their offspring she will be trusted.
  Sirach 4:16 — Generational wisdom parallel: Wisdom is trusted among people's offspring in Sirach 1; chapter 4 explains her possession as an inheritance held by descendants. Both describe wisdom continuing across generations.

Sirach 1:16
To fear the Lord is the fullness of wisdom, and she intoxicates them with her fruits.
  Sirach 24:19-21 — Personified wisdom imagery: Wisdom intoxicates with her fruits in chapter 1; in chapter 24 she invites people to eat her produce and drink. Both portray participation in wisdom through consumption imagery.

Sirach 1:17
She will fill their whole house with desirable things and their storehouses with her produce.
  Proverbs 24:3-4 — Wisdom and household abundance: Sirach's wisdom fills a house and storehouses with desirable produce; Proverbs joins a house built through wisdom to store-rooms filled with precious wealth through discernment.

Sirach 1:18
The fear of the Lord is the crown of wisdom, making peace and the health of healing flourish.
  Proverbs 3:7-8 — Reverence and healing: Sirach connects fear of the Lord with healing health; Proverbs connects fearing God and avoiding evil with healing for body and bones. Both describe reverence through bodily well-being.

Sirach 1:19
He saw her and counted her out; He rained down skill and discerning knowledge and raised high the glory of those who hold her fast.
  Job 28:27 — Divine ordering of wisdom: Sirach's divine seeing and distribution of wisdom corresponds to Job's account of God seeing, preparing and expounding wisdom. Job's wording is not represented as an identical counting formula.

Sirach 1:20
To fear the Lord is the root of wisdom, and her branches are length of days.
  Proverbs 10:27 — Reverence and longevity: Sirach pictures long days as branches growing from fear of the Lord; Proverbs states directly that fear of the Lord adds days. The image develops the same longevity teaching.

Sirach 1:22
Unjust anger cannot be justified, for the weight of a man’s anger brings about his fall.
  James 1:19-20 — Anger and righteousness: Sirach denies justification to unjust anger; James says human anger does not accomplish God's righteousness. Both reject anger as a reliable means of right conduct.

Sirach 1:23
A patient person will hold out until the right time, and afterward gladness will spring up for him.
  James 5:11 — Endurance and its outcome: Sirach promises gladness after patient endurance; James calls those who endure blessed and invokes Job's outcome from the compassionate Lord. Both look beyond present hardship.

Sirach 1:24
Until the right time he will conceal his words, and the lips of many will recount his understanding.
  Proverbs 17:27-28 — Wise restraint of speech: Sirach's wise person conceals words until the right time; Proverbs commends withholding a harsh word and associates silence with intelligence. Speech restraint displays discernment in both.

Sirach 1:25
In wisdom’s treasuries are sayings of knowledge, but reverence for God is an abomination to a sinner.
  Proverbs 1:7 — Wisdom and rejected reverence: Sirach contrasts wisdom's knowledge with the sinner's aversion to reverence; Proverbs joins reverence with perception and says the ungodly despise wisdom and discipline.

Sirach 1:26
If you desire wisdom, keep the commandments, and the Lord will supply her to you.
  Psalms 110:10 — Practiced reverence and understanding: Sirach tells wisdom-seekers to keep commandments; the psalm joins fear of the Lord with wisdom and good understanding for those who practice it. Both connect understanding with obedient practice.

Sirach 1:27
For wisdom and instruction are the fear of the Lord, and faithfulness and gentleness are His good pleasure.
  James 3:17 — Wisdom's moral character: Sirach associates wisdom with faithfulness and gentleness pleasing to God; James describes wisdom from above as peaceable, considerate and merciful. Both describe wisdom through conduct toward others.

Sirach 1:28
Do not disobey the fear of the Lord, and do not approach Him with a divided heart.
  James 4:8 — Undivided approach to God: Sirach forbids approaching God with a divided heart; James commands those of divided mind to purify their hearts as they draw near to God.

Sirach 1:29
Do not play the hypocrite in people’s talk, and keep watch over your lips.
  Matthew 23:27-28 — Hypocrisy condemned: Sirach forbids hypocritical conduct in people's speech; Jesus condemns an outward appearance of righteousness concealing inward hypocrisy. Both reject a deceptive public presentation of one's character.

Sirach 1:30
Do not exalt yourself, lest you fall and bring dishonor upon your soul. The Lord will uncover your hidden things and cast you down in the midst of the assembly, because you did not approach the fear of the Lord and your heart is full of deceit.
  Luke 14:7-11 — Self-exaltation and public shame: Sirach warns that self-exaltation ends in humiliation before the assembly; Jesus' banquet parable depicts shame before fellow guests and concludes that self-exalters will be humbled.

Sirach 2:1
Child, if you come to serve the Lord, prepare your soul for testing.
  James 1:2-4 — Testing in faithful service: Sirach prepares a new servant of the Lord for testing; James tells believers to expect trials that test faith and produce endurance. Testing belongs within faithful life in both.

Sirach 2:2
Set your heart straight and endure; do not rush in a time of calamity.
  Psalms 26:14 — Steadfast heart in distress: Sirach commands a steady heart and endurance in calamity; the psalm tells the waiting worshipper to act courageously and let the heart be strong while waiting for the Lord.

Sirach 2:3
Cling to Him and do not withdraw, so that you may grow in your last days.
  Deuteronomy 10:20 — Clinging to the Lord: Sirach's instruction to cling to the Lord without withdrawing closely corresponds to Deuteronomy's combined commands to fear, serve and cleave to Him.

Sirach 2:4
Accept whatever is brought upon you, and be patient amid the changes of your humiliation.
  Hebrews 12:5-11 — Enduring humbling hardship: Sirach calls for patience in humiliation; Hebrews urges endurance of painful discipline for its later benefit. The comparison concerns patient reception of hardship, without identifying every adversity as the same event.

Sirach 2:5
For gold is tested in fire, and acceptable people in the furnace of humiliation.
  1 Peter 1:6-7 — Testing by fire imagery: Sirach compares acceptable people tested in humiliation with gold tested in fire; Peter likewise compares faith tested through trials with fire-tested gold. Both use refining to explain human testing.

Sirach 2:6
Trust Him, and He will support you. Set your ways straight and hope in Him.
  Proverbs 3:5-6 — Trust and straight paths: Sirach combines trust in God with setting one's ways straight; Proverbs commands wholehearted trust in God and attendance to wisdom for straight paths, rather than self-reliance.

Sirach 2:7
You who fear the Lord, wait for His mercy, and do not turn aside, lest you fall.
  Psalms 32:18-22 — Waiting for divine mercy: Sirach addresses those fearing the Lord and waiting for mercy; the psalm describes precisely those who fear Him, hope in His mercy and wait for His help.

Sirach 2:8
You who fear the Lord, trust Him, and your reward will certainly not fail.
  Psalms 24:2-3 — Trust that will not fail: Sirach assures the Lord-fearing that trust will not lose its reward; the psalm declares that those waiting for God will not be put to shame. Both oppose trust to final disappointment.

Sirach 2:9
You who fear the Lord, hope for good things, for eternal gladness and mercy.
  Romans 15:13 — Hope and believing joy: Sirach urges hope for gladness and mercy; Romans asks the God of hope to fill believers with joy and peace so that hope abounds. Both join reliance on God with joy.

Sirach 2:10
Look at the generations of old and see: Who trusted in the Lord and was put to shame? Who remained in His fear and was abandoned? Who called upon Him and was disregarded by Him?
  Psalms 21:5-6 — Ancestral testimony of trust: Sirach asks whether earlier generations trusted and were ashamed or called without response; the psalm says the fathers hoped, cried to God, were rescued and were not ashamed.

Sirach 2:11
For the Lord is compassionate and merciful; He forgives sins and saves in a time of affliction.
  Psalms 102:8-10 — Compassion and forgiveness: Sirach explains trust through the Lord's compassion and forgiveness; the psalm calls Him compassionate and merciful and says He has not repaid people according to their sins.

Sirach 2:12
Woe to cowardly hearts and slack hands, and to the sinner who walks on two paths!
  James 1:6-8 — Divided allegiance and instability: Sirach condemns a sinner walking two paths; James describes the person of divided mind as unstable in all his ways. Both use divided direction to depict failed trust.

Sirach 2:13
Woe to the slack heart, because it does not trust; therefore it will not be sheltered.
  Hebrews 10:38-39 — Faithlessness and lost protection: Sirach's slack heart lacks trust and shelter; Hebrews contrasts faithful preservation of the soul with drawing back to destruction. The warnings connect abandoned trust with peril.

Sirach 2:14
Woe to you who have lost endurance! What will you do when the Lord visits?
  Hebrews 10:35-39 — The need for endurance: Sirach warns those who lose endurance before the Lord's visitation; Hebrews urges endurance to receive the promise and warns against drawing back while awaiting the coming One.

Sirach 2:15
Those who fear the Lord will not disobey His words, and those who love Him will keep His ways.
  John 14:21 — Love expressed in obedience: Sirach identifies love for the Lord with keeping His ways; Jesus identifies the person keeping His commandments as the one who loves Him. Both make obedience the expression of love.

Sirach 2:16
Those who fear the Lord will seek His good pleasure, and those who love Him will be filled with the law.
  Psalms 118:47-48 — Love directed toward God's law: Sirach says those loving the Lord are filled with the law; the psalm's speaker loves, meditates on and raises his hands toward God's commandments. Both depict affectionate engagement with divine instruction.

Sirach 2:17
Those who fear the Lord will prepare their hearts and humble their souls before Him.
  James 4:8-10 — Preparing and humbling the heart: Sirach's preparation of hearts and humbling of souls corresponds to James' commands to purify hearts and humble oneself before the Lord.

Sirach 2:18
Let us fall into the hands of the Lord and not into human hands, for as His greatness is, so also is His mercy.
  2 Samuel 24:14 — Close verbal parallel: Sirach prefers falling into the Lord's hands because of His mercy; David likewise chooses the Lord's hand rather than human hands because His compassions are many.

Sirach 3:1
Listen to me, your father, children, and act accordingly, so that you may be saved.
  Proverbs 4:1-4 — Paternal instruction formula: Sirach addresses children as a father asking them to listen and act; Proverbs likewise calls children to hear a father's discipline and retain his teaching.

Sirach 3:2
For the Lord has honored a father over his children and confirmed a mother’s authority over her sons.
  Exodus 20:12 — Command to honor parents: Sirach grounds parental honor and authority in the Lord; Exodus commands honor for both father and mother. Both present parental honor as a divine obligation.

Sirach 3:3
The one who honors a father atones for sins,
  Sirach 3:14 — Same-chapter filial-mercy parallel: Honoring a father is connected with atonement here; verse 14 develops this through mercy toward a father being remembered in place of sins. The two statements frame filial care's moral significance.

Sirach 3:4
and the one who glorifies his mother is like someone storing up treasure.
  Sirach 7:27-28 — Filial honor parallel: The mother's honor is valued like stored treasure here; chapter 7 commands remembrance of her labor pains and asks what repayment could match the parents' gift. Both stress the weight of filial gratitude.

Sirach 3:6
The one who glorifies a father will have length of days, and the one who obeys the Lord will give his mother rest;
  Exodus 20:12 — Parental honor and long life: Sirach promises long days to one glorifying a father; Exodus attaches long days and well-being to honoring father and mother. The stated benefit closely corresponds.

Sirach 3:7
he will serve those who brought him into being as masters.
  Colossians 3:20 — Filial obedience: Sirach describes service to those who brought one into being; Colossians tells children to obey parents because this pleases the Lord. Both connect filial obedience with religious duty.

Sirach 3:8
Honor your father in deed and word, so that a blessing may come upon you from him.
  Exodus 20:12 — Parental honor and blessing: Sirach commands honor in deeds and words in hope of a father's blessing; Exodus likewise commands parental honor and attaches a promise of well-being. The promised benefits are expressed differently.

Sirach 3:9
For a father’s blessing establishes the houses of children, but a mother’s curse uproots foundations.
  Genesis 27:27-29 — Narrative illustration of paternal blessing: Sirach describes a father's blessing establishing his children's houses; Isaac's blessing grants abundance and familial authority to Jacob. This illustrates the blessing's substance without commending Jacob's deceptive means of receiving it.

Sirach 3:10
Do not glory in your father’s dishonor, for a father’s dishonor is no glory to you.
  Proverbs 19:26 — Filial dishonor brings shame: Sirach denies glory to one who glories in a father's dishonor; Proverbs says dishonoring a father and expelling a mother brings shame and reproach upon the child.

Sirach 3:11
For a person’s glory comes from his father’s honor, and a mother in disrepute is a reproach to her children.
  Proverbs 17:6 — Honor shared across generations: Sirach says a person's glory comes from a father's honor; Proverbs calls fathers the boast of their children. Both connect children's standing with parental honor.

Sirach 3:12
Child, support your father in old age, and do not grieve him during his life.
  Mark 7:10-13 — Material care for parents: Sirach commands support for an aging father; Jesus condemns a religious practice that prevents children providing benefits to father or mother. Both require parental honor to include practical help.

Sirach 3:13
Even if he loses understanding, bear with him, and do not dishonor him in all your strength.
  Proverbs 23:22 — Respect for aging parents: Sirach forbids dishonoring a father who loses understanding; Proverbs forbids despising a mother because she is old. Both reject age or loss of understanding as grounds for disrespect.

Sirach 3:14
For mercy toward a father will not be forgotten; in place of sins it will be built up for you.
  Sirach 3:3 — Same-chapter filial-mercy parallel: Mercy toward a father being remembered in place of sins develops verse 3's claim that honoring a father atones for sins. The repeated theme connects honor with concrete care.

Sirach 3:15
In the day of your affliction you will be remembered; as fair weather upon frost, so your sins will dissolve.
  Sirach 29:12-13 — Mercy remembered in affliction: Sirach promises remembrance in affliction after filial mercy; chapter 29 pictures stored almsgiving rescuing its giver from affliction. Both connect prior mercy with help in later distress.

Sirach 3:16
The one who abandons a father is like a blasphemer, and the one who provokes his mother is cursed by the Lord.
  Exodus 21:16 — Violation of parental honor: Sirach condemns abandonment of a father and provocation of a mother; Exodus imposes death for reviling either parent. Both treat assault upon parental honor as a grave offense, while naming different actions.

Sirach 3:18
The greater you are, the more you must humble yourself, and you will find favor before the Lord,
  James 4:6 — Humility and divine favor: Sirach commands greater humility as one's greatness increases and promises divine favor; James contrasts God's opposition to the proud with grace given to the humble.

Sirach 3:20
because the Lord’s dominion is great, and He is glorified by the humble.
  Psalms 137:6 — The high Lord and the humble: Sirach joins the Lord's great dominion with His glorification by the humble; the psalm says the high Lord watches the lowly and knows the lofty from afar.

Sirach 3:21
Do not seek things too difficult for you, and do not investigate things beyond your strength.
  Psalms 130:1 — Limits of human inquiry: Sirach warns against investigating what exceeds one's strength; the psalm's humble speaker refuses to walk among great matters or wonders beyond him. Both express humility through restraint of ambition.

Sirach 3:22
Consider the things commanded you, for you have no need of hidden things.
  Deuteronomy 29:28 — Revealed duty and hidden things: Sirach directs attention to commanded duties instead of hidden matters; Deuteronomy reserves hidden things to God and gives revealed things to the people for doing the law.

Sirach 3:23
Do not busy yourself with matters beyond your tasks, for more than human understanding has been shown to you.
  Sirach 3:21-22 — Same-chapter limits of inquiry: This warning against matters beyond one's tasks continues verses 21–22's distinction between excessive inquiry and commanded duties. The sequence clarifies the practical boundary being urged.

Sirach 3:24
For their assumption has misled many, and an evil suspicion has caused their thoughts to slip.
  Proverbs 14:12 — Misleading self-assurance: Sirach warns that assumptions and bad suspicion mislead thought; Proverbs describes a seemingly upright way whose outcome is Hades. Both warn that apparent rightness can conceal dangerous error.

Sirach 3:26
A hard heart will suffer harm at the end, and the one who loves danger will perish in it.
  Proverbs 28:14 — Hardness of heart and harm: Sirach predicts harm for a hard heart; Proverbs likewise says the hard-hearted person will fall into evils. The shared warning opposes unyielding confidence.

Sirach 3:27
A hard heart will be weighed down with troubles, and the sinner will add sin upon sins.
  Romans 2:5 — Accumulated guilt: Sirach connects a hard heart with added sins and troubles; Romans says hardness and an unrepentant heart store up wrath for judgment. Both portray persistence in sin as accumulating consequences.

Sirach 3:28
There is no healing for a proud person’s calamity, for a plant of wickedness has taken root in him.
  Proverbs 29:1 — Incurable pride: Sirach says the proud person's calamity has no healing; Proverbs describes a stiff-necked person's sudden burning with no healing. Both associate obstinate pride with irreversible ruin.

Sirach 3:29
An understanding heart will consider a proverb, and a listening ear is the desire of a wise person.
  Proverbs 1:5-6 — Listening and interpreting wisdom: Sirach commends an understanding heart considering a proverb and a listening ear; Proverbs describes a wise listener becoming wiser and understanding comparisons, obscure sayings and riddles.

Sirach 3:30
Water will extinguish a blazing fire, and almsgiving will atone for sins.
  Daniel 4:27 — Almsgiving and sins: Sirach says almsgiving atones for sins; Daniel urges the king to redeem injustices by almsgiving. Both explicitly connect merciful giving with addressing wrongdoing.

Sirach 3:31
The one who repays favors takes thought for what comes afterward, and at the time of his fall he will find support.
  Luke 6:38 — Giving and later return: Sirach links repaying favors with future support; Jesus' exhortation to give promises a measure given back. Both connect generous action with subsequent benefit, without requiring identical circumstances of repayment.

Sirach 4:1
Child, do not deprive the poor person of his livelihood, and do not keep needy eyes waiting.
  Deuteronomy 15:7-8 — Aid to the needy: Sirach forbids depriving poor people of livelihood or delaying them; Deuteronomy forbids closing the hand to a needy brother and commands assistance according to his need.

Sirach 4:2
Do not grieve a hungry soul, and do not provoke a man in his helplessness.
  Proverbs 17:5 — Do not aggravate poverty: Sirach forbids grieving a hungry, helpless person; Proverbs condemns mocking the poor as provocation of their Maker and commends compassion instead.

Sirach 4:3
Do not add trouble to an embittered heart, and do not delay a gift to someone in need.
  Proverbs 3:27-28 — Timely generosity: Sirach forbids delaying a gift to someone in need; Proverbs forbids telling a needy person to return tomorrow when help can be given now.

Sirach 4:4
Do not reject an afflicted supplicant, and do not turn your face away from a poor person.
  Deuteronomy 15:7-8 — Refusing the poor condemned: Sirach prohibits rejecting the afflicted supplicant; Deuteronomy prohibits withholding affection and closing the hand against a needy brother. Both command a receptive response to need.

Sirach 4:5
Do not turn your eye away from someone in need, and do not give anyone occasion to curse you.
  Proverbs 28:27 — Turning away from need: Sirach forbids turning one's eye from the needy; Proverbs contrasts giving to the poor with turning away one's eye and encountering great difficulty. Both warn against deliberate refusal to see need.

Sirach 4:6
For if he curses you in the bitterness of his soul, his Maker will listen to his petition.
  Exodus 22:22-23 — God hears the afflicted: Sirach warns that the poor person's Maker hears his bitter petition; Exodus says God hears the cry of afflicted widows and orphans and responds against their oppressors.

Sirach 4:7
Make yourself welcome to the assembly, and bow your head to a person of high rank.
  Romans 13:7 — Honor toward authorities: Sirach counsels respectful bearing before a person of high rank; Romans commands honor where honor is due within its discussion of governing authorities. Both address conduct toward recognized authority.

Sirach 4:8
Incline your ear to the poor person, and answer him peaceably with gentleness.
  Proverbs 15:1 — Gentle response: Sirach requires a peaceable, gentle answer to the poor; Proverbs says a yielding answer turns away wrath whereas a painful word arouses it. The poor person's dignity is Sirach's particular application.

Sirach 4:9
Rescue the one being wronged from the hand of the wrongdoer, and do not lose courage when you judge.
  Psalms 81:3-4 — Rescue and just judgment: Sirach commands rescue from a wrongdoer's hand and courage in judgment; the psalm commands judgment for the vulnerable and rescue from a sinner's hand.

Sirach 4:10
Be like a father to orphans and in place of a husband to their mother. Then you will be like a son of the Most High, and He will love you more than your mother does.
  Job 29:12-16 — Narrative illustration of protective care: Sirach calls for fatherly care of orphans and support for their mother; Job recalls helping an orphan, receiving a widow's blessing, and acting as father to the powerless.

Sirach 4:11
Wisdom has raised her sons high and takes hold of those who seek her.
  Proverbs 8:17 — Wisdom responds to seekers: Sirach's wisdom takes hold of those seeking her; personified Wisdom in Proverbs promises that those seeking her will find her. Both portray a responsive encounter with wisdom.

Sirach 4:12
The one who loves her loves life, and those who rise early to seek her will be filled with gladness.
  Proverbs 8:35 — Wisdom and life: Sirach equates love of wisdom with love of life; Proverbs' Wisdom describes her ways as ways to life and divine favor. Both present life as wisdom's benefit.

Sirach 4:13
The one who holds her fast will inherit glory; wherever he enters, the Lord blesses.
  Proverbs 4:8-9 — Wisdom confers glory: Sirach promises glory to the one holding wisdom fast; Proverbs urges honoring her and says she will exalt, embrace and crown the learner. Both describe wisdom rewarding attachment with honor.

Sirach 4:14
Those who serve her will minister to the Holy One, and the Lord loves those who love her.
  Wisdom of Solomon 7:28 — God loves wisdom's adherents: Sirach says the Lord loves those loving wisdom; Wisdom of Solomon restricts God's love to those dwelling with wisdom. Both connect divine love with a person's relationship to wisdom, using different logical formulations.

Sirach 4:15
The one who obeys her will judge nations, and the one who attends to her will dwell confidently.
  Proverbs 8:15-16 — Wisdom equips rulers: Sirach says one obeying wisdom will judge nations; Proverbs' Wisdom claims that kings and rulers govern through her. Both locate sound public authority in wisdom.

Sirach 4:16
If he trusts, he will possess her as an inheritance, and his descendants will hold her in possession.
  Sirach 1:15 — Generational possession of wisdom: Wisdom becomes an inheritance held by descendants here; chapter 1 describes her as trusted among offspring. Both picture wisdom enduring within successive generations.

Sirach 4:17
For at first she will walk with him on winding paths; she will bring fear and dread upon him and torment him with her discipline until she trusts his soul, and she will test him by her ordinances.
  Hebrews 12:11 — Discipline before benefit: Wisdom initially frightens and disciplines her pupil in Sirach; Hebrews describes discipline as presently sorrowful but later fruitful. Both distinguish training's initial pain from its later benefit.

Sirach 4:18
Then she will return straight to him, gladden him, and reveal her hidden things to him.
  Sirach 6:27-28 — Wisdom's eventual gladness: After testing, wisdom gladdens and reveals hidden things here; chapter 6 promises that seeking her leads to finding her rest and gladness. Both describe her reward after persevering pursuit.

Sirach 4:19
If he goes astray, she will abandon him and hand him over into the grip of his downfall.
  Proverbs 1:24-31 — Rejected wisdom and ruin: Sirach warns that straying causes wisdom to abandon a person to downfall; Proverbs' Wisdom refuses those who rejected her counsel when their destruction arrives.

Sirach 4:20
Watch for the right time and guard against evil, and do not be ashamed concerning your soul.
  Ephesians 5:15-16 — Wise attention to time and evil: Sirach commands watchfulness for the right time and protection against evil; Ephesians joins careful wise conduct with taking the opportunity because the days are evil.

Sirach 4:21
For there is shame that brings sin, and there is shame that is glory and favor.
  Sirach 41:14-16 — Discerning kinds of shame: Sirach distinguishes shame leading to sin from shame bringing honor; chapter 41 explicitly warns that not every kind of shame should be preserved. The later instruction develops that distinction.

Sirach 4:22
Do not show partiality against your own soul, and do not defer to someone to your own downfall.
  Proverbs 24:23-25 — Partiality rejected: Sirach forbids harmful deference to persons; Proverbs declares showing deference to a face in judgment wrong and condemns acquitting the impious. Both subordinate personal regard to right judgment.

Sirach 4:23
Do not withhold a word at a time of need,
  Proverbs 15:23 — Ethical contrast in timely speech: Sirach commands speaking when needed; Proverbs describes the evil person's failure to say something timely and good for the community. The negative portrait contrasts with Sirach's positive duty.

Sirach 4:24
for wisdom will be known through speech, and instruction through the tongue’s utterance.
  Proverbs 16:23 — Understanding expressed through speech: Sirach says wisdom becomes known through speech; Proverbs describes a wise heart understanding its mouth's utterance and carrying discernment upon the lips.

Sirach 4:25
Do not speak against the truth, and be ashamed of your lack of instruction.
  Ephesians 4:25 — Truthful speech: Sirach forbids speaking against truth; Ephesians commands putting away falsehood and speaking truth with one's neighbor. Both make truth a governing rule for speech.

Sirach 4:26
Do not be ashamed to confess your sins, and do not force your way against a river’s current.
  1 John 1:9 — Confession of sins: Sirach forbids shame from preventing confession; First John promises forgiveness and cleansing when sins are confessed. Both commend acknowledged wrongdoing instead of its concealment.

Sirach 4:27
Do not lay yourself down beneath a foolish person, and do not show partiality to someone powerful.
  James 2:1-4 — Favoritism toward power: Sirach forbids deference to someone powerful; James condemns giving preferential treatment to a richly dressed person over a poor visitor. Both reject rank-based partiality.

Sirach 4:28
Struggle for the truth until death, and the Lord God will fight for you.
  2 Maccabees 7:37-38 — Fidelity at the cost of death: Sirach urges struggle for truth until death with God's help; the Maccabean speaker gives body and soul for ancestral laws while appealing for God's mercy. This is an ethical comparison, not an asserted quotation.

Sirach 4:29
Do not be rash with your tongue and sluggish and slack in your deeds.
  James 1:19-22 — Restrained speech and active obedience: Sirach contrasts rash speech with slack deeds; James commands slowness to speak and then becoming doers rather than merely hearers of the word.

Sirach 4:30
Do not be like a lion in your house, indulging vain fancies among your household slaves.
  Ephesians 6:9 — Restraint toward household servants: Sirach forbids lion-like conduct in one's household toward slaves; Ephesians instructs masters to give up threats because they themselves have a heavenly Master.

Sirach 4:31
Do not let your hand be stretched out to receive, but drawn back when repaying.
  Sirach 29:2-3 — Repaying what one receives: Sirach condemns a hand open to receive but closed when repaying; chapter 29 expressly commands repayment at the appointed time and faithfulness to one's word.

Sirach 5:1
Do not rely on your possessions, and do not say, “They are sufficient for me.”
  Luke 12:19-21 — Narrative illustration of false security: Sirach forbids declaring possessions sufficient; Jesus' rich man tells his soul its stored goods ensure ease, only to have his life demanded that night.

Sirach 5:2
Do not follow your soul and your strength in walking according to the desires of your heart.
  Numbers 15:39 — Self-directed desire versus obedience: Sirach warns against following one's soul and strength in the heart's desires; Numbers directs remembrance of God's commandments instead of turning aside after one's thoughts and eyes.

Sirach 5:3
Do not say, “Who will have power over me?” For the Lord will certainly exact retribution.
  Psalms 11:5 — Defiant denial of accountability: Sirach rejects the boast that no one has power over the speaker; the psalm reports boastful speakers claiming their lips as their own and asking who is their lord.

Sirach 5:4
Do not say, “I sinned, and what happened to me?” For the Lord is patient.
  Ecclesiastes 8:11-12 — Misreading apparent impunity: Sirach rejects the sinner's claim that nothing happened after sin; Ecclesiastes says lack of prompt contradiction emboldens people to do evil. Both expose a false inference from apparent impunity.

Sirach 5:5
Do not become fearless about atonement, adding sin upon sins.
  Romans 6:1-2 — Presuming on pardon: Sirach forbids adding sins through careless confidence about atonement; Romans rejects remaining in sin so that grace may increase. Both oppose using divine pardon as permission to continue sinning.

Sirach 5:6
Do not say, “His compassion is great; He will atone for the multitude of my sins.” For mercy and wrath are with Him, and His anger will settle upon sinners.
  Romans 2:4-5 — Mercy does not excuse persistence: Sirach warns against assuming great compassion cancels continued sin; Romans says God's patience calls for repentance and warns that hard-heartedness stores up wrath.

Sirach 5:7
Do not delay turning to the Lord, and do not put it off from day to day. For the Lord’s wrath will go out suddenly, and at the time of retribution you will perish.
  Hebrews 3:13-15 — Urgency of repentance: Sirach forbids postponing return to the Lord; Hebrews presses the present call of Today and warns against hardening through sin's deceit. Both oppose deferring the response to God.

Sirach 5:8
Do not rely on unjust possessions, for they will be of no benefit to you on the day of calamity.
  Ezekiel 7:19 — Wealth fails in calamity: Sirach denies benefit from unjust possessions in calamity; Ezekiel describes discarded silver and disregarded gold failing to satisfy lives, associated with torment for injustices.

Sirach 5:9
Do not winnow in every wind or walk on every path; that is how the double-tongued sinner acts.
  James 1:8 — Unstable divided conduct: Sirach pictures the double-tongued sinner walking every path; James calls the divided-minded person unstable in all his ways. Both portray instability through inconsistent direction; James' warning concerns doubting trust rather than explicitly deceptive speech.

Sirach 5:10
Be firm in your understanding, and let your word be one.
  Matthew 5:37 — Consistency of one's word: Sirach calls for firm understanding and a single word; Jesus commands straightforward yes or no rather than additions from evil. Both demand dependable speech.

Sirach 5:11
Be quick in listening, and give your answer with patience.
  James 1:19 — Close verbal parallel: Sirach commands quick listening and a patient answer; James commands being quick to hear and slow to speak. The paired directions closely correspond.

Sirach 5:12
If you have understanding, answer your neighbor; if not, let your hand be over your mouth.
  Proverbs 17:28 — Silence when lacking understanding: Sirach tells the person without understanding to cover his mouth; Proverbs says one making himself mute will seem intelligent. Both commend silence over exposing one's lack of discernment.

Sirach 5:13
Glory and dishonor are in speech, and a person’s tongue can bring about his fall.
  Proverbs 18:21 — The tongue's consequences: Sirach places honor, dishonor and a person's fall in speech; Proverbs places death and life in the tongue's hand. Both emphasize speech's power over one's outcome.

Sirach 5:14
Do not be called a whisperer, and do not set an ambush with your tongue. For shame rests upon a thief, and harsh condemnation upon someone double-tongued.
  Proverbs 11:13 — Double-tongued betrayal: Sirach condemns the whisperer who sets a verbal ambush and the double-tongued person; Proverbs contrasts a double-tongued man revealing plans in a council with a faithful person concealing matters.

Sirach 5:15
In great and small matters do not act ignorantly, and do not become an enemy in place of a friend. For a bad name will inherit shame and reproach; that is how it is with the double-tongued sinner.
  Proverbs 16:28 — Corrupt conduct destroys friendship: Sirach warns against becoming an enemy in place of a friend and links this to double-tongued conduct; Proverbs' crooked, deceitful man separates friends.

Sirach 6:2
Do not lift yourself up in the counsel of your soul, lest your soul be torn apart like a bull.
  Proverbs 16:18 — Pride before ruin: Sirach warns that self-exaltation tears apart the soul; Proverbs places insolence before ruin and evil-mindedness before a fall. Both present self-aggrandizement as destructive.

Sirach 6:3
You will devour your leaves and destroy your fruits, and leave yourself like a dry tree.
  Jeremiah 17:5-8 — Fruitless tree imagery: Sirach pictures self-destructive conduct as devoured leaves, lost fruit and a dry tree; Jeremiah contrasts the desert plant of misplaced trust with the fruitful tree trusting the Lord.

Sirach 6:4
An evil soul will destroy its possessor and make him an object of his enemies’ gloating.
  Proverbs 11:5-6 — Wrongdoing destroys its possessor: Sirach says an evil soul destroys its owner; Proverbs contrasts rescuing righteousness with lawless people caught in their own destruction. Both locate ruin in one's own moral condition.

Sirach 6:7
If you acquire a friend, acquire him through testing, and do not trust him quickly.
  Sirach 12:8-9 — Friendship tested by adversity: Sirach warns against trusting a new friend quickly; chapter 12 says prosperity does not vindicate a friend and that even friends separate in adversity. The later warning supplies the test's reason.

Sirach 6:8
For there is a friend for his own occasion who will certainly not remain on the day of your affliction.
  Proverbs 19:4 — Conditional friendship: Sirach's opportunistic friend fails in affliction; Proverbs contrasts wealth adding friends with a poor person abandoned even by his existing friend. Both expose friendship dependent on circumstances.

Sirach 6:9
There is also a friend who turns to enmity and will expose a quarrel to your reproach.
  Psalms 54:13-15 — Former friend as adversary: Sirach warns that a friend can turn to enmity and reproach; the psalm laments reproach from a familiar companion with whom the speaker formerly shared food and worship.

Sirach 6:10
There is also a friend who shares your tables but will certainly not remain on the day of your affliction.
  Psalms 40:10 — Table fellowship without loyalty: Sirach warns that a companion at table may fail in affliction; the psalm describes the trusted man who ate the speaker's bread becoming treacherous. Shared food proves no guarantee of loyalty.

Sirach 6:12
if you are brought low, he will be against you and hide from your face.
  Proverbs 19:4 — Abandonment when brought low: Sirach's friend hides when his companion is brought low; Proverbs says the poor person is abandoned even by the friend he has. Both depict withdrawal precisely when help is needed.

Sirach 6:13
Separate yourself from your enemies, and be cautious with your friends.
  Sirach 12:10-12 — Guarded distance from enemies: Sirach commands separation from enemies before urging caution with friends; chapter 12 forbids trusting an enemy or stationing him beside oneself where he could displace the host. Both make distance from enemies a safeguard against hostile intentions.

Sirach 6:14
A faithful friend is a strong shelter; and whoever has found him has found a treasure.
  Proverbs 17:17 — A dependable friend in need: Sirach calls the faithful friend a strong shelter; Proverbs calls for a friend at all times and brothers useful in need. Both value the security of enduring companionship.

Sirach 6:15
There is no exchange for a faithful friend, and no weight to measure his excellence.
  Sirach 7:18 — Friendship beyond exchange value: Sirach denies an exchange price for a faithful friend; chapter 7 explicitly forbids trading a friend for profit or a true brother for gold.

Sirach 6:16
A faithful friend is medicine for life, and those who fear the Lord will find him.
  Sirach 6:14 — Same-chapter preserving-friend imagery: The faithful friend as medicine for life develops verse 14's strong-shelter image. Both metaphors portray loyal friendship as preserving the recipient, rather than as a purchasable possession.

Sirach 6:17
The one who fears the Lord will guide his friendship rightly, for as he is, so also is his neighbor.
  Proverbs 13:20 — Character shaped through friendship: Sirach links one's own character and that of one's neighbor in rightly guided friendship; Proverbs says walking with the wise makes a person wise. Both connect character with companionship.

Sirach 6:18
Child, choose instruction from your youth, and until your hair is gray you will find wisdom.
  2 Timothy 3:14-15 — Instruction beginning in youth: Sirach commends instruction from youth toward lifelong wisdom; Timothy is told to remain in learning grounded in sacred writings known from infancy, which can make him wise for salvation.

Sirach 6:19
Approach her like one who plows and sows, and wait for her good fruits. For you will labor a little in her cultivation and soon eat from her produce.
  James 5:7-8 — Patient cultivation imagery: Sirach likens pursuing wisdom to cultivating and awaiting good fruit; James uses a farmer awaiting precious fruit to teach patience. The objects of waiting differ, while the agricultural comparison corresponds.

Sirach 6:20
How very harsh she is to the uninstructed! The fainthearted will not remain with her.
  Proverbs 1:7 — Resistance to instruction: Sirach describes wisdom's severity for the uninstructed and the fainthearted person's failure to remain with her; Proverbs says the ungodly despise wisdom and discipline. Both depict resistance to instruction.

Sirach 6:21
She will be upon him like a heavy testing stone, and he will not delay throwing her away.
  Sirach 4:17 — Wisdom's initially burdensome discipline: Wisdom feels like a testing stone discarded by the fainthearted; chapter 4 describes her first bringing fear and tormenting discipline while testing a person's soul. Both explain initial resistance to her training.

Sirach 6:23
Listen, child, and receive my judgment; do not reject my counsel.
  Proverbs 4:1-2 — Paternal appeal to receive counsel: Sirach addresses a child and forbids rejecting counsel; Proverbs addresses children, calls for attention to a father's discipline, and forbids abandoning his law.

Sirach 6:24
Put your feet into her fetters and your neck into her collar.
  Sirach 51:26 — Wisdom's bonds as instruction: Sirach commands placing the neck in wisdom's collar; chapter 51 similarly commands placing the neck beneath a yoke to receive instruction. Both transform restraint into a learning metaphor.

Sirach 6:25
Place your shoulder beneath her and carry her, and do not resent her bonds.
  Matthew 11:29-30 — Accepted burden in teaching: Sirach urges willingly shouldering wisdom's bonds; Jesus invites hearers to take His yoke and learn from Him. Both picture willing submission to teaching as bearing a burden, without asserting identical speakers.

Sirach 6:26
Approach her with all your soul, and keep her ways with your whole strength.
  Deuteronomy 6:5 — Whole-person devotion: Sirach requires all one's soul and strength in pursuing wisdom; Deuteronomy requires love of God with all heart, soul and strength. The shared total-devotion language concerns wisdom and God respectively.

Sirach 6:27
Trace her out and seek her, and she will become known to you; when you have gained hold of her, do not let her go.
  Proverbs 2:3-5 — Searching and finding wisdom: Sirach tells the pupil to trace out and seek wisdom until she is known; Proverbs compares seeking wisdom with searching for silver and treasures, leading to knowledge of God.

Sirach 6:28
For in the end you will find her rest, and she will turn to gladness for you.
  Matthew 11:29 — Learning that brings rest: Sirach promises rest at the end of wisdom's discipline; Jesus promises rest for souls to those taking His yoke and learning from Him. The teaching-and-rest pattern is shared.

Sirach 6:29
Her fetters will become a strong shelter for you, and her collars a robe of glory.
  Sirach 4:17-18 — Discipline transformed into benefit: Wisdom's fetters become shelter and her collars become glory; chapter 4 likewise moves from fear and tormenting discipline to gladness and revelation. Both reverse the initial experience of her demands.

Sirach 6:31
You will put her on as a robe of glory and place a crown of rejoicing upon yourself.
  Proverbs 4:9 — Wisdom's crowning glory: Sirach portrays wisdom as a robe of glory and a crown of rejoicing; Proverbs says wisdom will give the learner a crown of graces and delight. Both use crowning to describe her reward.

Sirach 6:32
If you are willing, child, you will be instructed; if you give your soul to it, you will become skillful.
  Proverbs 2:1-5 — Willing reception enables learning: Sirach links willingness and giving one's soul to becoming instructed; Proverbs makes receiving the command and applying ear and heart conditions for understanding the fear of the Lord and finding knowledge of God.

Sirach 6:33
If you love to listen, you will receive; if you incline your ear, you will become wise.
  Proverbs 1:5 — Listening makes one wise: Sirach promises wisdom to one who loves listening and inclines the ear; Proverbs says a wise hearer becomes wiser and the discerning gain guidance.

Sirach 6:34
Stand among a multitude of elders, and if anyone is wise, cling to him.
  Job 12:12 — Learning among elders: Sirach directs the learner to elders and wise companions; Job says wisdom belongs with much time and knowledge with a long life. Both acknowledge experience as a place to seek understanding.

Sirach 6:35
Be willing to listen to every godly discourse, and do not let proverbs of understanding escape you.
  Proverbs 1:6 — Attending to wise discourse: Sirach commands attention to godly speech and proverbs of understanding; Proverbs names wise utterances, comparisons and riddles as things the discerning learner will understand.

Sirach 6:36
If you see someone understanding, rise early to go to him, and let your foot wear down the steps of his doors.
  Proverbs 8:34 — Attending at wisdom's doors: Sirach urges repeated early visits to an understanding person's doors; Proverbs' Wisdom blesses the listener watching daily at her entrances. Both picture persistent attendance as pursuit of wisdom.

Sirach 6:37
Consider the Lord’s ordinances and meditate continually on His commandments. He Himself will establish your heart, and the wisdom you desire will be given to you.
  Psalms 118:97-100 — Meditating on commandments gives wisdom: Sirach joins continuous meditation on the Lord's ordinances with receiving wisdom; the psalm's speaker attributes increased understanding to meditating on and seeking God's commandments.

Sirach 7:1
Do not do evil things, and evil will certainly not overtake you.
  Sirach 7:2 — Same-chapter parallel injunction: The prohibition of evil and promise that evil will not overtake the reader is restated in verse 2 as withdrawal from injustice so it turns aside. The paired formulation reinforces the ethical cause and outcome.

Sirach 7:2
Withdraw from injustice, and it will turn aside from you.
  Proverbs 16:17 — Avoiding evil preserves life: Sirach commands withdrawal from injustice; Proverbs calls life's paths those turning aside from evils and says guarding one's ways preserves life. Both connect avoidance with protection.

Sirach 7:3
Son, do not sow in the furrows of injustice, and you will certainly not reap them sevenfold.
  Proverbs 22:8 — Sowing and reaping wrongdoing: Sirach warns against sowing injustice and reaping it sevenfold; Proverbs says sowing worthless things yields evils. Both use agricultural consequences to describe wrongdoing's return.

Sirach 7:4
Do not seek leadership from the Lord or a seat of glory from a king.
  Proverbs 25:6-7 — Restraint of ambition: Sirach forbids seeking seats of leadership and royal glory; Proverbs forbids stationing oneself among rulers and warns against resulting humiliation. Both counsel restraint around high office.

Sirach 7:5
Do not justify yourself before the Lord, and do not make yourself out to be wise before a king.
  Luke 18:9-14 — Self-justification before God: Sirach forbids justifying oneself before the Lord; Jesus' parable contrasts the self-assured Pharisee with the penitent tax collector whom God justifies. The narrative illustrates the prohibition's danger.

Sirach 7:6
Do not seek to become a judge, lest you prove unable to remove injustices, lest you be afraid before someone powerful and place a stumbling block in your uprightness.
  Deuteronomy 1:16-17 — Fearless impartial judgment: Sirach warns that fear of a powerful person corrupts a judge's uprightness; Deuteronomy commands judges not to show partiality or shrink before a human face because judgment belongs to God.

Sirach 7:8
Do not bind a sin twice, for even with one you will not be innocent.
  Sirach 5:5 — Repeated sin adds guilt: Sirach forbids binding a sin twice and denies innocence even with one; chapter 5 likewise forbids adding sin upon sins through misplaced confidence about atonement.

Sirach 7:9
Do not say, “He will look upon the multitude of my gifts, and when I present an offering to God Most High, He will accept it.”
  Isaiah 1:11-17 — Offerings cannot replace repentance: Sirach rejects confidence that numerous gifts ensure divine acceptance; Isaiah rejects multiplied sacrifices and prayers from bloodstained hands and demands abandonment of evil and justice instead.

Sirach 7:10
Do not lose heart in your prayer, and do not neglect to give alms.
  Acts 10:2-4 — Persistent prayer and almsgiving: Sirach commands perseverance in prayer and almsgiving; Acts depicts Cornelius praying continually and giving many alms, both remembered before God.

Sirach 7:11
Do not laugh at someone in bitterness of soul, for there is One who brings low and raises high.
  Proverbs 17:5 — Mockery of suffering condemned: Sirach forbids laughing at someone in bitterness of soul; Proverbs condemns laughing at the poor and rejoicing over someone perishing, relating that conduct to the Maker's response.

Sirach 7:12
Do not plow a lie against your brother, or do the same to a friend.
  Leviticus 19:11 — Do not deceive one's neighbor: Sirach forbids devising a lie against brother or friend; Leviticus forbids lying and defrauding one's neighbor. Both prohibit deceit within personal relationships.

Sirach 7:13
Do not be willing to tell any lie, for persisting in it leads to no good.
  Ephesians 4:25 — Truth in ongoing speech: Sirach forbids willingness to tell any lie; Ephesians commands putting away falsehood and speaking truth to neighbors. Both reject deception as ordinary conduct.

Sirach 7:14
Do not chatter among a multitude of elders, and do not repeat a word in your prayer.
  Matthew 6:7-8 — Prayer without multiplying words: Sirach warns against repeating a word in prayer; Jesus warns against babbling and supposing many words secure a hearing. Both challenge quantity of speech as a devotional advantage.

Sirach 7:15
Do not hate laborious work or farming, which the Most High created.
  Genesis 2:15 — Work appointed by God: Sirach describes farming as work created by the Most High; Genesis places the man in God's garden to tend and keep it. The creation narrative gives a concrete instance of divinely appointed cultivation.

Sirach 7:16
Do not count yourself among the multitude of sinners; remember that wrath will not delay.
  Proverbs 1:10-15 — Refusing the company of sinners: Sirach forbids counting oneself among a multitude of sinners; Proverbs warns against accepting the ungodly group's invitation or walking their path toward violence and plunder.

Sirach 7:17
Humble your soul greatly, for the retribution of the ungodly is fire and the worm.
  Isaiah 66:24 — Judgment by fire and worm: Sirach names fire and the worm as the ungodly's retribution; Isaiah depicts transgressors with undying worm and unquenched fire. The paired punishment images closely correspond.

Sirach 7:18
Do not exchange a friend for profit or a true brother for gold of Souphir.
  Sirach 6:15 — Friendship beyond monetary price: Sirach forbids exchanging a friend or brother for profit or gold; chapter 6 says a faithful friend has no exchange price and his excellence cannot be measured by weight.

Sirach 7:19
Do not disregard a wise and good wife, for her grace is beyond gold.
  Proverbs 31:10 — A good wife exceeds material wealth: Sirach values a wise, good wife's grace above gold; Proverbs values the courageous woman above costly stones. Both state that a worthy woman surpasses precious possessions.

Sirach 7:20
Do not mistreat a household slave who works faithfully, or a hired worker who gives his soul to the task.
  Colossians 4:1 — Justice toward servants: Sirach forbids mistreating a faithful slave or dedicated hired worker; Colossians commands masters to provide slaves with justice and equity. Both impose duties upon those exercising household authority.

Sirach 7:21
Let your soul love an understanding household slave; do not deprive him of freedom.
  Deuteronomy 15:12-15 — Freedom for the servant: Sirach forbids depriving an understanding slave of freedom; Deuteronomy commands the Hebrew servant's seventh-year release with generous provision. The legal passage supplies a concrete release obligation, with its own stated scope.

Sirach 7:22
Do you have livestock? Look after them, and if they are useful to you, let them remain with you.
  Proverbs 27:23-27 — Care of livestock: Sirach commands attention to useful livestock; Proverbs commands knowing flocks' lives, tending herds and gathering fodder so sheep provide clothing. Both join husbandry to continued household provision.

Sirach 7:23
Do you have children? Discipline them, and bend their neck from youth.
  Proverbs 13:24 — Parental discipline: Sirach commands discipline of children beginning in youth; Proverbs connects love for a son with careful discipline rather than withholding the staff. Both frame correction as a parental responsibility.

Sirach 7:26
Do you have a wife after your heart? Do not put her away; and do not entrust yourself to an unloved woman.
  1 Corinthians 7:12 — Do not dismiss a wife: Sirach forbids dismissing a wife after one's heart; Paul forbids a brother dismissing an unbelieving wife willing to remain. Both require marital constancy, under different stated conditions.

Sirach 7:27
Glorify your father with your whole heart, and do not forget your mother’s labor pains.
  Exodus 20:12 — Honor for both parents: Sirach commands wholehearted glory to a father and remembrance of a mother's labor; Exodus commands honoring father and mother. Both bind the child to both parents.

Sirach 7:28
Remember that through them you were born; what will you repay them to match what they have given you?
  1 Timothy 5:4 — Repayment to forebears: Sirach asks how one can repay the parents through whom one was born; First Timothy tells children and grandchildren to repay forebears through household care, particularly for a widow.

Sirach 7:29
Revere the Lord with your whole soul, and honor His priests.
  Deuteronomy 18:3-5 — Priestly service and honor: Sirach joins reverence for the Lord with honoring His priests; Deuteronomy grounds priestly dues in God's choosing them to minister and bless in His name. Their divine service explains the commanded regard.

Sirach 7:30
Love your Maker with your whole strength, and do not abandon His ministers.
  Deuteronomy 12:19 — Do not abandon sacred ministers: Sirach commands love of the Maker and not abandoning His ministers; Deuteronomy specifically forbids forsaking the Levite throughout one's life in the land.

Sirach 7:31
Fear the Lord and glorify the priest; give him his portion, as you have been commanded: firstfruits, the guilt offering, the gift of shoulders, the sacrifice of sanctification, and the firstfruits of holy things.
  Deuteronomy 18:3-5 — Priestly portions and firstfruits: Sirach orders the priest's commanded portion, including shoulders and firstfruits; Deuteronomy specifies the foreleg and first produce as priestly dues. The lists overlap without being represented as identical.

Sirach 7:32
Stretch out your hand also to the poor person, so that your blessing may be complete.
  Deuteronomy 15:10 — Generosity accompanied by blessing: Sirach connects an open hand to the poor with completed blessing; Deuteronomy commands generous giving to the needy and promises God's blessing upon the giver's work.

Sirach 7:33
Let the favor of a gift extend to every living person, and do not withhold kindness from the dead.
  2 Samuel 2:5 — Narrative illustration of kindness to the dead: Sirach forbids withholding kindness from the dead; David calls burial of Saul and Jonathan an act of mercy and blesses those who performed it. Burial supplies a specific instance of that kindness.

Sirach 7:34
Do not hold back from those who weep, and mourn with those who mourn.
  Romans 12:15 — Close verbal parallel: Sirach commands mourning with those who mourn; Romans commands weeping with those who weep. Both require sharing another person's grief.

Sirach 7:35
Do not hesitate to visit a sick person, for through such deeds you will be loved.
  Matthew 25:36 — Visiting the sick: Sirach urges visiting a sick person; Jesus' judgment scene commends those who visited Him sick, explained as service to the least of His brothers. Both value personal presence with sufferers.

Sirach 7:36
In all your words remember your last things, and you will never sin.
  Psalms 38:2-6 — Speech considered in light of mortality: Sirach links remembering one's last things in every word with avoiding sin; the psalm joins guarding the tongue against sin with asking God to make one's limit and numbered days known.

Sirach 8:1
Do not fight with a powerful person, lest you fall into his hands.
  Proverbs 20:2 — Danger of provoking power: Sirach warns against fighting a powerful person and falling into his hands; Proverbs likens a king's threat to a lion and says provoking him imperils one's life.

Sirach 8:2
Do not quarrel with a rich person, lest he set his weight against yours; for gold has destroyed many and turned aside the hearts of kings.
  Proverbs 22:7 — Wealth and unequal power: Sirach warns that a rich person's weight can overwhelm an opponent; Proverbs explicitly says the rich rule the poor. The connection concerns economic power, not an imported borrower-servant clause absent from this edition.

Sirach 8:3
Do not fight with a talkative person, and do not pile wood upon his fire.
  Proverbs 26:20-21 — Contention as fuel for fire: Sirach forbids adding wood to a talkative person's fire; Proverbs compares wood sustaining fire with an abusive man sustaining battle. Both use fuel imagery for escalating conflict.

Sirach 8:5
Do not reproach someone turning away from sin; remember that we are all under penalties.
  Galatians 6:1 — Gentleness toward a sinner's recovery: Sirach forbids reproaching someone turning from sin and recalls shared liability; Galatians commands gentle restoration of one overtaken in transgression while remembering one's own vulnerability to temptation.

Sirach 8:6
Do not dishonor a person in his old age, for some of us too grow old.
  Leviticus 19:32 — Honor in old age: Sirach forbids dishonoring an elderly person; Leviticus commands rising before the grey-haired and honoring an elder. Both require respectful treatment of age.

Sirach 8:7
Do not rejoice over a dead person; remember that we all die.
  Ecclesiastes 7:2 — Mortality governs responses to death: Sirach forbids rejoicing over a dead person because all die; Ecclesiastes commends the house of mourning because death is every person's end and the living must take it to heart.

Sirach 8:8
Do not disregard the discourse of the wise; dwell among their proverbs, for from them you will learn instruction and how to serve people of high rank.
  Daniel 1:17-20 — Wisdom equips service before rulers: Sirach says wise discourse teaches understanding and service to high-ranking people; Daniel describes divinely given learning and understanding that distinguish the young men before the king and equip royal service.

Sirach 8:9
Do not neglect the discourse of the old, for they themselves learned from their fathers; from them you will learn understanding and how to give an answer at a time of need.
  Deuteronomy 32:7 — Learning from former generations: Sirach values elders' discourse because they learned from their fathers; Deuteronomy commands remembering earlier generations and asking fathers and elders to recount them.

Sirach 8:10
Do not kindle a sinner’s coals, lest you be burned in his blazing fire.
  Proverbs 26:21 — Provoked evil as burning coals: Sirach warns against kindling a sinner's coals and being burned; Proverbs joins coals and wood sustaining fire with an abusive person stirring battle. Both depict provocation through fire imagery.

Sirach 8:12
Do not lend to someone stronger than you; if you do lend, regard it as lost.
  Sirach 29:4-6 — Risk of an unrecoverable loan: Sirach warns that lending to someone stronger may mean losing the loan; chapter 29 describes borrowers delaying repayment, returning insults, and leaving lenders without their money. The later passage details the warned-of risk.

Sirach 8:13
Do not stand surety beyond your means; if you do stand surety, take thought as someone who will have to pay.
  Proverbs 22:26-27 — Surety beyond one's means: Sirach requires prospective guarantors to consider their ability to pay; Proverbs warns against pledging oneself when inability to pay may cost even one's bedding.

Sirach 8:14
Do not litigate with a judge, for they will decide in his favor in keeping with his prestige.
  Deuteronomy 16:19 — Warning about prohibited judicial bias: Sirach warns that a judge's prestige will influence a case in his favor; Deuteronomy forbids partiality and perverted judgment. The warning describes a danger the legal command condemns.

Sirach 8:15
Do not travel on a road with a reckless person, lest he become a burden against you. For he will act according to his own will, and you will perish along with him through his folly.
  Proverbs 22:24-25 — Hazardous companionship: Sirach warns that a reckless travel companion's willfulness can endanger another; Proverbs forbids lodging with an angry friend lest his ways become snares for one's life. Both require caution about a companion's conduct.

Sirach 8:16
Do not fight with a hot-tempered person or cross the wilderness with him. For blood is as nothing in his eyes, and where there is no help, he will strike you down.
  Proverbs 22:24-25 — Avoiding the hot-tempered companion: Sirach forbids traveling with a hot-tempered person whose violence can kill where help is absent; Proverbs forbids companionship with a quick-tempered man lest his ways entangle one's life.

Sirach 8:17
Do not take counsel with a fool, for he will not be able to keep a word concealed.
  Proverbs 11:13 — Confidential counsel requires trustworthiness: Sirach rejects counsel with a fool who cannot conceal a word; Proverbs contrasts a double-tongued revealer of plans with a faithful person who conceals matters.

Sirach 8:18
Do not do something secret before a stranger, for you do not know what it will bring forth.
  Micah 7:5 — Limits on entrusting private matters: Sirach warns against acting secretly before an unknown person; Micah warns against entrusting matters even to familiar companions. Both caution against exposing private matters to people whose reliability is not assured.

Sirach 8:19
Do not reveal your heart to every person, and do not let him return you a favor.
  Micah 7:5 — Reserve in personal confidence: Sirach forbids revealing one's heart to every person; Micah warns against confidence in friends, leaders and a bed companion. The link concerns guarded trust, not an interpretation of Sirach's final favor clause.
