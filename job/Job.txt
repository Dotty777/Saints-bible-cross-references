Job 1:1
A certain man lived in the land of Ausitis whose name was Job. That man was true, blameless, righteous, and God-fearing, keeping away from every evil deed.
  Genesis 6:9 — Character description parallel: Job and Noah are both introduced as righteous and blameless men before the accounts of calamity affecting their surroundings. The parallel concerns the narrators' character judgments, not an identification of the two men.
  Ezekiel 14:14 — Explicit later reference: Ezekiel names Job alongside Noah and Daniel as an example of righteousness, corresponding to the prologue's description of Job as righteous, blameless and God-fearing.
  Job 42:17b — Explicit narrative identification: The appendix again locates Job in Ausitis and adds its borders and the former name Jobab. This links the book's opening identification with its own closing identification without independently verifying the appendix's historical claims.

Job 1:2
He had seven sons and three daughters.
  Job 42:13 — Narrative restoration parallel: The seven sons and three daughters in the opening household correspond numerically to the seven sons and three daughters born to Job at the close. The later verse does not identify them as the children who died.

Job 1:3
His livestock numbered seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred pairs of oxen, and five hundred grazing female donkeys. He had a very large body of servants and great enterprises in the land. That man was of noble standing among those from the sunrise.
  Job 42:12 — Narrative restoration parallel: The closing inventory doubles each opening livestock total: sheep, camels, pairs of oxen and female donkeys. Both passages explicitly enumerate the same four categories of Job's wealth.

Job 1:4
His sons would gather with one another and hold a drinking feast each day, taking their three sisters along to eat and drink with them.
  Job 1:13 — Same-chapter literary recurrence: The recurring feasts involving Job's sons and daughters establish the family pattern repeated when the children gather to drink wine in their elder brother's house on the day of the losses.

Job 1:5
When the days of the drinking feast had ended, Job would send for them and purify them, rising in the morning and offering sacrifices for them according to their number, and one calf as a sin offering for their lives. For Job said, “Perhaps my sons have conceived evil thoughts toward God in their minds.” So Job did this continually.
  Job 42:8 — Intercession and sacrifice parallel: Job offers sacrifices for his children because they may have entertained evil thoughts toward God; later God directs his friends to obtain an offering and prayer through Job after their false speech. Both passages present Job interceding for others.

Job 1:6
When that day came, behold, the angels of God came to stand before the Lord, and the devil came with them.
  1 Kings 22:19-22 — Heavenly assembly comparison: Both scenes place heavenly beings before the Lord and include a spirit whose subsequent harmful activity occurs under an expressed commission or limit. The lying spirit in Micaiah's vision is not identified as Job's devil.
  Zechariah 3:1-2 — Accusation scene comparison: Job's devil appears in the assembly before the Lord, while Zechariah's slanderer stands beside Joshua to oppose him and receives the Lord's rebuke. Both scenes situate hostile spiritual opposition before divine authority.

Job 1:7
The Lord said to the devil, “Where have you come from?” The devil answered the Lord, “I have come after going around the earth and walking through the world beneath heaven.”
  1 Peter 5:8 — Adversary imagery parallel: The devil describes traveling around the earth in Job; Peter describes the devil walking about in search of someone to devour. The shared roaming image accompanies the adversary's threatening role.
  Job 2:2 — Repeated dialogue: The second heavenly scene repeats the Lord's question about the devil's origin and the devil's report of walking through the world, recalling this first exchange.

Job 1:8
The Lord said to him, “Have you paid attention in your mind to My servant Job? For among those on earth there is no man like him—blameless, true, God-fearing, keeping away from every evil deed.”
  Job 1:1 — Same-chapter narrative confirmation: The Lord's assessment of Job repeats the opening narrator's description of his innocence, truthfulness, fear of God and avoidance of evil. The coming losses are therefore not introduced as proof that Job was secretly wicked.
  Ezekiel 14:14 — Explicit later reference: The Lord calls attention to Job's outstanding righteousness here; Ezekiel subsequently names Job among three righteous men whose own righteousness would deliver them in the judgment described there.

Job 1:9
The devil answered before the Lord, “Does Job worship God for nothing?
  Job 2:4-5 — Repeated accusation: The first accusation asks whether Job's worship is disinterested; the second argues that preserving his life explains his endurance and proposes bodily harm. Both accusations challenge the motive behind his fidelity.
  Job 1:21-22 — Same-chapter accusation and outcome: The devil questions whether Job worships without reward, but after losing his possessions and children Job blesses the Lord and the narrator reports no sin. The ending of this trial answers the accusation through Job's response.

Job 1:10
Hast Thou not fenced in what is outside him and what is inside his house, and everything around all that belongs to him? Thou hast blessed the works of his hands and made his livestock numerous in the land.
  Job 29:2-6 — Retrospective description: The devil describes divine protection around Job's household and abundant possessions; Job later remembers God's guarding care, attention to his house, children around him and former abundance.

Job 1:11
But stretch out Thy hand and touch everything he has; surely he will curse Thee to Thy face.”
  Job 2:5 — Escalating test proposal: The first proposal predicts that taking Job's possessions will make him curse God; the second repeats that prediction while shifting the proposed blow to his bones and flesh.

Job 1:12
Then the Lord said to the devil, “Behold, everything he has I give into your hand, but do not touch him.” And the devil went out from the Lord.
  Job 2:6 — Contrasting limits on testing: The first permission excludes touching Job himself, whereas the second hands Job over but requires preservation of his life. The two scenes state different limits on the devil's activity.
  Luke 22:31-32 — Adversarial testing comparison: Job's trial proceeds after the devil's proposal and the Lord's limited permission. Jesus likewise speaks of Satan demanding to sift the disciples and of His prayer that Simon's faith not fail; the outcomes and limits are not identical.

Job 1:13
When that day came, Job’s sons and his daughters were drinking wine in the house of their elder brother.
  Job 1:4 — Same-chapter literary recurrence: The children drinking together in the elder brother's house repeats the family feasting pattern already described, identifying the ordinary gathering interrupted by the ensuing disaster.

Job 1:14
Behold, a messenger came to Job and said to him, “The pairs of oxen were plowing, and the female donkeys were grazing beside them.
  Job 1:3 — Same-chapter inventory recall: The first messenger names oxen and female donkeys, two of the livestock categories enumerated in Job's opening inventory. His report begins to connect the particular losses with that earlier account of wealth.

Job 1:15
The raiders came and carried them away, and killed the servants with swords. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  Job 1:17 — Same-chapter report parallel: Both reports describe attackers carrying off livestock, killing servants with swords and leaving one messenger to report. This edition calls the first attackers raiders and the later attackers horsemen, without identifying them as the same attacking group.

Job 1:16
While this one was still speaking, another messenger came and said to Job, “Fire fell from heaven and burned up the sheep and likewise consumed the shepherds. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  2 Kings 1:10-12 — Fire-from-heaven imagery: Job's messenger reports fire falling from heaven and consuming sheep and shepherds; the Elijah account describes fire from heaven consuming military companies. The similar description does not establish the same cause or moral status for the victims.

Job 1:17
While this one was still speaking, another messenger came and said to Job, “The horsemen formed three companies against us, surrounded the camels and carried them away, and killed the servants with swords. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  Job 1:15 — Same-chapter report parallel: The horsemen's attack repeats the earlier report's combination of stolen livestock, servants killed with swords and a sole surviving messenger, while involving camels rather than the earlier animals.

Job 1:18
While this one was still speaking, another messenger came, saying to Job, “While your sons and daughters were eating and drinking with their elder brother,
  Job 2:9b — Narrative recollection: The messenger recalls Job's sons and daughters eating together immediately before the collapse reported next; Job's wife later mourns those sons and daughters as the vanished memorial for whom she labored.

Job 1:19
a great wind suddenly came from the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house. The house fell on your children, and they died. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  Job 2:9b — Narrative recollection: The house's collapse kills Job's children; his wife's later lament specifically recalls the loss of the sons and daughters she bore. Her speech gives the mother's perspective on this reported catastrophe.
  Job 42:13 — Loss and later restoration: The deaths of Job's children precede the closing report that seven sons and three daughters were born to him. The connection follows the household's loss and later renewal without asserting that the later children are the same individuals.

Job 1:20
Then Job rose, tore his garments, and cut off the hair of his head. He fell to the ground, bowed in worship, and said:
  Job 2:12 — Mourning gesture parallel: Job tears his garments on receiving the reports, and his friends later tear their robes and weep when they see his condition. Both passages portray mourning through torn clothing.
  Ezra 9:3 — Mourning gesture comparison: Both Job and Ezra tear their garments and remove hair from their heads in distress. Job cuts his hair after bereavement, while Ezra plucks hair after hearing of covenant-breaking, so the shared gestures have different occasions.

Job 1:21
“I myself came out naked from my mother’s womb, and naked I shall depart there: the Lord gave, and the Lord took away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so it happened: blessed be the name of the Lord.”
  Ecclesiastes 5:14-15 — Close verbal and thematic parallel: Both passages describe coming naked from the mother's womb and departing as one came. Ecclesiastes emphasizes that no earnings can be carried away, corresponding to Job's acknowledgment that what was given can be taken.
  1 Timothy 6:7 — Possessions and mortality parallel: Job contrasts his naked arrival and departure with the Lord's giving and taking; First Timothy says that nothing was brought into the world and nothing can be carried out. Both detach possessions from the beginning and end of life.

Job 1:22
In all these things that happened to him, Job did not sin at all before the Lord or attribute foolishness to God.
  Job 2:10 — Repeated narrative judgment: After the first losses the narrator says Job did not sin or attribute foolishness to God; after bodily affliction the narrator again reports that he did not sin with his lips before God.
  James 5:11 — Explicit later reference: The narrator's judgment that Job remained without sin through these losses supplies an episode of the endurance James explicitly recalls when naming Job and the Lord's compassionate outcome.

Job 2:1
When that day came, the angels of God came to stand before the Lord, and the devil came among them to stand before the Lord.
  Job 1:6 — Repeated heavenly scene: The second scene repeats the angels' appearance before the Lord and the devil's presence among them, reopening the setting in which the first trial was authorized.

Job 2:2
The Lord said to the devil, “Where are you coming from?” Then the devil said before the Lord, “I have come after traveling through the world beneath heaven and walking through all of it.”
  Job 1:7 — Repeated dialogue: The Lord again asks where the devil has come from, and the devil again reports traveling through the world. The repeated exchange frames the second challenge as a continuation of the first.

Job 2:3
The Lord said to the devil, “Have you then paid attention to My servant Job? For among those on earth there is no man like him—innocent, true, blameless, God-fearing, keeping away from every evil. He still holds to innocence, but you said to destroy his belongings for nothing.”
  Job 1:8 — Repeated assessment with development: The Lord again describes Job's exceptional innocence and fear of God, now adding that he still holds his innocence after the destruction of his possessions. The earlier assessment is expressly maintained after the first test.

Job 2:4
The devil replied to the Lord, “Skin for skin! Whatever a person has, he will pay for his life.
  Matthew 16:26 — Life and possessions comparison: The devil claims that a person will pay whatever he possesses to preserve his life; Jesus asks what profit the whole world offers if life is forfeited and what can be given in exchange for life. The valuation is comparable without endorsing the devil's accusation.

Job 2:5
But indeed, stretch out Thy hand and touch his bones and his flesh; surely he will curse Thee to Thy face.”
  Job 1:11 — Escalating test proposal: The prediction that Job will curse God repeats the first proposal, but the object of the requested blow changes from possessions to bones and flesh. This identifies the second trial's escalation.

Job 2:6
The Lord said to the devil, “Behold, I hand him over to you; only preserve his life.”
  Job 1:12 — Contrasting limits on testing: The Lord now permits harm to Job but requires that his life be preserved. The earlier permission placed his possessions in the devil's hand while forbidding harm to Job himself.

Job 2:7
The devil went out from the Lord and struck Job with a grievous ulcer from his feet to his head.
  Deuteronomy 28:35 — Close bodily description with contextual difference: Both passages describe a grievous ulcer extending from the feet toward the head. Deuteronomy places it among covenant curses, whereas Job's prologue explicitly calls him innocent; the shared description does not prove Job's guilt.
  2 Corinthians 12:7 — Adversarial bodily affliction comparison: Job's ulcer is inflicted by the devil, while Paul describes a thorn in the flesh and a messenger of Satan striking him. Both connect affliction with hostile spiritual agency, but Paul's unspecified thorn is not identified as Job's ulcer.

Job 2:8
He took a potsherd to scrape off the discharge, and sat on the dung heap outside the city.
  Job 7:5 — Explicit bodily recollection: The narrator describes Job scraping discharge with a potsherd; Job later describes his decaying body and scraping off its discharge. The later complaint recalls the same distinctive bodily action.
  Lamentations 4:5 — Reversal of former status: Job, introduced as wealthy, now sits on a dung heap; Lamentations describes people formerly nursed upon scarlet wrapping themselves in dung heaps. The unusual setting marks a collapse from former prosperity in both scenes.

Job 2:9
After much time had passed, his wife said to him, “How long will you persist, saying,
  Job 2:3 — Same-chapter contrast over persistence: Job's wife questions how long he will persist, whereas the Lord has just commended him for still holding to innocence. Her challenge and the divine assessment take different views of his continuing endurance.

Job 2:9a
‘Behold, I will wait a little while longer, expecting the hope of my deliverance’?
  Job 13:16-18 — Hope of deliverance parallel: His wife's quotation attributes to Job an expectation of deliverance; Job later says that speaking before God will become deliverance and that he expects to be shown righteous. Both passages explicitly voice that expectation.

Job 2:9b
For behold, your memorial has vanished from the earth: sons and daughters, the pangs and pains of my womb, for whom I labored in vain with hardships.
  Job 1:19 — Explicit recollection of bereavement: The wife's lament names the sons and daughters she bore as lost; the earlier messenger reports the house collapsing on Job's children and killing them.

Job 2:9c
You yourself sit amid the decay of worms, spending the night in the open air,
  Job 7:3-5 — Bodily suffering recollection: The wife describes Job among worm-decay through the night; his later complaint joins nights of pain with a body fouled by worm-decay and discharge. The correspondence concerns these particular features of the suffering.

Job 2:9d
while I am a wanderer and a hired servant, going from place to place and from house to house, waiting for the sun to set so that I may rest from the hardships and pains that now hold me fast.
  Job 7:1-2 — Hired-worker imagery: The wife describes her own wandering hired service and waiting for sunset to rest; Job later compares human life to hired work and a servant obtaining shade. The later comparison is an image, not a claim that Job shares her employment.

Job 2:9e
But say some word to the Lord and die.”
  Job 2:10 — Same-chapter opposing counsel: The wife urges a word to the Lord and death; Job answers that good received from the Lord must be accompanied by bearing bad things. His response opposes her counsel, while this edition's phrase 'some word' is left unchanged.

Job 2:10
He looked at her and said, “You have spoken like one of the foolish women. If we received good things from the Lord’s hand, shall we not bear the bad things?” In all these things that happened to him, Job did not sin at all with his lips before God.
  Job 1:21-22 — Repeated response to adversity: Job's acceptance of bad as well as good from the Lord recalls his acknowledgment of the Lord's giving and taking. Both responses are followed by the narrator's statement that Job did not sin.

Job 2:11
His three friends heard of all the bad things that had come upon him and came to him, each from his own country: Eliphaz king of the Temanites, Bildad ruler of the Shuhites, and Zophar king of the Minaeans. They came to him together to comfort him and visit him.
  Job 42:7-9 — Narrative resolution: The three named friends arrive to comfort Job; the ending names the same men when God rebukes their speech and directs them to seek an offering and prayer through Job.
  Job 42:17e — Explicit identification of the friends: The appendix repeats the three friends' names and titles: Eliphaz king of the Temanites, Bildad ruler of the Shuhites and Zophar king of the Minaeans. It directly recalls the group introduced here.
  Job 16:2 — Narrative contrast: The friends initially come to comfort Job, but he later calls them all comforters of evils. The later rebuke contrasts the stated purpose of their visit with Job's experience of their speech.

Job 2:12
When they saw him from far away, they did not recognize him. Crying out loudly, they wept, each tearing his own robe and sprinkling earth upon himself.
  Job 1:20 — Mourning gesture parallel: The friends' torn robes and grief correspond to Job's earlier tearing of garments after the losses. The friends now participate in outward mourning for the suffering they see.

Job 2:13
They sat beside him seven days and seven nights, and none of them spoke, for they saw that his affliction was terrible and exceedingly great.
  Ezekiel 3:15-16 — Seven-day presence comparison: The friends sit beside the afflicted Job for seven days and nights before speaking; Ezekiel sits among the exiles for seven days before the next divine word. Both narratives mark an extended seven-day presence before subsequent speech, without equating the events.
  Job 13:5 — Silence and subsequent speech contrast: The friends initially refrain from speaking because of Job's great affliction. After their speeches, Job wishes that they would be silent and says that silence would count as wisdom for them.

Job 3:1
After this Job opened his mouth and cursed his day,
  Jeremiah 20:14 — Birth-day lament parallel: Job opens his lament by cursing his day; Jeremiah explicitly curses the day of his birth. Both passages introduce a sufferer's repudiation of the day on which his life began.

Job 3:3
“May the day perish on which I was born, and the night in which they said, ‘Behold, a male child!’
  Jeremiah 20:14-15 — Close birth-announcement parallel: Both laments curse the day of birth and recall the announcement of a male child. Job wishes the day and announcement-night to perish, while Jeremiah also curses the bearer of the birth announcement.

Job 3:4
May that day be darkness; may the Lord above not seek it out, and may no brightness come upon it.
  Amos 8:9-10 — Darkened-day imagery: Job wishes his birth-day deprived of brightness; Amos depicts daylight becoming dark as festivals turn to mourning. The darkness accompanies anguish in both passages, but Amos's announced judgment is not identified with Job's birth-day.

Job 3:5
May darkness and death’s shadow take possession of it; may gloom come upon it.
  Job 10:21-22 — Recurring darkness imagery: Job wishes darkness and death's shadow to claim his birth-day; later he describes the destination before him as a gloomy land with no brightness. The later lament develops the same association between darkness and death.

Job 3:6
May that day and night be cursed; may darkness carry it away. May it not belong among the days of the year or be numbered among the days of the months.
  Job 10:18-19 — Wish for erased existence: Removing his birth-day from the calendar expresses Job's repudiation of his beginning. Later he wishes he had died unseen and become as though he had never existed, making the underlying wish explicit.

Job 3:7
But may that night be pain, and may neither gladness nor joy come upon it.
  Jeremiah 20:15 — Birth-joy reversal: Job wishes the birth-night stripped of gladness and joy; Jeremiah curses the messenger who announced a boy's birth with rejoicing. Both laments reverse the joy normally attached to that announcement.

Job 3:9
May the stars of that night be darkened; may it wait and not come into light, and may it not see the morning star rising,
  Amos 5:18-20 — Expected light denied: Job imagines a night waiting for light but receiving none; Amos warns those desiring the Lord's day that it is darkness without brightness. Both overturn an expectation of light, in distinct lament and judgment settings.

Job 3:10
because it did not close the gates of my mother’s womb; for it would have removed pain from my eyes.
  Jeremiah 20:17-18 — Womb and suffering parallel: Job blames the night for failing to close the womb and spare him pain. Jeremiah likewise wishes he had died in the womb and asks why he emerged to experience labors and pains.

Job 3:11
For why did I not die in the womb, come out from the belly and immediately perish?
  Job 10:18-19 — Repeated question about birth: Job asks why he did not die as he emerged from the womb; later he repeats the question and imagines being taken directly from womb to tomb.

Job 3:12
Why did knees meet me? Why did I suck breasts?
  Psalms 21:10-11 — Contrasting recollections of infancy: Job questions why he was received and nursed; the psalmist remembers being drawn from the womb and finding hope from his mother's breasts. The common birth-and-nursing imagery serves lament in Job and a plea grounded in former care in the psalm.
  Isaiah 66:11-12 — Nursing and knees imagery: Job names breasts and welcoming knees while questioning his survival; Isaiah combines nursing at a comforting breast with children comforted on knees. The same nurturing images have contrasting emotional force.

Job 3:13
Now I would have lain down and been quiet; I would have slept and rested
  Job 14:12 — Death-as-sleep imagery: Job imagines that early death would have meant lying quietly, sleeping and resting. Later he describes the person who lies down in death and does not wake from sleep until heaven is no longer stitched together.

Job 3:14
with kings, counselors of the earth, who exulted in swords,
  Isaiah 14:9-11 — Kings among the dead: Job imagines resting with kings and counselors of the earth; Isaiah's taunt depicts former rulers and kings meeting the fallen Babylonian king in Hades. Royal standing does not keep either passage's rulers from the company of the dead.

Job 3:15
or with rulers who had much gold, who filled their houses with silver,
  Psalms 48:7-12 — Wealth and the grave: Job places rulers with houses full of silver among the dead. The psalm likewise describes those boasting in wealth, leaving it to strangers and having tombs as their enduring houses.

Job 3:16
or like a miscarriage coming out of a mother’s womb, or like infants who never saw light.
  Ecclesiastes 6:3-5 — Stillbirth and unseen light parallel: Job wishes for the condition of a miscarriage or infants who never saw light. Ecclesiastes similarly compares a troubled long life with a stillborn child who never saw the sun and is said to have more rest.

Job 3:17
There the ungodly kindled the fury of anger; there those worn out in body rested.
  Isaiah 14:3-4 — Rest from suffering comparison: Job's second clause imagines rest for bodies worn out by suffering. Isaiah announces rest from pain and harsh slavery before a taunt over the fallen oppressor; the comparison concerns relief from suffering, not Job's separate clause about the ungodly's anger.

Job 3:19
Small and great are there, and the servant does not fear his master.
  Job 21:23-26 — Death levels earthly distinctions: Job places small and great together where the servant no longer fears a master. Later he contrasts a prosperous death with a bitter one, then says both people sleep together in the earth and decay covers them.

Job 3:20
For why has light been given to those in bitterness, and life to souls in pain,
  Jeremiah 20:18 — Questioning life amid pain: Job asks why light and life are given to the bitter and suffering; Jeremiah asks why he left the womb to see labors and pains. Both laments question the purpose of continuing life under distress.

Job 3:21
who long for death and do not obtain it, digging for it as for treasures,
  Jonah 4:3 — Longing for death comparison: Job describes sufferers longing for death without obtaining it; Jonah asks the Lord to take his life because he regards death as better than living. The shared longing arises in different narrative circumstances and is not a command to readers.

Job 3:22
and become overjoyed if they obtain it?
  Ecclesiastes 4:2 — Preference for death in lament: Job pictures rejoicing if death is obtained, while Ecclesiastes, after describing extortion, praises the dead above those still living. Both passages express a sufferer's or observer's preference for death over distressing life.

Job 3:23
Death is rest for a man, for God has shut him in.
  Lamentations 3:7-9 — Divine confinement imagery: Job describes God shutting a man in; the speaker in Lamentations describes God building against him, blocking his exit and obstructing his paths. Both use confinement to describe their experience of affliction.
  Job 1:10 — Protective enclosure reversed: The devil earlier described God's fencing around Job as protection for household and possessions. Job now experiences God's enclosing action as confinement, reversing the earlier account's favorable meaning.

Job 3:24
For sighing comes to me before my food, and I weep, gripped by fear.
  Psalms 41:4 — Tears and food imagery: Job describes sighing before his food and weeping under fear; the psalmist says tears became his bread day and night. Both connect sustained grief with the experience of eating.

Job 3:25
For the fear I thought about came upon me, and what I had feared met me.
  Job 30:26 — Contrasting expectations of calamity: Job says that feared trouble met him; later he recalls waiting for good things but meeting days of evils instead. Both recollections describe the arrival of calamity in relation to his earlier expectations.

Job 3:26
I had no peace, no quiet, no rest; yet wrath came upon me.”
  Job 7:3-4 — Restlessness in affliction: Job says he has neither peace, quiet nor rest. His later complaint describes empty months and nights of pain, continually waiting for day or evening without relief.

Job 4:2
“Have you not often been spoken to amid trouble? Who will endure the force of your words?
  Job 6:26 — Dispute about enduring the other's words: Eliphaz asks who can endure the force of Job's words; Job replies that he will not endure even the sound of a word from his reprovers. The repeated challenge belongs to their disagreement, not a narrator's judgment that either speech is worthless.

Job 4:3
For if you admonished many and encouraged a weak person’s hands,
  Isaiah 35:3-4 — Encouraging weak hands: Eliphaz depicts Job as having encouraged weak hands; Isaiah calls limp hands and paralyzed knees to strength and urges encouragement of the fainthearted. The shared bodily language describes encouragement in distinct settings.

Job 4:4
and raised up the weak with words and put courage into powerless knees,
  Hebrews 12:12-13 — Strengthening powerless knees: Eliphaz credits Job's words with raising the weak and strengthening powerless knees. Hebrews likewise urges the strengthening of weakened knees, with healing for the lame; it does not thereby endorse Eliphaz's subsequent accusation against Job.

Job 4:5
yet now pain has come upon you and touched you, and you have become agitated.
  Job 2:7-8 — The counselor's own affliction: Eliphaz contrasts Job's earlier help to others with his agitation when pain touches him. The prologue supplies the particular affliction: a grievous ulcer and scraping its discharge. Eliphaz's appraisal of Job's response remains his own.

Job 4:7
Remember, then: who perished while pure, or when were true people utterly uprooted?
  Job 2:3 — The innocence assumed by the prologue: Eliphaz's question about whether pure people perish is read against God's prior declaration that Job still holds to innocence despite the destruction of his belongings for nothing. The prologue prevents treating the question as proof that Job must be guilty.

Job 4:8
As I have seen those who plow improper things, those who sow them will reap pains for themselves.
  Proverbs 22:8 — Sowing wrongdoing and reaping harm: Eliphaz describes those sowing improper things as reaping pains; Proverbs likewise says one who sows worthless things reaps evils. The shared agricultural maxim does not establish that Job committed the wrongdoing Eliphaz implies.

Job 4:12
But if some true word had been among your words, none of these evils would have met you. Will not my ear receive extraordinary things from Him?
  Job 42:7-8 — Truthful speech and the closing rebuke: Eliphaz says true words would have prevented Job's evils. The closing divine judgment instead rebukes Eliphaz and his friends for failing to speak truth before God and against Job. That verdict checks the accusation rather than establishing Job's guilt.

Job 4:13
There were terrors and a nocturnal sound, fear falling upon people;
  Job 7:13-14 — Terrifying experiences at night: Eliphaz introduces his reported experience with nocturnal terror; Job later says hoped-for comfort on his bed is overcome by frightening dreams and visions. These are different speakers' experiences, not one identified vision or visitor.

Job 4:17
‘What then? Can a mortal be pure before the Lord, or a man blameless on account of his works?
  Psalms 142:2 — Mortal righteousness before God: The voice reported by Eliphaz questions whether a mortal can be pure before the Lord; the psalmist asks not to enter judgment because no living person will be justified before God. The parallel does not authenticate Eliphaz's unidentified voice or diagnose Job's suffering.

Job 4:19
as for those who inhabit houses of clay, from which we ourselves also are, of the same clay, He struck them as one does a moth.
  Genesis 2:7 — Human bodies described through earth: Eliphaz's reported voice calls humans inhabitants of clay houses, themselves of clay. Genesis describes God forming the human from earth's dust. The comparison concerns earthen human formation, not a literal clay building or the voice's further moth imagery.

Job 4:20

Job 4:21

Job 5:2
For anger kills a fool, and jealousy puts one who has gone astray to death.

Job 5:3

Job 5:7

Job 5:9
who does great and unsearchable things, glorious and extraordinary things beyond numbering,
  Job 9:10 — Job repeats the description of divine wonders: Job later repeats Eliphaz's description of God doing great, unsearchable and innumerable extraordinary things. The shared wording does not make their arguments identical: Job's surrounding reply wrestles with judgment and the limits of answering God.

Job 5:10
who gives rain upon the earth and sends water upon the world beneath heaven,
  Matthew 5:45 — Rain given across the earth: Eliphaz includes rain upon the earth among God's works. Jesus describes the Father's rain falling on righteous and unrighteous alike. Their accounts share the divine provision of rain, while Jesus explicitly names a moral breadth that cannot be used to infer a sufferer's guilt.

Job 5:11
who raises the lowly to a height and rouses those who have perished,
  1 Samuel 2:6-8 — Raising the lowly and the dead: Eliphaz describes God raising the lowly and rousing those who have perished. Hannah's song pairs bringing up from Hades with lifting the needy from earth to seats of honor. The corresponding reversals do not validate Eliphaz's application to Job.

Job 5:12
who changes the counsels of the unscrupulous, and their hands will certainly do nothing true.
  Psalms 32:10-11 — Human counsels made ineffective: Eliphaz says God changes unscrupulous counsels and frustrates their hands. The psalm describes the Lord nullifying the counsels of nations and rulers while His own counsel remains. The actors and wording differ, and neither identifies Job as one of those plotters.

Job 5:13
He catches the wise in their understanding and throws the counsel of the devious into confusion.
  1 Corinthians 3:18-20 — The saying about catching the wise cited: Paul cites the written saying that God catches the wise in their craftiness when warning against worldly wisdom. Eliphaz's saying uses understanding and adds the confusion of devious counsel. Paul's use of this saying does not endorse Eliphaz's entire argument against Job.

Job 5:14

  Deuteronomy 28:28-29 — Groping at noon as in darkness: Eliphaz portrays the devious meeting darkness by day and groping at noon as at night. Deuteronomy's curse likewise describes groping at noon as the blind grope in darkness. The shared image does not establish that Job is under that covenant curse.

Job 5:15
May they perish in war, but may the powerless escape from a ruler’s hand.
  Psalms 34:10 — The powerless rescued from stronger hands: Eliphaz wishes that the powerless escape a ruler's hand. The psalm praises the Lord for rescuing the poor from those stronger than they are and from plunderers. This matches the rescue image, not Eliphaz's accompanying wish about death in war.

Job 5:16
May the powerless have hope, and may an unjust person’s mouth be stopped.
  Psalms 106:41-42 — The poor helped and lawlessness silenced: Eliphaz joins hope for the powerless with an unjust mouth being stopped. The psalm joins help for the poor with the upright rejoicing and lawlessness stopping its mouth. The paired reversal is shared without identifying the speakers' occasions.

Job 5:17
Happy is the person whom the Lord reproved; do not refuse the Almighty’s admonition.
  Proverbs 3:11-12 — Receiving divine correction: Eliphaz calls the person reproved by the Lord happy and warns against refusing admonition. Proverbs similarly warns against making light of the Lord's discipline and connects correction with love. The counsel's parallel does not prove that Job's affliction is punishment for wrongdoing.

Job 5:18
For He causes pain and restores again; He struck, and His hands healed.
  Deuteronomy 32:39 — The one who wounds also heals: Eliphaz says God causes pain and restores, strikes and heals. The divine declaration in Moses' song likewise pairs smiting with healing. The shared pair of actions does not resolve why Job suffers or authenticate Eliphaz's diagnosis.

Job 5:20
In famine He will rescue you from death, and in war He will release you from the hand of iron.
  Psalms 32:18-19 — Rescue from famine and death: Eliphaz promises rescue from death in famine. The psalm describes the Lord watching those who fear Him to rescue their souls from death and nourish them in famine. Its promise does not repeat Eliphaz's further assurance about warfare.

Job 5:21
He will hide you from the lash of the tongue, and you will not fear coming evils.
  Psalms 30:20-21 — Shelter from hostile tongues: Eliphaz promises concealment from the tongue's lash. The psalm describes shelter in God's presence from human turmoil and in a tent from the contradiction of tongues. These are corresponding images of protection from harmful speech.

Job 5:22
You will laugh at unjust and lawless people; you will not fear wild beasts,
  Leviticus 26:3-6 — Safety from frightening beasts: Eliphaz promises that Job will not fear wild beasts. Leviticus's covenant blessing promises peace, rest without fear and removal of harmful animals. The parallel does not make Eliphaz's personal assurance the covenant's own verdict about Job.

Job 5:23
for wild beasts will be at peace with you.
  Hosea 2:20 — Peace involving wild animals: Eliphaz promises that wild beasts will be at peace with Job. Hosea's restoration promise establishes a covenant with field beasts, birds and creeping things and ends warfare. The scope and recipients differ; Hosea is not a record of Eliphaz's promise being enacted.

Job 5:25
You will know that your offspring are many, and your children will be like all the vegetation of the field.
  Job 42:13-16 — Promised offspring and the narrated ending: Eliphaz promises Job many offspring. The ending records seven sons, three daughters and later generations seen by Job. The narrative outcome can be compared with the assurance without overturning the closing rebuke of Eliphaz's speech.

Job 5:26
You will come to the grave like ripe grain harvested at the proper time, or like a heap on the threshing floor gathered in its season.
  Job 42:16-17 — A full life before death: Eliphaz imagines Job reaching the grave like grain harvested in season. The ending records his extended life and death elderly and full of days. It supplies a comparable outcome, without using the grain image or validating Eliphaz's account of the suffering's cause.

Job 5:27
Behold, we have searched these things out in this way; these are the things we have heard. But know for yourself whether you have done anything.”
  Job 42:7-8 — Claimed investigation tested by the closing verdict: Eliphaz concludes that he and his companions have searched out what they say. The Lord's closing rebuke of Eliphaz and his two friends prevents treating that claim of investigation as a guarantee that their accusations against Job are true.

Job 6:2
“If only someone would weigh my anger and lift my pains together in a balance!
  Job 31:5-6 — A requested weighing: Job wishes his anger and pains could be weighed together. Later he asks to be set in a righteous balance while declaring that the Lord knows his innocence. The same weighing image serves distinct purposes: measuring distress and testing conduct.

Job 6:3
It would indeed be heavier than the sand beside the sea; but, it seems, my words are worthless.
  Proverbs 27:3 — Anger compared with sand's weight: Job says his anger and pains would outweigh the sand beside the sea. Proverbs compares a fool's anger with heavy stone and sand. The weight comparison is shared, but Job is not thereby classified as the proverb's fool.

Job 6:4
For the Lord’s arrows are in my body; their fury drinks up my blood. When I begin to speak, they stab me.
  Psalms 37:2-4 — Divine arrows and bodily suffering: Job describes the Lord's arrows lodged in his body and their fury. The psalmist likewise describes God's arrows lodged in him and bodily distress under divine anger. The psalmist's explicit statement about his sins is not imported as Job's confession.

Job 6:5
What then? Does a wild donkey cry out for nothing, rather than seeking food? Would an ox raise its voice at a manger when it has food?
  Amos 3:4-5 — Animal behavior used in questions about causes: Job asks whether a donkey cries without seeking food or an ox cries when fed. Amos asks whether a lion calls without prey or a trap springs without a catch. Both arguments use questions about animal behavior and its occasion; hunger and possession of prey remain different situations.

Job 6:6
Will bread be eaten without salt? Is there any taste in empty words?
  Colossians 4:6 — Speech compared with seasoned food: Job places unsalted bread beside the lack of taste in empty words. Colossians directs that speech be gracious and seasoned with salt. The shared food-and-speech image serves a complaint in Job and positive instruction in the letter.

Job 6:9
Let the Lord begin and wound me, but let Him not destroy me completely.
  Job 2:6 — A limit short of complete destruction: Job asks the Lord to wound him but not destroy him completely. The prologue permits the devil to afflict him while preserving his life. The comparison preserves both the limit and the different agents; this version is not rewritten as an unqualified request to be killed.

Job 6:10
May my city be a grave, on whose walls I used to leap; I will certainly not hold back. For I have not falsified the holy words of my God.
  Job 4:12 — Reply about truthful speech: Job says he has not falsified God's holy words. Eliphaz had claimed that true words would have prevented these evils. Job's assertion responds to the truthfulness at issue in that accusation, without requiring an explanation of the separate grave-and-city image.

Job 6:12
Is my strength the strength of stones? Is my flesh made of bronze?
  Job 40:15-18 — Human frailty and metallic bodily strength: Job asks whether his flesh is bronze. In the later divine speech the described grass-eating creature has bronze ribs and a cast-iron spine. The contrast concerns imagined bodily strength; it does not identify Job with that creature or supply a species name absent from the passage.

Job 6:14
Mercy has disowned me, and the Lord’s care has overlooked me.
  Isaiah 49:14-15 — The complaint of being forgotten: Job says mercy has disowned him and the Lord's care overlooked him. Zion similarly complains that the Lord abandoned and forgot her, before receiving an answer of enduring care. The shared complaint is attributed to its speakers; Zion's answer is not presented as a direct speech to Job.

Job 6:15
Those nearest me paid no attention to me; like a failing winter torrent or a wave they passed me by.
  Jeremiah 15:18 — Unreliable water as an image in complaint: Job compares neglectful companions to a failing winter torrent. Jeremiah's complaint describes false water having no faithfulness. The water image expresses disappointed reliance in both; the target's wording is not rewritten to name Job's companions or explicitly call God a dried-up stream.

Job 6:18
so I too was abandoned by everyone; I perished and became homeless.
  Job 42:11 — Abandonment and the later return of acquaintances: Job laments being abandoned by everyone and becoming homeless. The ending records brothers, sisters and former acquaintances coming to eat, drink and console him. That later return contrasts with his reported isolation, without equating every visitor with the three debating friends.

Job 6:20
Those who trust in cities and possessions will incur shame.
  Proverbs 11:28 — Reliance on possessions fails: Job warns that those trusting cities and possessions incur shame. Proverbs says the one trusting wealth will fall. The connection concerns the expressly named reliance on possessions, not a claim that the passages describe the same people or a particular ruined city.

Job 6:21
But you too have come upon me without mercy, so that on seeing my wound you became afraid.
  Job 2:11-13 — Comfort intended and mercy disputed: Job charges the visitors with coming without mercy after seeing his wound. The prologue says the friends came to comfort him and sat silent before his great affliction. His later accusation is contrasted with that stated purpose, not substituted for the narrator's account.

Job 6:24
Teach me, and I will be silent; if I have gone astray in anything, tell me.
  Job 13:20-23 — Asking for specified wrongdoing: Job challenges his friends to teach him where he has gone astray. Later he asks God to teach him what his sins and lawless deeds are. The repeated demand seeks concrete identification of wrongdoing; the addressees differ.

Job 6:26
Your reproof will not stop me with words, for I will not endure even the sound of a word from you.
  Job 4:2 — Refusing to endure the other's words: Job says he will not endure even the sound of the friends' reproof. Eliphaz had asked who could endure the force of Job's words. This is a particular verbal reversal within the dispute, not a judgment that the entire exchange lacks value.

Job 6:27
Except that you fall upon an orphan and leap upon your friend.
  Job 22:9 — Accusations involving orphans: Job accuses his friends of falling upon an orphan and leaping upon a friend. Eliphaz later accuses Job of mistreating orphans and sending widows away empty. The corresponding accusations remain claims by different participants, not established narratorial findings about their conduct.

Job 6:30
For there is no injustice on my tongue; does not my throat ponder understanding?
  Job 27:3-6 — Insisting on truthful speech and innocence: Job denies injustice on his tongue. In a later speech he again refuses lawless words or unjust thoughts and insists on retaining his innocence. The connection follows Job's continuing self-defense without making the friends' contrary allegations factual.

Job 7:1

  Sirach 40:1-2 — Human life as arduous labor: Job compares human life to a trial and a day worker’s existence. Sirach describes great occupation and a heavy yoke across human life from birth to return to the mother of all. These are related portrayals of life’s burden, without making every sufferer guilty.

Job 7:2

  Job 2:9d — Waiting for respite from hired labor: Job likens life to a servant obtaining shade or a hired worker waiting for wages. His wife’s earlier speech describes her own hired service and waiting for sunset to rest. The shared labor-and-waiting image retains her particular account and his comparison.

Job 7:4
If I lie down, I say, ‘When will it be day?’ And when I rise, again, ‘When will it be evening?’ I am filled with pains from evening until morning.
  Deuteronomy 28:65-67 — Morning and evening desired in distress: Job's pain makes him ask at night for day and on rising for evening. Deuteronomy's curse likewise alternates wishing for evening and for morning amid fear. The verbal pattern is compared without declaring Job a covenant-breaker subject to that curse.

Job 7:5

  Job 2:7-9c — The narrated ulcer and the sufferer's account: Job describes worms, earth and scraping discharge from his body. Job 7:7

Breath and spirit retain their supplied wording and the passages' distinct subjects.

Job 7:9
like a cloud cleared away from heaven. For if a person goes down to Hades, he will never come up again,
  Job 14:10-15 — Death's finality and a later hope of return: Job here says a person descending to Hades never comes up again. The later hope is retained rather than suppressing it to make both passages a single unqualified denial of return.
  Job 42:17-17a — The lament compared with the resurrection notice: Job's lament says the person descending to Hades never returns. The despairing speech and the closing assertion remain distinct voices; their tension is not erased.

Job 7:10

  Psalms 102:15-16 — Death and loss of one's place: Job says the dead person's place will no longer recognize him. The psalm's actual wording says the passing human will no longer recognize his place. Both concern mortality and the severed relation to one's place, while the reversed direction of recognition is preserved.

Job 7:11
So I too will not restrain my mouth; I will speak in my distress; held fast, I will disclose the bitterness of my soul.
  Job 10:1 — Speaking the soul's bitterness: Job declares that he will not restrain his mouth and will disclose his soul's bitterness. His later speech again lets words loose and speaks from the soul's bitterness. The repeated formula marks Job's continuing complaint.

Job 7:12

  Job 38:8-11 — The sea kept within bounds: Job asks whether he is a sea or dragon requiring a guard. In the later divine speech the Lord describes fencing the sea with gates, bars and limits. Job 7:14

Job 7:17

Job 7:18

Job 7:19
