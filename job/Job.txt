Job 1:1
A certain man lived in the land of Ausitis whose name was Job. That man was true, blameless, righteous, and God-fearing, keeping away from every evil deed.
  Genesis 6:9 — Character description parallel: Job and Noah are both introduced as righteous and blameless men before the accounts of calamity affecting their surroundings. The parallel concerns the narrators' character judgments, not an identification of the two men.
  Ezekiel 14:14 — Explicit later reference: Ezekiel names Job alongside Noah and Daniel as an example of righteousness, corresponding to the prologue's description of Job as righteous, blameless and God-fearing.
  Job 42:17b — Explicit narrative identification: The appendix again locates Job in Ausitis and adds its borders and the former name Jobab. This links the book's opening identification with its own closing identification without independently verifying the appendix's historical claims.

Job 1:2
He had seven sons and three daughters.
  Job 42:13 — Narrative restoration parallel: The seven sons and three daughters in the opening household correspond numerically to the seven sons and three daughters born to Job at the close. The later verse does not identify them as the children who died.

Job 1:3
His livestock numbered seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred pairs of oxen, and five hundred grazing female donkeys. He had a very large body of servants and great enterprises in the land. That man was of noble standing among those from the sunrise.
  Job 42:12 — Narrative restoration parallel: The closing inventory doubles each opening livestock total: sheep, camels, pairs of oxen and female donkeys. Both passages explicitly enumerate the same four categories of Job's wealth.

Job 1:4
His sons would gather with one another and hold a drinking feast each day, taking their three sisters along to eat and drink with them.
  Job 1:13 — Same-chapter literary recurrence: The recurring feasts involving Job's sons and daughters establish the family pattern repeated when the children gather to drink wine in their elder brother's house on the day of the losses.

Job 1:5
When the days of the drinking feast had ended, Job would send for them and purify them, rising in the morning and offering sacrifices for them according to their number, and one calf as a sin offering for their lives. For Job said, “Perhaps my sons have conceived evil thoughts toward God in their minds.” So Job did this continually.
  Job 42:8 — Intercession and sacrifice parallel: Job offers sacrifices for his children because they may have entertained evil thoughts toward God; later God directs his friends to obtain an offering and prayer through Job after their false speech. Both passages present Job interceding for others.

Job 1:6
When that day came, behold, the angels of God came to stand before the Lord, and the devil came with them.
  1 Kings 22:19-22 — Heavenly assembly comparison: Both scenes place heavenly beings before the Lord and include a spirit whose subsequent harmful activity occurs under an expressed commission or limit. The lying spirit in Micaiah's vision is not identified as Job's devil.
  Zechariah 3:1-2 — Accusation scene comparison: Job's devil appears in the assembly before the Lord, while Zechariah's slanderer stands beside Joshua to oppose him and receives the Lord's rebuke. Both scenes situate hostile spiritual opposition before divine authority.

Job 1:7
The Lord said to the devil, “Where have you come from?” The devil answered the Lord, “I have come after going around the earth and walking through the world beneath heaven.”
  1 Peter 5:8 — Adversary imagery parallel: The devil describes traveling around the earth in Job; Peter describes the devil walking about in search of someone to devour. The shared roaming image accompanies the adversary's threatening role.
  Job 2:2 — Repeated dialogue: The second heavenly scene repeats the Lord's question about the devil's origin and the devil's report of walking through the world, recalling this first exchange.

Job 1:8
The Lord said to him, “Have you paid attention in your mind to My servant Job? For among those on earth there is no man like him—blameless, true, God-fearing, keeping away from every evil deed.”
  Job 1:1 — Same-chapter narrative confirmation: The Lord's assessment of Job repeats the opening narrator's description of his innocence, truthfulness, fear of God and avoidance of evil. The coming losses are therefore not introduced as proof that Job was secretly wicked.
  Ezekiel 14:14 — Explicit later reference: The Lord calls attention to Job's outstanding righteousness here; Ezekiel subsequently names Job among three righteous men whose own righteousness would deliver them in the judgment described there.

Job 1:9
The devil answered before the Lord, “Does Job worship God for nothing?
  Job 2:4-5 — Repeated accusation: The first accusation asks whether Job's worship is disinterested; the second argues that preserving his life explains his endurance and proposes bodily harm. Both accusations challenge the motive behind his fidelity.
  Job 1:21-22 — Same-chapter accusation and outcome: The devil questions whether Job worships without reward, but after losing his possessions and children Job blesses the Lord and the narrator reports no sin. The ending of this trial answers the accusation through Job's response.

Job 1:10
Hast Thou not fenced in what is outside him and what is inside his house, and everything around all that belongs to him? Thou hast blessed the works of his hands and made his livestock numerous in the land.
  Job 29:2-6 — Retrospective description: The devil describes divine protection around Job's household and abundant possessions; Job later remembers God's guarding care, attention to his house, children around him and former abundance.

Job 1:11
But stretch out Thy hand and touch everything he has; surely he will curse Thee to Thy face.”
  Job 2:5 — Escalating test proposal: The first proposal predicts that taking Job's possessions will make him curse God; the second repeats that prediction while shifting the proposed blow to his bones and flesh.

Job 1:12
Then the Lord said to the devil, “Behold, everything he has I give into your hand, but do not touch him.” And the devil went out from the Lord.
  Job 2:6 — Contrasting limits on testing: The first permission excludes touching Job himself, whereas the second hands Job over but requires preservation of his life. The two scenes state different limits on the devil's activity.
  Luke 22:31-32 — Adversarial testing comparison: Job's trial proceeds after the devil's proposal and the Lord's limited permission. Jesus likewise speaks of Satan demanding to sift the disciples and of His prayer that Simon's faith not fail; the outcomes and limits are not identical.

Job 1:13
When that day came, Job’s sons and his daughters were drinking wine in the house of their elder brother.
  Job 1:4 — Same-chapter literary recurrence: The children drinking together in the elder brother's house repeats the family feasting pattern already described, identifying the ordinary gathering interrupted by the ensuing disaster.

Job 1:14
Behold, a messenger came to Job and said to him, “The pairs of oxen were plowing, and the female donkeys were grazing beside them.
  Job 1:3 — Same-chapter inventory recall: The first messenger names oxen and female donkeys, two of the livestock categories enumerated in Job's opening inventory. His report begins to connect the particular losses with that earlier account of wealth.

Job 1:15
The raiders came and carried them away, and killed the servants with swords. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  Job 1:17 — Same-chapter report parallel: Both reports describe attackers carrying off livestock, killing servants with swords and leaving one messenger to report. This edition calls the first attackers raiders and the later attackers horsemen, without identifying them as the same attacking group.

Job 1:16
While this one was still speaking, another messenger came and said to Job, “Fire fell from heaven and burned up the sheep and likewise consumed the shepherds. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  2 Kings 1:10-12 — Fire-from-heaven imagery: Job's messenger reports fire falling from heaven and consuming sheep and shepherds; the Elijah account describes fire from heaven consuming military companies. The similar description does not establish the same cause or moral status for the victims.

Job 1:17
While this one was still speaking, another messenger came and said to Job, “The horsemen formed three companies against us, surrounded the camels and carried them away, and killed the servants with swords. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  Job 1:15 — Same-chapter report parallel: The horsemen's attack repeats the earlier report's combination of stolen livestock, servants killed with swords and a sole surviving messenger, while involving camels rather than the earlier animals.

Job 1:18
While this one was still speaking, another messenger came, saying to Job, “While your sons and daughters were eating and drinking with their elder brother,
  Job 2:9b — Narrative recollection: The messenger recalls Job's sons and daughters eating together immediately before the collapse reported next; Job's wife later mourns those sons and daughters as the vanished memorial for whom she labored.

Job 1:19
a great wind suddenly came from the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house. The house fell on your children, and they died. I alone was saved and came to report to you.”
  Job 2:9b — Narrative recollection: The house's collapse kills Job's children; his wife's later lament specifically recalls the loss of the sons and daughters she bore. Her speech gives the mother's perspective on this reported catastrophe.
  Job 42:13 — Loss and later restoration: The deaths of Job's children precede the closing report that seven sons and three daughters were born to him. The connection follows the household's loss and later renewal without asserting that the later children are the same individuals.

Job 1:20
Then Job rose, tore his garments, and cut off the hair of his head. He fell to the ground, bowed in worship, and said:
  Job 2:12 — Mourning gesture parallel: Job tears his garments on receiving the reports, and his friends later tear their robes and weep when they see his condition. Both passages portray mourning through torn clothing.
  Ezra 9:3 — Mourning gesture comparison: Both Job and Ezra tear their garments and remove hair from their heads in distress. Job cuts his hair after bereavement, while Ezra plucks hair after hearing of covenant-breaking, so the shared gestures have different occasions.

Job 1:21
“I myself came out naked from my mother’s womb, and naked I shall depart there: the Lord gave, and the Lord took away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so it happened: blessed be the name of the Lord.”
  Ecclesiastes 5:14-15 — Close verbal and thematic parallel: Both passages describe coming naked from the mother's womb and departing as one came. Ecclesiastes emphasizes that no earnings can be carried away, corresponding to Job's acknowledgment that what was given can be taken.
  1 Timothy 6:7 — Possessions and mortality parallel: Job contrasts his naked arrival and departure with the Lord's giving and taking; First Timothy says that nothing was brought into the world and nothing can be carried out. Both detach possessions from the beginning and end of life.

Job 1:22
In all these things that happened to him, Job did not sin at all before the Lord or attribute foolishness to God.
  Job 2:10 — Repeated narrative judgment: After the first losses the narrator says Job did not sin or attribute foolishness to God; after bodily affliction the narrator again reports that he did not sin with his lips before God.
  James 5:11 — Explicit later reference: The narrator's judgment that Job remained without sin through these losses supplies an episode of the endurance James explicitly recalls when naming Job and the Lord's compassionate outcome.

Job 2:1
When that day came, the angels of God came to stand before the Lord, and the devil came among them to stand before the Lord.
  Job 1:6 — Repeated heavenly scene: The second scene repeats the angels' appearance before the Lord and the devil's presence among them, reopening the setting in which the first trial was authorized.

Job 2:2
The Lord said to the devil, “Where are you coming from?” Then the devil said before the Lord, “I have come after traveling through the world beneath heaven and walking through all of it.”
  Job 1:7 — Repeated dialogue: The Lord again asks where the devil has come from, and the devil again reports traveling through the world. The repeated exchange frames the second challenge as a continuation of the first.

Job 2:3
The Lord said to the devil, “Have you then paid attention to My servant Job? For among those on earth there is no man like him—innocent, true, blameless, God-fearing, keeping away from every evil. He still holds to innocence, but you said to destroy his belongings for nothing.”
  Job 1:8 — Repeated assessment with development: The Lord again describes Job's exceptional innocence and fear of God, now adding that he still holds his innocence after the destruction of his possessions. The earlier assessment is expressly maintained after the first test.

Job 2:4
The devil replied to the Lord, “Skin for skin! Whatever a person has, he will pay for his life.
  Matthew 16:26 — Life and possessions comparison: The devil claims that a person will pay whatever he possesses to preserve his life; Jesus asks what profit the whole world offers if life is forfeited and what can be given in exchange for life. The valuation is comparable without endorsing the devil's accusation.

Job 2:5
But indeed, stretch out Thy hand and touch his bones and his flesh; surely he will curse Thee to Thy face.”
  Job 1:11 — Escalating test proposal: The prediction that Job will curse God repeats the first proposal, but the object of the requested blow changes from possessions to bones and flesh. This identifies the second trial's escalation.

Job 2:6
The Lord said to the devil, “Behold, I hand him over to you; only preserve his life.”
  Job 1:12 — Contrasting limits on testing: The Lord now permits harm to Job but requires that his life be preserved. The earlier permission placed his possessions in the devil's hand while forbidding harm to Job himself.

Job 2:7
The devil went out from the Lord and struck Job with a grievous ulcer from his feet to his head.
  Deuteronomy 28:35 — Close bodily description with contextual difference: Both passages describe a grievous ulcer extending from the feet toward the head. Deuteronomy places it among covenant curses, whereas Job's prologue explicitly calls him innocent; the shared description does not prove Job's guilt.
  2 Corinthians 12:7 — Adversarial bodily affliction comparison: Job's ulcer is inflicted by the devil, while Paul describes a thorn in the flesh and a messenger of Satan striking him. Both connect affliction with hostile spiritual agency, but Paul's unspecified thorn is not identified as Job's ulcer.

Job 2:8
He took a potsherd to scrape off the discharge, and sat on the dung heap outside the city.
  Job 7:5 — Explicit bodily recollection: The narrator describes Job scraping discharge with a potsherd; Job later describes his decaying body and scraping off its discharge. The later complaint recalls the same distinctive bodily action.
  Lamentations 4:5 — Reversal of former status: Job, introduced as wealthy, now sits on a dung heap; Lamentations describes people formerly nursed upon scarlet wrapping themselves in dung heaps. The unusual setting marks a collapse from former prosperity in both scenes.

Job 2:9
After much time had passed, his wife said to him, “How long will you persist, saying,
  Job 2:3 — Same-chapter contrast over persistence: Job's wife questions how long he will persist, whereas the Lord has just commended him for still holding to innocence. Her challenge and the divine assessment take different views of his continuing endurance.

Job 2:9a
‘Behold, I will wait a little while longer, expecting the hope of my deliverance’?
  Job 13:16-18 — Hope of deliverance parallel: His wife's quotation attributes to Job an expectation of deliverance; Job later says that speaking before God will become deliverance and that he expects to be shown righteous. Both passages explicitly voice that expectation.

Job 2:9b
For behold, your memorial has vanished from the earth: sons and daughters, the pangs and pains of my womb, for whom I labored in vain with hardships.
  Job 1:19 — Explicit recollection of bereavement: The wife's lament names the sons and daughters she bore as lost; the earlier messenger reports the house collapsing on Job's children and killing them.

Job 2:9c
You yourself sit amid the decay of worms, spending the night in the open air,
  Job 7:3-5 — Bodily suffering recollection: The wife describes Job among worm-decay through the night; his later complaint joins nights of pain with a body fouled by worm-decay and discharge. The correspondence concerns these particular features of the suffering.

Job 2:9d
while I am a wanderer and a hired servant, going from place to place and from house to house, waiting for the sun to set so that I may rest from the hardships and pains that now hold me fast.
  Job 7:1-2 — Hired-worker imagery: The wife describes her own wandering hired service and waiting for sunset to rest; Job later compares human life to hired work and a servant obtaining shade. The later comparison is an image, not a claim that Job shares her employment.

Job 2:9e
But say some word to the Lord and die.”
  Job 2:10 — Same-chapter opposing counsel: The wife urges a word to the Lord and death; Job answers that good received from the Lord must be accompanied by bearing bad things. His response opposes her counsel, while this edition's phrase 'some word' is left unchanged.

Job 2:10
He looked at her and said, “You have spoken like one of the foolish women. If we received good things from the Lord’s hand, shall we not bear the bad things?” In all these things that happened to him, Job did not sin at all with his lips before God.
  Job 1:21-22 — Repeated response to adversity: Job's acceptance of bad as well as good from the Lord recalls his acknowledgment of the Lord's giving and taking. Both responses are followed by the narrator's statement that Job did not sin.

Job 2:11
His three friends heard of all the bad things that had come upon him and came to him, each from his own country: Eliphaz king of the Temanites, Bildad ruler of the Shuhites, and Zophar king of the Minaeans. They came to him together to comfort him and visit him.
  Job 42:7-9 — Narrative resolution: The three named friends arrive to comfort Job; the ending names the same men when God rebukes their speech and directs them to seek an offering and prayer through Job.
  Job 42:17e — Explicit identification of the friends: The appendix repeats the three friends' names and titles: Eliphaz king of the Temanites, Bildad ruler of the Shuhites and Zophar king of the Minaeans. It directly recalls the group introduced here.
  Job 16:2 — Narrative contrast: The friends initially come to comfort Job, but he later calls them all comforters of evils. The later rebuke contrasts the stated purpose of their visit with Job's experience of their speech.

Job 2:12
When they saw him from far away, they did not recognize him. Crying out loudly, they wept, each tearing his own robe and sprinkling earth upon himself.
  Job 1:20 — Mourning gesture parallel: The friends' torn robes and grief correspond to Job's earlier tearing of garments after the losses. The friends now participate in outward mourning for the suffering they see.

Job 2:13
They sat beside him seven days and seven nights, and none of them spoke, for they saw that his affliction was terrible and exceedingly great.
  Ezekiel 3:15-16 — Seven-day presence comparison: The friends sit beside the afflicted Job for seven days and nights before speaking; Ezekiel sits among the exiles for seven days before the next divine word. Both narratives mark an extended seven-day presence before subsequent speech, without equating the events.
  Job 13:5 — Silence and subsequent speech contrast: The friends initially refrain from speaking because of Job's great affliction. After their speeches, Job wishes that they would be silent and says that silence would count as wisdom for them.

Job 3:1
After this Job opened his mouth and cursed his day,
  Jeremiah 20:14 — Birth-day lament parallel: Job opens his lament by cursing his day; Jeremiah explicitly curses the day of his birth. Both passages introduce a sufferer's repudiation of the day on which his life began.

Job 3:3
“May the day perish on which I was born, and the night in which they said, ‘Behold, a male child!’
  Jeremiah 20:14-15 — Close birth-announcement parallel: Both laments curse the day of birth and recall the announcement of a male child. Job wishes the day and announcement-night to perish, while Jeremiah also curses the bearer of the birth announcement.

Job 3:4
May that day be darkness; may the Lord above not seek it out, and may no brightness come upon it.
  Amos 8:9-10 — Darkened-day imagery: Job wishes his birth-day deprived of brightness; Amos depicts daylight becoming dark as festivals turn to mourning. The darkness accompanies anguish in both passages, but Amos's announced judgment is not identified with Job's birth-day.

Job 3:5
May darkness and death’s shadow take possession of it; may gloom come upon it.
  Job 10:21-22 — Recurring darkness imagery: Job wishes darkness and death's shadow to claim his birth-day; later he describes the destination before him as a gloomy land with no brightness. The later lament develops the same association between darkness and death.

Job 3:6
May that day and night be cursed; may darkness carry it away. May it not belong among the days of the year or be numbered among the days of the months.
  Job 10:18-19 — Wish for erased existence: Removing his birth-day from the calendar expresses Job's repudiation of his beginning. Later he wishes he had died unseen and become as though he had never existed, making the underlying wish explicit.

Job 3:7
But may that night be pain, and may neither gladness nor joy come upon it.
  Jeremiah 20:15 — Birth-joy reversal: Job wishes the birth-night stripped of gladness and joy; Jeremiah curses the messenger who announced a boy's birth with rejoicing. Both laments reverse the joy normally attached to that announcement.

Job 3:9
May the stars of that night be darkened; may it wait and not come into light, and may it not see the morning star rising,
  Amos 5:18-20 — Expected light denied: Job imagines a night waiting for light but receiving none; Amos warns those desiring the Lord's day that it is darkness without brightness. Both overturn an expectation of light, in distinct lament and judgment settings.

Job 3:10
because it did not close the gates of my mother’s womb; for it would have removed pain from my eyes.
  Jeremiah 20:17-18 — Womb and suffering parallel: Job blames the night for failing to close the womb and spare him pain. Jeremiah likewise wishes he had died in the womb and asks why he emerged to experience labors and pains.

Job 3:11
For why did I not die in the womb, come out from the belly and immediately perish?
  Job 10:18-19 — Repeated question about birth: Job asks why he did not die as he emerged from the womb; later he repeats the question and imagines being taken directly from womb to tomb.

Job 3:12
Why did knees meet me? Why did I suck breasts?
  Psalms 21:10-11 — Contrasting recollections of infancy: Job questions why he was received and nursed; the psalmist remembers being drawn from the womb and finding hope from his mother's breasts. The common birth-and-nursing imagery serves lament in Job and a plea grounded in former care in the psalm.
  Isaiah 66:11-12 — Nursing and knees imagery: Job names breasts and welcoming knees while questioning his survival; Isaiah combines nursing at a comforting breast with children comforted on knees. The same nurturing images have contrasting emotional force.

Job 3:13
Now I would have lain down and been quiet; I would have slept and rested
  Job 14:12 — Death-as-sleep imagery: Job imagines that early death would have meant lying quietly, sleeping and resting. Later he describes the person who lies down in death and does not wake from sleep until heaven is no longer stitched together.

Job 3:14
with kings, counselors of the earth, who exulted in swords,
  Isaiah 14:9-11 — Kings among the dead: Job imagines resting with kings and counselors of the earth; Isaiah's taunt depicts former rulers and kings meeting the fallen Babylonian king in Hades. Royal standing does not keep either passage's rulers from the company of the dead.

Job 3:15
or with rulers who had much gold, who filled their houses with silver,
  Psalms 48:7-12 — Wealth and the grave: Job places rulers with houses full of silver among the dead. The psalm likewise describes those boasting in wealth, leaving it to strangers and having tombs as their enduring houses.

Job 3:16
or like a miscarriage coming out of a mother’s womb, or like infants who never saw light.
  Ecclesiastes 6:3-5 — Stillbirth and unseen light parallel: Job wishes for the condition of a miscarriage or infants who never saw light. Ecclesiastes similarly compares a troubled long life with a stillborn child who never saw the sun and is said to have more rest.

Job 3:17
There the ungodly kindled the fury of anger; there those worn out in body rested.
  Isaiah 14:3-4 — Rest from suffering comparison: Job's second clause imagines rest for bodies worn out by suffering. Isaiah announces rest from pain and harsh slavery before a taunt over the fallen oppressor; the comparison concerns relief from suffering, not Job's separate clause about the ungodly's anger.

Job 3:18
Together the age-old ones did not hear a tax collector’s voice.
  Job 39:7 — freedom from the tax collector's voice: Job's death-lament describes the age-old ones not hearing a tax collector's voice. The later wild-donkey portrait likewise describes not hearing a tax collector's reproach. This is the specific shared auditory image, with death and wilderness life remaining distinct settings.

Job 3:19
Small and great are there, and the servant does not fear his master.
  Job 21:23-26 — Death levels earthly distinctions: Job places small and great together where the servant no longer fears a master. Later he contrasts a prosperous death with a bitter one, then says both people sleep together in the earth and decay covers them.

Job 3:20
For why has light been given to those in bitterness, and life to souls in pain,
  Jeremiah 20:18 — Questioning life amid pain: Job asks why light and life are given to the bitter and suffering; Jeremiah asks why he left the womb to see labors and pains. Both laments question the purpose of continuing life under distress.

Job 3:21
who long for death and do not obtain it, digging for it as for treasures,
  Jonah 4:3 — Longing for death comparison: Job describes sufferers longing for death without obtaining it; Jonah asks the Lord to take his life because he regards death as better than living. The shared longing arises in different narrative circumstances and is not a command to readers.

Job 3:22
and become overjoyed if they obtain it?
  Ecclesiastes 4:2 — Preference for death in lament: Job pictures rejoicing if death is obtained, while Ecclesiastes, after describing extortion, praises the dead above those still living. Both passages express a sufferer's or observer's preference for death over distressing life.

Job 3:23
Death is rest for a man, for God has shut him in.
  Lamentations 3:7-9 — Divine confinement imagery: Job describes God shutting a man in; the speaker in Lamentations describes God building against him, blocking his exit and obstructing his paths. Both use confinement to describe their experience of affliction.
  Job 1:10 — Protective enclosure reversed: The devil earlier described God's fencing around Job as protection for household and possessions. Job now experiences God's enclosing action as confinement, reversing the earlier account's favorable meaning.

Job 3:24
For sighing comes to me before my food, and I weep, gripped by fear.
  Psalms 41:4 — Tears and food imagery: Job describes sighing before his food and weeping under fear; the psalmist says tears became his bread day and night. Both connect sustained grief with the experience of eating.

Job 3:25
For the fear I thought about came upon me, and what I had feared met me.
  Job 30:26 — Contrasting expectations of calamity: Job says that feared trouble met him; later he recalls waiting for good things but meeting days of evils instead. Both recollections describe the arrival of calamity in relation to his earlier expectations.

Job 3:26
I had no peace, no quiet, no rest; yet wrath came upon me.”
  Job 7:3-4 — Restlessness in affliction: Job says he has neither peace, quiet nor rest. His later complaint describes empty months and nights of pain, continually waiting for day or evening without relief.

Job 4:2
“Have you not often been spoken to amid trouble? Who will endure the force of your words?
  Job 6:26 — Dispute about enduring the other's words: Eliphaz asks who can endure the force of Job's words; Job replies that he will not endure even the sound of a word from his reprovers. The repeated challenge belongs to their disagreement, not a narrator's judgment that either speech is worthless.

Job 4:3
For if you admonished many and encouraged a weak person’s hands,
  Isaiah 35:3-4 — Encouraging weak hands: Eliphaz depicts Job as having encouraged weak hands; Isaiah calls limp hands and paralyzed knees to strength and urges encouragement of the fainthearted. The shared bodily language describes encouragement in distinct settings.

Job 4:4
and raised up the weak with words and put courage into powerless knees,
  Hebrews 12:12-13 — Strengthening powerless knees: Eliphaz credits Job's words with raising the weak and strengthening powerless knees. Hebrews likewise urges the strengthening of weakened knees, with healing for the lame; it does not thereby endorse Eliphaz's subsequent accusation against Job.

Job 4:5
yet now pain has come upon you and touched you, and you have become agitated.
  Job 2:7-8 — The counselor's own affliction: Eliphaz contrasts Job's earlier help to others with his agitation when pain touches him. The prologue supplies the particular affliction: a grievous ulcer and scraping its discharge. Eliphaz's appraisal of Job's response remains his own.

Job 4:7
Remember, then: who perished while pure, or when were true people utterly uprooted?
  Job 2:3 — The innocence assumed by the prologue: Eliphaz's question about whether pure people perish is read against God's prior declaration that Job still holds to innocence despite the destruction of his belongings for nothing. The prologue prevents treating the question as proof that Job must be guilty.

Job 4:8
As I have seen those who plow improper things, those who sow them will reap pains for themselves.
  Proverbs 22:8 — Sowing wrongdoing and reaping harm: Eliphaz describes those sowing improper things as reaping pains; Proverbs likewise says one who sows worthless things reaps evils. The shared agricultural maxim does not establish that Job committed the wrongdoing Eliphaz implies.

Job 4:12
But if some true word had been among your words, none of these evils would have met you. Will not my ear receive extraordinary things from Him?
  Job 42:7-8 — Truthful speech and the closing rebuke: Eliphaz says true words would have prevented Job's evils. The closing divine judgment instead rebukes Eliphaz and his friends for failing to speak truth before God and against Job. That verdict checks the accusation rather than establishing Job's guilt.

Job 4:13
There were terrors and a nocturnal sound, fear falling upon people;
  Job 7:13-14 — Terrifying experiences at night: Eliphaz introduces his reported experience with nocturnal terror; Job later says hoped-for comfort on his bed is overcome by frightening dreams and visions. These are different speakers' experiences, not one identified vision or visitor.

Job 4:17
‘What then? Can a mortal be pure before the Lord, or a man blameless on account of his works?
  Psalms 142:2 — Mortal righteousness before God: The voice reported by Eliphaz questions whether a mortal can be pure before the Lord; the psalmist asks not to enter judgment because no living person will be justified before God. The parallel does not authenticate Eliphaz's unidentified voice or diagnose Job's suffering.

Job 4:19
as for those who inhabit houses of clay, from which we ourselves also are, of the same clay, He struck them as one does a moth.
  Genesis 2:7 — Human bodies described through earth: Eliphaz's reported voice calls humans inhabitants of clay houses, themselves of clay. Genesis describes God forming the human from earth's dust. The comparison concerns earthen human formation, not a literal clay building or the voice's further moth imagery.

Job 4:20
From morning until evening they are no more; because they cannot help themselves, they perished.
  Psalms 89:5-6 — Mortality between morning and evening: The reported voice describes people disappearing between morning and evening. The psalm's grass image likewise moves from morning growth to evening drying; it supplies comparable imagery of brevity without repeating the voice's explanation of helplessness.

Job 4:21
For He blew upon them, and they withered; they perished because they had no wisdom.’
  Job 15:30 — Eliphaz's repeated withering image: Eliphaz's reported voice depicts people blown upon and withering. His later speech portrays a wind withering the wicked person's shoot. Both passages belong to Eliphaz's presentation, and the later plant image does not repeat the earlier claim about lacking wisdom.

Job 5:2
For anger kills a fool, and jealousy puts one who has gone astray to death.
  Sirach 30:24 — Anger and jealousy shorten life: Eliphaz says anger and jealousy bring death; Sirach explicitly says jealousy and anger shorten days. The shared warning about those passions does not establish that Job's calamity resulted from them.

Job 5:3
I have seen fools taking root, but immediately their way of life was devoured.
  Psalms 36:35-36 — Rooted prosperity abruptly lost: Eliphaz describes a fool taking root before his way of life is devoured. The psalmist sees an ungodly person elevated like Lebanon's cedars and then gone. The flourishing-then-disappearance image is shared, without identifying Job as either depicted offender.

Job 5:7
but a human being is born to toil, and a vulture’s young fly to the heights.
  Genesis 3:17-19 — Human life marked by toil: Eliphaz says a human is born to toil. Genesis assigns Adam lifelong painful labor and bread obtained by sweat. The comparison concerns human toil; Job's vulture image is not changed into sparks, and Adam's particular offense is not assigned to Job.

Job 5:9
who does great and unsearchable things, glorious and extraordinary things beyond numbering,
  Job 9:10 — Job repeats the description of divine wonders: Job later repeats Eliphaz's description of God doing great, unsearchable and innumerable extraordinary things. The shared wording does not make their arguments identical: Job's surrounding reply wrestles with judgment and the limits of answering God.

Job 5:10
who gives rain upon the earth and sends water upon the world beneath heaven,
  Matthew 5:45 — Rain given across the earth: Eliphaz includes rain upon the earth among God's works. Jesus describes the Father's rain falling on righteous and unrighteous alike. Their accounts share the divine provision of rain, while Jesus explicitly names a moral breadth that cannot be used to infer a sufferer's guilt.

Job 5:11
who raises the lowly to a height and rouses those who have perished,
  1 Samuel 2:6-8 — Raising the lowly and the dead: Eliphaz describes God raising the lowly and rousing those who have perished. Hannah's song pairs bringing up from Hades with lifting the needy from earth to seats of honor. The corresponding reversals do not validate Eliphaz's application to Job.

Job 5:12
who changes the counsels of the unscrupulous, and their hands will certainly do nothing true.
  Psalms 32:10-11 — Human counsels made ineffective: Eliphaz says God changes unscrupulous counsels and frustrates their hands. The psalm describes the Lord nullifying the counsels of nations and rulers while His own counsel remains. The actors and wording differ, and neither identifies Job as one of those plotters.

Job 5:13
He catches the wise in their understanding and throws the counsel of the devious into confusion.
  1 Corinthians 3:18-20 — The saying about catching the wise cited: Paul cites the written saying that God catches the wise in their craftiness when warning against worldly wisdom. Eliphaz's saying uses understanding and adds the confusion of devious counsel. Paul's use of this saying does not endorse Eliphaz's entire argument against Job.

Job 5:14
By day darkness will meet them; may they grope at noon as in the night.
  Deuteronomy 28:28-29 — Groping at noon as in darkness: Eliphaz portrays the devious meeting darkness by day and groping at noon as at night. Deuteronomy's curse likewise describes groping at noon as the blind grope in darkness. The shared image does not establish that Job is under that covenant curse.

Job 5:15
May they perish in war, but may the powerless escape from a ruler’s hand.
  Psalms 34:10 — The powerless rescued from stronger hands: Eliphaz wishes that the powerless escape a ruler's hand. The psalm praises the Lord for rescuing the poor from those stronger than they are and from plunderers. This matches the rescue image, not Eliphaz's accompanying wish about death in war.

Job 5:16
May the powerless have hope, and may an unjust person’s mouth be stopped.
  Psalms 106:41-42 — The poor helped and lawlessness silenced: Eliphaz joins hope for the powerless with an unjust mouth being stopped. The psalm joins help for the poor with the upright rejoicing and lawlessness stopping its mouth. The paired reversal is shared without identifying the speakers' occasions.

Job 5:17
Happy is the person whom the Lord reproved; do not refuse the Almighty’s admonition.
  Proverbs 3:11-12 — Receiving divine correction: Eliphaz calls the person reproved by the Lord happy and warns against refusing admonition. Proverbs similarly warns against making light of the Lord's discipline and connects correction with love. The counsel's parallel does not prove that Job's affliction is punishment for wrongdoing.

Job 5:18
For He causes pain and restores again; He struck, and His hands healed.
  Deuteronomy 32:39 — The one who wounds also heals: Eliphaz says God causes pain and restores, strikes and heals. The divine declaration in Moses' song likewise pairs smiting with healing. The shared pair of actions does not resolve why Job suffers or authenticate Eliphaz's diagnosis.

Job 5:20
In famine He will rescue you from death, and in war He will release you from the hand of iron.
  Psalms 32:18-19 — Rescue from famine and death: Eliphaz promises rescue from death in famine. The psalm describes the Lord watching those who fear Him to rescue their souls from death and nourish them in famine. Its promise does not repeat Eliphaz's further assurance about warfare.

Job 5:21
He will hide you from the lash of the tongue, and you will not fear coming evils.
  Psalms 30:20-21 — Shelter from hostile tongues: Eliphaz promises concealment from the tongue's lash. The psalm describes shelter in God's presence from human turmoil and in a tent from the contradiction of tongues. These are corresponding images of protection from harmful speech.

Job 5:22
You will laugh at unjust and lawless people; you will not fear wild beasts,
  Leviticus 26:3-6 — Safety from frightening beasts: Eliphaz promises that Job will not fear wild beasts. Leviticus's covenant blessing promises peace, rest without fear and removal of harmful animals. The parallel does not make Eliphaz's personal assurance the covenant's own verdict about Job.

Job 5:23
for wild beasts will be at peace with you.
  Hosea 2:20 — Peace involving wild animals: Eliphaz promises that wild beasts will be at peace with Job. Hosea's restoration promise establishes a covenant with field beasts, birds and creeping things and ends warfare. The scope and recipients differ; Hosea is not a record of Eliphaz's promise being enacted.

Job 5:25
You will know that your offspring are many, and your children will be like all the vegetation of the field.
  Job 42:13-16 — Promised offspring and the narrated ending: Eliphaz promises Job many offspring. The ending records seven sons, three daughters and later generations seen by Job. The narrative outcome can be compared with the assurance without overturning the closing rebuke of Eliphaz's speech.

Job 5:26
You will come to the grave like ripe grain harvested at the proper time, or like a heap on the threshing floor gathered in its season.
  Job 42:16-17 — A full life before death: Eliphaz imagines Job reaching the grave like grain harvested in season. The ending records his extended life and death elderly and full of days. It supplies a comparable outcome, without using the grain image or validating Eliphaz's account of the suffering's cause.

Job 5:27
Behold, we have searched these things out in this way; these are the things we have heard. But know for yourself whether you have done anything.”
  Job 42:7-8 — Claimed investigation tested by the closing verdict: Eliphaz concludes that he and his companions have searched out what they say. The Lord's closing rebuke of Eliphaz and his two friends prevents treating that claim of investigation as a guarantee that their accusations against Job are true.

Job 6:2
“If only someone would weigh my anger and lift my pains together in a balance!
  Job 31:5-6 — A requested weighing: Job wishes his anger and pains could be weighed together. Later he asks to be set in a righteous balance while declaring that the Lord knows his innocence. The same weighing image serves distinct purposes: measuring distress and testing conduct.

Job 6:3
It would indeed be heavier than the sand beside the sea; but, it seems, my words are worthless.
  Proverbs 27:3 — Anger compared with sand's weight: Job says his anger and pains would outweigh the sand beside the sea. Proverbs compares a fool's anger with heavy stone and sand. The weight comparison is shared, but Job is not thereby classified as the proverb's fool.

Job 6:4
For the Lord’s arrows are in my body; their fury drinks up my blood. When I begin to speak, they stab me.
  Psalms 37:2-4 — Divine arrows and bodily suffering: Job describes the Lord's arrows lodged in his body and their fury. The psalmist likewise describes God's arrows lodged in him and bodily distress under divine anger. The psalmist's explicit statement about his sins is not imported as Job's confession.

Job 6:5
What then? Does a wild donkey cry out for nothing, rather than seeking food? Would an ox raise its voice at a manger when it has food?
  Amos 3:4-5 — Animal behavior used in questions about causes: Job asks whether a donkey cries without seeking food or an ox cries when fed. Amos asks whether a lion calls without prey or a trap springs without a catch. Both arguments use questions about animal behavior and its occasion; hunger and possession of prey remain different situations.

Job 6:6
Will bread be eaten without salt? Is there any taste in empty words?
  Colossians 4:6 — Speech compared with seasoned food: Job places unsalted bread beside the lack of taste in empty words. Colossians directs that speech be gracious and seasoned with salt. The shared food-and-speech image serves a complaint in Job and positive instruction in the letter.

Job 6:9
Let the Lord begin and wound me, but let Him not destroy me completely.
  Job 2:6 — A limit short of complete destruction: Job asks the Lord to wound him but not destroy him completely. The prologue permits the devil to afflict him while preserving his life. The comparison preserves both the limit and the different agents; this version is not rewritten as an unqualified request to be killed.

Job 6:10
May my city be a grave, on whose walls I used to leap; I will certainly not hold back. For I have not falsified the holy words of my God.
  Job 4:12 — Reply about truthful speech: Job says he has not falsified God's holy words. Eliphaz had claimed that true words would have prevented these evils. Job's assertion responds to the truthfulness at issue in that accusation, without requiring an explanation of the separate grave-and-city image.

Job 6:12
Is my strength the strength of stones? Is my flesh made of bronze?
  Job 40:15-18 — Human frailty and metallic bodily strength: Job asks whether his flesh is bronze. In the later divine speech the described grass-eating creature has bronze ribs and a cast-iron spine. The contrast concerns imagined bodily strength; it does not identify Job with that creature or supply a species name absent from the passage.

Job 6:14
Mercy has disowned me, and the Lord’s care has overlooked me.
  Isaiah 49:14-15 — The complaint of being forgotten: Job says mercy has disowned him and the Lord's care overlooked him. Zion similarly complains that the Lord abandoned and forgot her, before receiving an answer of enduring care. The shared complaint is attributed to its speakers; Zion's answer is not presented as a direct speech to Job.

Job 6:15
Those nearest me paid no attention to me; like a failing winter torrent or a wave they passed me by.
  Jeremiah 15:18 — Unreliable water as an image in complaint: Job compares neglectful companions to a failing winter torrent. Jeremiah's complaint describes false water having no faithfulness. The water image expresses disappointed reliance in both; the target's wording is not rewritten to name Job's companions or explicitly call God a dried-up stream.

Job 6:18
so I too was abandoned by everyone; I perished and became homeless.
  Job 42:11 — Abandonment and the later return of acquaintances: Job laments being abandoned by everyone and becoming homeless. The ending records brothers, sisters and former acquaintances coming to eat, drink and console him. That later return contrasts with his reported isolation, without equating every visitor with the three debating friends.

Job 6:20
Those who trust in cities and possessions will incur shame.
  Proverbs 11:28 — Reliance on possessions fails: Job warns that those trusting cities and possessions incur shame. Proverbs says the one trusting wealth will fall. The connection concerns the expressly named reliance on possessions, not a claim that the passages describe the same people or a particular ruined city.

Job 6:21
But you too have come upon me without mercy, so that on seeing my wound you became afraid.
  Job 2:11-13 — Comfort intended and mercy disputed: Job charges the visitors with coming without mercy after seeing his wound. The prologue says the friends came to comfort him and sat silent before his great affliction. His later accusation is contrasted with that stated purpose, not substituted for the narrator's account.

Job 6:24
Teach me, and I will be silent; if I have gone astray in anything, tell me.
  Job 13:20-23 — Asking for specified wrongdoing: Job challenges his friends to teach him where he has gone astray. Later he asks God to teach him what his sins and lawless deeds are. The repeated demand seeks concrete identification of wrongdoing; the addressees differ.

Job 6:26
Your reproof will not stop me with words, for I will not endure even the sound of a word from you.
  Job 4:2 — Refusing to endure the other's words: Job says he will not endure even the sound of the friends' reproof. Eliphaz had asked who could endure the force of Job's words. This is a particular verbal reversal within the dispute, not a judgment that the entire exchange lacks value.

Job 6:27
Except that you fall upon an orphan and leap upon your friend.
  Job 22:9 — Accusations involving orphans: Job accuses his friends of falling upon an orphan and leaping upon a friend. Eliphaz later accuses Job of mistreating orphans and sending widows away empty. The corresponding accusations remain claims by different participants, not established narratorial findings about their conduct.

Job 6:30
For there is no injustice on my tongue; does not my throat ponder understanding?
  Job 27:3-6 — Insisting on truthful speech and innocence: Job denies injustice on his tongue. In a later speech he again refuses lawless words or unjust thoughts and insists on retaining his innocence. The connection follows Job's continuing self-defense without making the friends' contrary allegations factual.

Job 7:1
“Is not a human being’s life on earth a trial, and his existence like that of a worker hired for the day?
  Sirach 40:1-2 — Human life as arduous labor: Job compares human life to a trial and a day worker’s existence. Sirach describes great occupation and a heavy yoke across human life from birth to return to the mother of all. These are related portrayals of life’s burden, without making every sufferer guilty.

Job 7:2
Or like a servant fearing his master and obtaining shade, or like a hired worker waiting for his wages,
  Job 2:9d — Waiting for respite from hired labor: Job likens life to a servant obtaining shade or a hired worker waiting for wages. His wife’s earlier speech describes her own hired service and waiting for sunset to rest. The shared labor-and-waiting image retains her particular account and his comparison.

Job 7:4
If I lie down, I say, ‘When will it be day?’ And when I rise, again, ‘When will it be evening?’ I am filled with pains from evening until morning.
  Deuteronomy 28:65-67 — Morning and evening desired in distress: Job's pain makes him ask at night for day and on rising for evening. Deuteronomy's curse likewise alternates wishing for evening and for morning amid fear. The verbal pattern is compared without declaring Job a covenant-breaker subject to that curse.

Job 7:5
My body is fouled with the decay of worms; I dissolve clods of earth as I scrape off the discharge.
  Job 2:7-9c — The narrated ulcer and the sufferer's account: Job describes worms, earth and scraping discharge from his body. The prologue records the ulcer, his scraping with a potsherd, and his wife's description of decay and worms. These concern his narrated physical affliction; no moral cause is added.

Job 7:7
Remember, then, that my life is a breath, and my eye will no longer return to see good.
  Psalms 77:38-39 — Remembering mortal life that does not return: Job asks to be remembered because his life is a breath and his eye will not return to see good. The psalm says God remembered that people are flesh, a spirit going away without returning, in its account of compassion. Breath and spirit retain their supplied wording and the passages' distinct subjects.

Job 7:9
like a cloud cleared away from heaven. For if a person goes down to Hades, he will never come up again,
  Job 14:10-15 — Death's finality and a later hope of return: Job here says a person descending to Hades never comes up again. His later speech first describes death and not rising, then wishes for protection in Hades and says he will wait until he comes to be again. The later hope is retained rather than suppressing it to make both passages a single unqualified denial of return.
  Job 42:17-17a — The lament compared with the resurrection notice: Job's lament says the person descending to Hades never returns. The book's closing notice, after recording Job's death, says it is written that he will rise with those the Lord raises. The despairing speech and the closing assertion remain distinct voices; their tension is not erased.

Job 7:10
nor will he ever return again to his own house, nor will his place recognize him any longer.
  Psalms 102:15-16 — Death and loss of one's place: Job says the dead person's place will no longer recognize him. The psalm's actual wording says the passing human will no longer recognize his place. Both concern mortality and the severed relation to one's place, while the reversed direction of recognition is preserved.

Job 7:11
So I too will not restrain my mouth; I will speak in my distress; held fast, I will disclose the bitterness of my soul.
  Job 10:1 — Speaking the soul's bitterness: Job declares that he will not restrain his mouth and will disclose his soul's bitterness. His later speech again lets words loose and speaks from the soul's bitterness. The repeated formula marks Job's continuing complaint.

Job 7:12
Am I a sea or a dragon, that Thou didst post a guard over me?
  Job 38:8-11 — The sea kept within bounds: Job asks whether he is a sea or dragon requiring a guard. In the later divine speech the Lord describes fencing the sea with gates, bars and limits. This gives a particular counterpart to the sea image without identifying Job or his dragon comparison with a named creature elsewhere.

Job 7:14
Thou frightenest me with dreams and overwhelmest me with visions.
  Job 4:13-16 — Frightening night experiences compared: Job says dreams and visions frighten him. Eliphaz had described nocturnal terror, trembling and an unidentified experience of spirit, breeze and voice. The shared experience of fear does not identify their visions or certify the source of Eliphaz's report.

Job 7:16
For I will not live for ever, so as to keep enduring; leave me alone, for my life is empty.
  Psalms 38:13-14 — A plea for relief before departure: Job asks to be left alone because he cannot live and endure for ever. The psalmist asks for relief and refreshment before departing and no longer existing. Both are pleas for respite within a limited life, not detached teachings about every person's final fate.

Job 7:17
For what is a human being, that Thou didst magnify him, or that Thou givest Thy mind to him,
  Psalms 8:4-7 — The question about divine attention to humanity: Job asks what a human is that God magnifies him and fixes His mind upon him. The psalm asks what a human is that God remembers and visits him, then celebrates honor and dominion. Job's surrounding complaint turns the shared question toward burdensome scrutiny rather than the psalm's celebration.

Job 7:18
or that Thou visitest him until morning and judgest him for rest?
  Psalms 8:5 — Visitation experienced as scrutiny: Continuing his question about a human's significance, Job asks why God visits him and judges him. The psalm's question likewise includes God's visitation of humanity, but its surrounding praise gives that attention a different tone from Job's painful complaint.

Job 7:19
How long wilt Thou not leave me alone, nor let me go until I swallow my saliva in pain?
  Job 10:20-21 — Asking for a little respite: Job asks how long God will refuse to leave him alone even long enough to swallow. Later he asks to rest a little before going to the dark land from which he will not return. These are Job's repeated pleas for brief relief; the swallowing detail remains particular to this passage.

Job 7:20
If I sinned, what can I do to Thee, Thou who understandest the human mind? Why hast Thou made me Thine accuser, and why am I a burden to Thee?
  Job 35:3-8 — What human sin can do to God: Job conditionally asks what he can do to God if he has sinned. Elihu later raises the question of what sin or righteousness can accomplish for God and says their effects concern a fellow human. Elihu's reply is attributed to him, not treated as God's own verdict on Job.

Job 8:2
“How long will you speak these things? The breath of your mouth is full of words.
  Job 18:1-3 — Bildad again demands an end to Job's speech: Bildad asks how long Job will keep speaking; at his next reply he again asks how long before Job stops so the others may speak. The repeated opening shows Bildad's stance in the dispute without replacing the actual breath-and-words wording with a familiar wind comparison.

Job 8:3
Will the Lord do wrong in judging, or will the Maker of all disturb what is right?
  Deuteronomy 32:4 — The assertion of divine justice: Bildad asks whether the Lord can do wrong in judging. Moses' song likewise calls God faithful, righteous and without unrighteousness. The shared assertion does not establish Bildad's subsequent inference about Job or his children's guilt.

Job 8:4
If your sons sinned before Him, He sent them into the power of their lawlessness.
  Job 1:18-19 — An inference about the children's catastrophe: Bildad conditionally says that if Job's sons sinned, God handed them to their lawlessness. The prologue reports the children's deaths in a collapsing house but does not pronounce them guilty. The connection exposes Bildad's inference as a speaker's claim, not the narrator's explanation.

Job 8:5
But rise early to the Lord Almighty in supplication.
  Job 42:8-9 — The adviser later needs Job's prayer: Bildad urges Job to petition the Almighty. At the ending Bildad and the other friends must approach Job, whose offering and prayer God accepts for them. The adviser later depends on the prayer of the man he instructed; this does not make the act of praying wrong.

Job 8:6
If you are pure and true, He will hear your supplication and restore to you a righteous way of life.
  Job 2:3 — Conditional purity beside the prologue's verdict: Bildad makes restoration conditional on Job being pure and true. Before the dialogue, God had already called Job innocent, true and blameless and said he still held to innocence. That framing prevents treating Bildad's condition as evidence that Job was known to be impure.

Job 8:7
Then your first things will be few, but your last things beyond telling.
  Job 42:12 — Former and latter circumstances compared: Bildad predicts that Job's first things will be few beside his latter things. The ending explicitly says the Lord blessed Job's latter things more than the former. The corresponding reversal does not cancel the ending's rebuke of the friends' accusations.

Job 8:8
For inquire of the former generation; search out the family of the fathers.
  Deuteronomy 32:7 — Consulting earlier generations: Bildad directs inquiry into the former generation and the fathers' family. Moses' song likewise tells its hearers to remember earlier generations and ask fathers and elders who will tell them. The method of appeal is shared; Bildad's conclusions are not thereby certified.

Job 8:9
For we are of yesterday and do not know; our life on earth is a shadow.
  1 Chronicles 29:14-15 — Human days upon earth as a shadow: Bildad invokes the brevity of human life as a shadow to explain limited knowledge. David's prayer likewise calls human days on earth a shadow without endurance. The matching image occurs in distinct arguments about inherited knowledge and dependence on God.

Job 8:11
Does papyrus flourish without water, or will a rush grow tall without a drink?
  Jeremiah 17:7-8 — Plants dependent on water in a moral comparison: Bildad asks whether papyrus or a rush grows without water, before applying withering to those forgetting the Lord. Jeremiah compares the one trusting the Lord to a tree sending roots toward moisture. The images use water-dependent plants in contrasting moral illustrations, not identical species or an established diagnosis of Job.

Job 8:13
So, then, will be the latter end of all who forget the Lord; for the hope of an ungodly person will perish.
  Proverbs 10:28 — The hope of the ungodly perishes: Bildad says the ungodly person's hope perishes. Proverbs states that the hope of the impious perishes while gladness remains with the righteous. The close maxim does not establish that Job belongs to the condemned class.

Job 8:14
For his house will be uninhabited, and his tent will turn into a spider’s web.
  Job 27:18 — A fragile house and the spider image: Bildad describes the ungodly person's tent becoming a spider's web. In a later speech Job describes an ungodly person's house as like moths and a spider. The shared house-and-spider imagery is preserved without supplying a web where the later wording does not explicitly name one.

Job 8:15
If he props up his house, it will certainly not stand; when he takes hold of it, it will certainly not endure.
  Matthew 7:26-27 — An unreliable house that will not stand: Bildad portrays the ungodly person's house as unable to stand despite being propped up. Jesus's parable describes a foolish hearer's house on sand falling under rain, rivers and wind. Both use a failed house as an image of unreliable security, with different grounds and circumstances.

Job 8:18
If it swallows him up, the place will disown him. Have you not seen such things?
  Psalms 102:15-16 — Losing recognition of one's place: Bildad depicts the swallowed-up person's place disowning him, within his description of the ungodly. The psalm describes mortal humanity generally and says the person no longer recognizes his place. The shared loss of relation to place preserves both the reversed grammatical direction and the different moral scope.

Job 8:20
For the Lord will certainly not reject the innocent, but He will accept no gift of an ungodly person.
  Proverbs 15:8 — The innocent and offerings of the impious: Bildad says God will not reject the innocent or accept an ungodly person's gift. Proverbs contrasts the abomination of impious sacrifices with acceptable prayers from those going straight. The moral distinction does not identify Job as an impious giver.

Job 8:21
He will fill the mouth of true people with laughter, and their lips with thanksgiving.
  Psalms 125:1-2 — The mouth filled with gladness: Bildad promises true people mouths full of laughter and lips of thanksgiving. The psalm of Zion's restored captivity describes mouths filled with joy and tongues with exultation. The paired speech-and-gladness image is shared, with the supplied terms and different recipients retained.

Job 8:22
Their enemies will be clothed with shame, and an ungodly person’s dwelling will be no more.”
  Psalms 131:17-18 — Enemies clothed with shame: Bildad says the true people’s enemies will be clothed with shame. The psalm’s royal promise concerning David and the Lord’s anointed likewise clothes the enemies with shame. The clothing image is particular, while the beneficiaries and the psalm’s royal setting remain distinct.

Job 9:2
“Truly I know that it is so; for how can a mortal be righteous before the Lord?
  Job 4:17 — Dialogue echo: mortal righteousness: Job asks how a mortal can be righteous before the Lord, taking up the question of mortal purity and blamelessness in Eliphaz's reported speech. The repeated question does not endorse every conclusion Eliphaz draws about Job.
  Psalms 142:2 — Prayer and judgment parallel: Job's question about a mortal being righteous before the Lord parallels the psalm's plea not to enter judgment because no living person will be justified before Him. Both frame the difficulty as standing in divine judgment.

Job 9:3
For if he wishes to enter judgment with Him, He certainly will not listen to him, lest he answer back to one of His words out of a thousand.
  Job 40:4-5 — Contrast in the possibility of an answer: Job anticipates a divine refusal to listen that prevents an answer to even one word out of a thousand. After actually hearing the Lord, he later asks what answer he can give, covers his mouth and says he will add nothing. The comparison distinguishes the earlier denied hearing from his later inability to answer.

Job 9:5
He wears the mountains out, and they do not know; He overturns them in anger.
  Job 14:18 — Recurring image: mountains wearing away: Job describes God wearing out and overturning mountains; he later compares human endurance with a falling mountain and a rock worn from its place. The repeated landscape imagery concerns vulnerability, without identifying one geological event.

Job 9:6
He shakes the world beneath heaven from its foundations, and its pillars sway.
  Psalms 74:4 — Cosmic pillars: contrasting actions: Job describes the world's foundations shaking and its pillars swaying; the psalm describes the earth melting and its pillars being established. Both express control over the world's stability through the particular image of pillars.

Job 9:7
He speaks to the sun and it does not rise, and He seals up the stars.
  Isaiah 13:10 — Cosmic judgment imagery: Job attributes the sun's failure to rise and the sealing of stars to divine command. Isaiah's account of the Lord's day likewise describes stars withholding light and the rising sun darkening; the passages do not identify the same occasion.

Job 9:8
He alone stretched out heaven and walks on the sea as on ground.
  Isaiah 40:22 — Creation imagery: stretched heaven: Job says God alone stretched out heaven; Isaiah describes Him stretching it like a dwelling tent. Both attribute heaven's extension to God, with Isaiah supplying the tent comparison.
  Matthew 14:25-27 — Narrative comparison: walking on the sea: Job describes God walking on the sea as on ground. Matthew depicts Jesus approaching the disciples by walking on the sea and identifying Himself to their frightened group. The comparison concerns this distinctive action, without turning Job's line into a narrated forecast of that voyage.

Job 9:9
He makes the Pleiades, the Evening Star, and Arcturus, and the storehouses of the south.
  Job 38:31-33 — Divine speech revisits the constellations: Job names the Pleiades, Evening Star and Arcturus among God's works. The Lord later asks him about the Pleiades, Orion, mazourōth and the Evening Star. The named heavenly bodies overlap, but the two lists are not made identical.

Job 9:10
He does great and unsearchable things, glorious and extraordinary things beyond numbering.
  Job 5:9 — Repeated wording across disputants: Job repeats Eliphaz's description of great, unsearchable, glorious and extraordinary works beyond numbering. Their shared praise of divine power therefore coexists with their disagreement about Job's suffering.

Job 9:11
If He passes over me, I will not see; if He passes by me, even then I do not perceive Him.
  Job 23:9 — Recurring complaint: unseen divine action: Job cannot perceive God passing him; later he says he did not grasp God's action on the left and will not see Him at the right. Both distinguish divine activity from Job's inability to apprehend it.

Job 9:12
If He takes away, who will turn Him back? Who will say to Him, ‘What hast Thou done?’
  Job 23:13 — Recurring question: unopposable divine action: Job asks who can turn God back or demand what He has done. He later asks who contradicted God's judgment and says God did whatever He wished, repeating the difficulty of contesting divine action.

Job 9:13
For He Himself has turned away anger; beneath Him the sea creatures under heaven bent down.
  Job 26:12-13 — Recurring cosmic imagery: sea creatures subject to God: Job says the sea creatures under heaven bent beneath God. Later he credits God with calming the sea, wounding the great sea creature and killing the rebellious dragon. The lines share divine mastery over the sea and its great creatures, without equating every creature or action.

Job 9:15
For even if I am righteous, He will not listen to me; I will plead for His judgment.
  Job 13:18 — Contrast within Job's legal argument: Job initially says even his righteousness would not secure a hearing and he must plead for judgment. Later he declares that he is near judgment and knows he will be shown righteous. The comparison preserves the movement in his confidence rather than flattening both statements.

Job 9:16
Even if I call and He answers, I do not believe that He has heard me.
  Job 30:20 — Recurring complaint: an unheard cry: Job doubts that God has heard him even if an answer comes; later he explicitly complains that he cried and God does not hear. The later protest sharpens the earlier uncertainty about a hearing.

Job 9:17
Might He crush me with gloom? He has multiplied my injuries without cause.
  Job 2:3 — Complaint tested against the prologue: Job describes injuries multiplied without cause. In the prologue the Lord affirms Job's continuing innocence and says the devil urged destruction of his belongings for nothing. The narrative supports the distinction between suffering and a demonstrated offense by Job.

Job 9:18
For He does not let me breathe, but has filled me with bitterness.
  Lamentations 3:15 — Shared lament: filled with bitterness: Job says God has filled him with bitterness; the lamenting speaker likewise says He filled him with bitterness and made him drunk with gall. Both use filling imagery for suffering attributed to divine action.

Job 9:19
For He prevails in strength. Who, then, will resist His judgment?
  Job 23:6-7 — Contrast: strength and a hearing: Job initially asks who can resist God's overwhelming strength in judgment. Later he envisages great strength without threat and asks for his case to reach its conclusion, expressing a changed hope about the same unequal encounter.

Job 9:20
For even if I am righteous, my mouth will act impiously; even if I am blameless, I will turn out crooked.
  Job 15:6 — Anticipated and asserted self-condemnation: Job fears that his own mouth will act impiously even if he is righteous. Eliphaz later says Job's mouth and lips will reprove and testify against him. Job's apprehension and his friend's accusation remain distinct positions.

Job 9:21
For whether I acted impiously, I do not know in my soul; I know only that my life is being taken away.
  Job 27:5-6 — Contrast in professions of innocence: Job says he does not know in his soul whether he acted impiously, knowing only that his life is being taken. Later he refuses to surrender innocence and says he is not conscious of improper deeds. The link preserves these different formulations of self-knowledge.

Job 9:23
for the worthless meet an extraordinary death, but the righteous are mocked,
  Job 12:4 — Recurring protest: righteous people mocked: Job says the righteous are mocked, then describes a righteous and blameless man becoming an object of mockery. The later line expands the description with blamelessness while repeating the complaint about mockery of the righteous.

Job 9:24
for they have been handed over into an ungodly person’s hands. He covers the faces of its judges; if it is not He, who is it?’
  Job 16:11 — Recurring complaint: handed to the ungodly: Job describes people handed into an ungodly person's hands and later says the Lord handed him into an unjust person's hands. The shared handover language belongs to Job's protest, not a new identification of the unnamed oppressor.

Job 9:26
Is there a trace of a ship’s way, or of an eagle flying in search of food?
  Proverbs 30:18-19 — Shared paired images: eagle and ship paths: Job asks about the trace of a ship's way and an eagle seeking food. Proverbs likewise pairs the tracks of a flying eagle with a seagoing ship's paths among things difficult to comprehend. The matching pair is more specific than a general statement about life's brevity.
  Wisdom of Solomon 5:10-11 — Shared paired images: vanished passage: Job's question about a ship's trace and an eagle's flight parallels the ship and bird whose passage leaves no trace in Wisdom. Wisdom places those images in the speech of the repentant wrongdoers; that speaker identity is not assigned to Job.

Job 9:28
I shake in all my limbs, for I know that Thou wilt not leave me innocent.
  Job 10:14-15 — Recurring anxiety about acquittal: Job fears God will not leave him innocent. He next says lawlessness has not been acquitted and that even righteousness leaves him unable to look up, extending the same concern about his standing before God.

Job 9:30
For if I wash myself with snow and purify myself with clean hands,
  Jeremiah 2:22 — Cleansing imagery with different claims: Job imagines washing with snow and clean hands, yet in the following line remains in filth. Jeremiah says washing with natron and cleansing herb leaves injustice stained before God. The shared failure of washing does not establish that Job bears the particular apostasy accused in Jeremiah.

Job 9:31
Thou hast thoroughly dipped me in filth, and my robe abhorred me.
  Zechariah 3:3-5 — Contrast: filthy garments and their removal: Job says he has been plunged into filth and his own robe abhors him. Joshua's filthy garments are instead removed and replaced as his lawless deeds are taken away. The garment comparison contrasts Job's complaint with a narrated act of cleansing.

Job 9:32
For Thou art not a human being like me, with whom I could contend, so that we might come together for judgment.
  Numbers 23:19 — Divine and human distinction: Job cannot litigate with God as with a fellow human being. Balaam's utterance likewise says God is not as a man, there emphasizing that He is not led astray or threatened and fulfills His words. The shared distinction serves different arguments.

Job 9:33
Would that our mediator were present, one to reprove and hear the cause between us both!
  1 Timothy 2:5-6 — Mediation: request and explicit identification: Job wishes for a mediator who can hear the cause between both sides. First Timothy explicitly names Christ Jesus as the mediator between God and humanity and speaks of His ransom. This compares the mediating role; Job's unnamed desired mediator is not treated as an explicit personal identification.

Job 9:34
Let him remove the rod from me, and let His terror not whirl me about,
  Job 13:20-22 — Repeated conditions for speaking: Job asks for the rod and terror to be removed so he can speak. His later two requests again seek the withdrawal of God's hand and overwhelming terror before an exchange of speech.

Job 10:1
“Weary in my soul and groaning, I will let my words loose upon Him; I will speak, held fast by the bitterness of my soul,
  Job 7:11 — Repeated declaration of bitter speech: Job announces that he will let his words loose and speak from bitterness, repeating his earlier refusal to restrain his mouth while disclosing his soul's bitterness. Both mark his deliberate decision to voice the complaint.

Job 10:2
and say to the Lord, ‘Do not teach me to act impiously! Why hast Thou judged me in this way?
  Job 13:23 — Legal protest and request for disclosure: Job asks why the Lord judged him this way, alongside his plea not to be taught to act impiously. Later he asks to be taught how many sins and lawless deeds he has. Both seek an explanation of the charge, without replacing this verse's unusual wording.

Job 10:4
Dost Thou observe as a mortal sees, or look as a human being looks?
  1 Samuel 16:7 — Divine sight distinguished from human sight: Job asks whether God sees as a mortal. In Samuel's encounter with Jesse's sons, God explicitly distinguishes human attention to the face from His own attention to the heart. Both contrast the modes of divine and human seeing.

Job 10:5
Is Thy life human, or are Thy years those of a man,
  Psalms 89:2-4 — Divine duration and human time: Job asks whether God's years are those of a man. Moses' prayer contrasts God's existence from age to age with a thousand years like yesterday or a watch in the night, supplying a particular comparison of divine and human time.

Job 10:6
that Thou didst search out my lawlessness and investigate my sins?
  Psalms 138:23-24 — Contrast in requests for divine examination: Job questions why God investigates his sins. The psalm instead asks God to examine the heart and paths and discover any way of lawlessness. Both concern divine scrutiny of wrongdoing, approached as complaint and invitation respectively.

Job 10:7
For Thou knowest that I did not act impiously; but who is there to rescue from Thy hands?
  Job 2:3 — Claim of innocence and narrative testimony: Job appeals to God's knowledge that he did not act impiously. The prologue has the Lord affirm his innocence and blamelessness even after loss, providing narrative context for his claim without endorsing every later accusation he makes.
  Deuteronomy 32:39 — Shared formula: no rescue from God's hands: Job asks who can rescue from God's hands. The divine declaration in Moses' song similarly says no one can deliver from His hands, alongside His power to kill, make alive, strike and heal.

Job 10:8
Thy hands molded me and made me; afterward Thou didst change and strike me.
  Isaiah 64:7-8 — Creator's hands invoked amid affliction: Job sets God's molding hands against the subsequent blow. Isaiah's communal prayer calls the people clay and the work of God's hands, then asks Him not to be exceedingly angry. Both appeal to the maker-creature relation during distress.

Job 10:9
Remember that Thou didst mold me as clay, and Thou art turning me back again into earth.
  Genesis 2:7 — Formation from earth: Job recalls being molded as clay; Genesis narrates God forming the human from the earth's dust. The correspondence is the divine shaping of an earthly body, not a claim that the two accounts use identical material terms.
  Genesis 3:19 — Return to the material of formation: Job says God is turning him back into earth. The sentence to Adam likewise speaks of returning to the earth from which he was taken, connecting mortal dissolution with earthly formation.

Job 10:11
Thou didst clothe me with skin and flesh and thread me together with bones and sinews.
  Psalms 138:13-16 — The body as divine handiwork: Job describes being clothed with skin and flesh and threaded with bones and sinews. The psalm speaks of God's knowledge and making of the body's hidden bone and unworked substance in the context of the womb. These are particular depictions of embodied formation, not identical anatomical accounts.

Job 10:13
Thou holdest these things within Thyself; I know that Thou canst do all things, and nothing is impossible for Thee.
  Job 42:2 — Repeated confession of divine possibility: Job's acknowledgment that God can do all things and nothing is impossible for Him recurs in his final reply to the Lord. The repeated wording spans the complaint and the concluding response.

Job 10:14
For if I sin, Thou guardest me, and Thou hast not acquitted me of lawlessness.
  Job 7:21 — Recurring plea concerning unremoved sin: Job says lawlessness has not been acquitted after previously asking why forgetfulness and cleansing of his sin had not been granted. Both make the absence of release from sin part of his address to God.

Job 10:15
For if I am ungodly, alas for me! If I am righteous, I cannot look up, for I am full of dishonor.
  Job 9:20 — Righteousness without security: Job contrasts his cry of alas if ungodly with inability to look up even if righteous. Earlier he feared that even righteousness and blamelessness would end with an impious mouth and a crooked result. Both express his sense that innocence does not secure a fair outcome.

Job 10:16
For I am hunted like a lion for slaughter; changing again, Thou destroyest me terribly,
  Lamentations 3:10-11 — Lament imagery: lion and pursuit: Job describes being hunted like a lion for slaughter. Lamentations portrays the divine adversary as a hidden lion who pursues and desolates the speaker. Both employ predation imagery for affliction, but Job is the hunted comparison while the lament describes the pursuer.

Job 10:17
renewing my examination against me. Thou didst deal with me in great anger and bring trials upon me.
  Job 13:26 — Recurring forensic complaint: Job complains that examination against him is renewed in anger; later he says evils have been recorded against him and the sins of youth put around him. Both express the sense of an accumulating divine case against him.

Job 10:18
Why, then, didst Thou bring me out of the womb? Why did I not die with no eye seeing me,
  Job 3:11 — Repeated wish concerning birth and death: Job asks why he was brought from the womb rather than dying unseen. His opening lament already asked why he did not die in the womb or perish immediately upon coming out.

Job 10:19
and become as though I had not existed? For why was I not taken from the womb to the tomb?
  Job 3:16 — Repeated image: existence without seeing life: Job wishes he had passed from womb to tomb as though he never existed. The earlier comparison with a miscarriage or infants who never saw light expresses the same counterfactual wish without identifying it as an actual event.

Job 10:20
Is not the time of my life short? Let me rest a little
  Job 7:16-19 — Repeated request for brief respite: Job asks to rest a little because his lifetime is short. Earlier he asked to be left alone because he would not live forever and wanted even enough respite to swallow, giving concrete form to the same appeal.

Job 10:21
before I go where I will not return, to a dark and gloomy land,
  Job 7:9-10 — Recurring description of departure without return: Job anticipates going to a land from which he will not return. Earlier he described descent to Hades as not returning to one's house or recognized place. These remain statements within his lament.

Job 11:2
“The one who says much will hear an answer in return. Does an eloquent person also think himself righteous? Blessed is the one born of woman who lives a short life.
  Proverbs 10:19 — Many words and the claim to righteousness: Zophar challenges the righteousness of an eloquent, much-speaking person. Proverbs warns that much talking does not escape sin and commends sparing the lips. The proverb parallels the warning about speech, without proving Zophar's accusation against Job.

Job 11:4
For do not say, ‘I am pure in my works and blameless before Him.’
  Job 10:7 — Disputed profession of innocence: Zophar tells Job not to claim purity and blamelessness before God. Job had just appealed to God's knowledge that he did not act impiously. The link identifies a specific claim under dispute between the speakers.

Job 11:5
But how might the Lord speak to you? He will open His lips to speak with you.
  Job 38:1-3 — Requested divine speech and eventual answer: Zophar wishes the Lord would speak to Job. The narrative later records the Lord speaking through a whirlwind and requiring Job to answer. That eventual speech does not itself validate Zophar's following accusation about deserved suffering.

Job 11:6
Then He will tell you the power of wisdom, for it will be twice what you have. Then you will know that what has come to you from the Lord is worthy of the sins you committed.
  Job 42:7-9 — Narrative correction of a friend's verdict: Zophar says Job's suffering is worthy of his committed sins. The closing narrative instead includes Zophar among the friends who did not speak truth against Job and who require his offering and prayer. The narrative verdict prevents treating Zophar's sentence as an established explanation of Job's affliction.

Job 11:7
Will you find a trace of the Lord, or have you reached the furthest things the Almighty made?
  Romans 11:33-34 — Unsearchable divine wisdom: Zophar asks whether Job can find the Lord's trace or reach the furthest divine works. Paul's doxology describes divine judgments as unsearchable and ways as untraceable, then asks who knew His mind or became His counselor. The wisdom comparison does not adopt Zophar's personal charges.

Job 11:8
Heaven is high—what will you do? What do you know of things deeper than those in Hades,
  Psalms 138:8 — Heaven and Hades as limits of human reach: Zophar uses heaven's height and depths beyond Hades to challenge human knowledge. The psalm pairs ascent to heaven and descent into Hades to describe God's presence. The shared vertical extremes serve distinct arguments about knowledge and presence.

Job 11:10
If He overturns everything, who will say to Him, ‘What hast Thou done?’
  Job 9:12 — A question repeated by an opponent: Zophar's question, who can say to God what He has done, repeats Job's own question. Their agreement about unopposable divine action does not settle their disagreement about Job's guilt.

Job 11:11
For He knows the works of the lawless; when He sees improper things, He will not overlook them.
  Job 34:21-22 — Divine knowledge of lawless conduct: Zophar says God knows lawless works and will not overlook improper things. Elihu later states that no human action escapes God and lawless people have nowhere to hide. The two speakers share this assertion without its proving their specific charges against Job.

Job 11:13
For if you make your heart pure and turn your hands upward toward Him,
  Lamentations 3:40-41 — Prayer joined to inward turning: Zophar urges purity of heart and hands turned upward toward God. Lamentations joins a return to the Lord with lifting hearts upon hands to the Exalted One in heaven. Both combine inward turning and an embodied prayer gesture.

Job 11:14
if anything lawless is in your hands, put it far from you, and let injustice not lodge in your dwelling.
  Isaiah 1:15-17 — Removing wrongdoing before supplication: Zophar requires lawlessness to be removed from the hands and dwelling. Isaiah's rebuke of blood-filled praying hands likewise calls for washing, removing evil deeds and seeking justice. This is a comparison of the prescribed response, not evidence that Job committed the crimes Isaiah condemns.

Job 11:15
For then your face will shine like pure water; you will take off filth and will not fear.
  Job 9:30-31 — Conflicting cleansing expectations: Zophar promises a face like pure water and removal of filth. Job had imagined washing himself yet being plunged into filth. Their opposing outcomes for cleansing express a concrete disagreement about Job's prospects before God.

Job 11:16
You will forget toil like a wave that has passed, and you will not be terrified.
  Isaiah 65:16-17 — Promised forgetting of former affliction: Zophar promises Job that toil will be forgotten like a passing wave. Isaiah likewise promises that former affliction will be forgotten and not enter the heart, within a wider account of renewal. The common promise does not identify Zophar's assurance with Isaiah's setting.

Job 11:17
Your prayer will be like the morning star, and from noon life will rise for you.
  Psalms 36:6 — Light at noon as an image of restoration: Zophar compares prayer to the morning star and says life will rise from noon. The psalm promises righteousness like light and judgment like noon. The link preserves Zophar's particular prayer-and-life wording while comparing the use of bright-day imagery for vindication or renewal.

Job 11:19
For you will be quiet, and no one will wage war against you; many will change their ways and entreat you.
  Job 42:8-9 — Narrative reversal: others entreating Job: Zophar predicts that many will change their ways and entreat Job. At the end, Zophar himself joins the other friends in going to Job as commanded for the offering and prayer through which their sin is released. This is a particular reversal of the adviser's role.

Job 11:20
But deliverance will abandon them, for their hope is destruction, and the eyes of the ungodly will waste away.”
  Job 8:13 — Shared warning: the ungodly person's hope: Zophar concludes that the ungodly person's hope is destruction; Bildad had likewise said that the hope of an ungodly person will perish. These parallel conclusions belong to the friends' speeches and do not independently establish Job's guilt.

Job 12:2
“So you are the people! Surely wisdom will die with you!
  Job 13:2 — Rebuke of exclusive wisdom: Job sarcastically says wisdom must die with his companions, then explicitly says he knows as much as they do and is not less intelligent. The second assertion makes the force of the earlier sarcasm clear.

Job 12:3
I too have a heart like yours.
  Job 13:2 — Repeated claim of equal understanding: Job's claim to have a heart like his companions is developed as knowing as much as they know and being no less intelligent. The two statements reject their assumed intellectual superiority.

Job 12:4
For a righteous and blameless man became an object of mockery.
  Job 1:1 — Narrative characterization and humiliating treatment: Job describes a righteous and blameless man made a mockery. Those are characteristics the narrator gives Job at the book's opening, making the mismatch between his characterization and treatment particularly significant.

Job 12:6
But indeed, let no wicked person trust that he will be innocent, nor those who provoke the Lord suppose that there will be no examination of them.
  Job 34:9 — Agreement that wickedness faces examination: Job warns wicked people not to suppose they will escape examination. Elihu later likewise rejects the claim that a man faces no inspection from the Lord. This comparison retains the actual warning in 12:6 instead of importing a different wording about secure robbers' tents.

Job 12:7
But ask the four-footed animals, to see whether they will tell you, and the birds of heaven, to see whether they will report to you.
  Proverbs 6:6-8c — Learning through nonhuman creatures: Job directs his listeners to ask animals and birds for instruction. Proverbs tells the sluggard to observe the ant and learn from the bee. Both use nonhuman creatures as teachers, with different lessons and named creatures.

Job 12:9
Who among all these did not know that the Lord’s hand made these things?
  Genesis 1:20-25 — Creation of the creatures invoked as witnesses: Job says these creatures know the Lord's hand made them, after naming land animals, birds and fish. Genesis narrates God's making of sea creatures, winged birds and land animals, providing the corresponding creation account for those classes.

Job 12:10
Is not the life of all living things in His hand, and the breath of every human being?
  Acts 17:24-28 — Every life and breath dependent on God: Job places all living things and every human breath in God's hand. Paul's speech similarly describes the creator as giving everyone life and breath and sustaining human existence. Both specify universal bodily dependence on the creator.

Job 12:11
For the ear discerns words, and the throat tastes food.
  Job 34:3 — Repeated analogy: ear and throat: Elihu later repeats Job's comparison of an ear testing words with a throat tasting food. The same sensory analogy is used by different speakers in their effort to evaluate the debate.

Job 12:12
In much time is wisdom, and in a long life knowledge.
  Job 32:6-9 — Age and wisdom reconsidered: Job associates wisdom and knowledge with long life. Elihu recalls having deferred to age for that reason but then attributes instruction to the Almighty's breath and denies that elders necessarily know judgment. The comparison preserves Elihu's qualification of the assumption.

Job 12:13
With Him are wisdom and power; to Him belong counsel and understanding.
  Daniel 2:20-21 — Divine possession and giving of wisdom: Job locates wisdom, power, counsel and understanding with God. Daniel blesses the Lord for wisdom and majesty and for giving wisdom and understanding, moving from divine possession to its gift to humans.

Job 12:15
If He restrains water, He will dry the earth; if He lets it loose, He destroys it by overturning it.
  Genesis 7:17-23 — Narrative illustration: destructive released water: Job describes water let loose to destroy the earth. The flood narrative depicts rising waters covering mountains and destroying life on dry land. It illustrates the kind of power Job names without claiming that his general statement explicitly identifies Noah's flood.
  Genesis 8:1-3 — Narrative illustration: restrained water: Job contrasts released destructive waters with restraint that dries the earth. Genesis describes God bringing a wind, closing the fountains and floodgates, restraining rain and causing waters to withdraw. The link concerns the particular reversal of flooding through divine restraint.

Job 12:17
He leads counselors away captive and throws the earth’s judges into confusion.
  Isaiah 19:11-14 — Counselors and rulers deprived of sound judgment: Job describes counselors led captive and judges confused. Isaiah's Egypt oracle depicts royal counselors becoming foolish and rulers leading the country astray. This compares the collapse of governing wisdom, not a direct prediction of that oracle's events.

Job 12:18
He seats kings on thrones and bound their waists with a belt.
  Daniel 2:21 — Divine appointment of kings: Job says God seats kings on thrones; Daniel says He transfers kings and appoints them. The link retains the actual enthroning wording in Job, rather than replacing it with an alternative about loosening kings' bonds.

Job 12:19
He sends priests away captive and overthrows the earth’s rulers.
  2 Kings 25:18-21 — Historical illustration: priests carried captive: Job says God sends priests captive and overturns rulers. The fall of Judah includes the capture and removal of the first priest and other officials to the Babylonian king. The episode illustrates captured religious and civil leaders without becoming an identified prediction in Job.

Job 12:21
He pours dishonor upon rulers and heals the lowly.
  Psalms 106:40-41 — Shared reversal: rulers dishonored, poor helped: Job pairs dishonor poured on rulers with healing of the lowly. The psalm similarly pairs contempt poured on rulers with help for the poor. The two-part reversal is more specific than a general statement about divine power.

Job 12:22
He uncovers deep things from darkness and brings death’s shadow into light.
  Daniel 2:22 — Shared image: deep darkness disclosed: Job says God uncovers deep things from darkness and brings death's shadow to light. Job 12:23

The comparison concerns nations as objects of divine governance without equating the unnamed peoples in Job with one specific kingdom.

Job 12:24

  Psalms 106:40 — Shared image: rulers wandering off a road: Job describes earthly rulers' changed hearts and wandering on a road they did not know. The psalm describes rulers wandering in an impassable place that is not a road. Both join rulers' humiliation with loss of a navigable route.

Job 12:25

Job 13:1

  Job 42:5 — Hearing and seeing reconsidered: Job appeals to what his eye has seen and ear heard as credentials in the dispute. His final response contrasts earlier hearing with now seeing God. The later claim is a new encounter, not an assertion that the earlier sight was already that encounter.

Job 13:2

  Job 12:3 — Repeated claim to understanding equal to the friends: Job says he knows as much as his companions and is not less intelligent, spelling out his earlier assertion that he too has a heart like theirs. Both resist the friends' claim to superior discernment.

  Job 23:3-7 — Recurring desire to state a case before God: Job wants to speak and argue before the Lord if He is willing. Later he wishes to find Him, state his case, fill his mouth with arguments and hear the response, expanding the same legal request.

Job 13:4
But you are unjust physicians and healers of evils, all of you.
  Job 16:2 — Failed care: physicians and comforters: Job calls the friends unjust physicians and healers of evils, and later comforters of evils. The repeated description criticizes their failure in the caring role for which they came.

Job 13:5

  Proverbs 17:28 — Silence reckoned as wisdom: Job says silence would become wisdom for his companions. Proverbs similarly says someone who makes himself mute will seem intelligent. Both invert the assumption that speaking demonstrates understanding.

Job 13:7
Are you not speaking before the Lord, and uttering deceit before Him?
  Job 42:7-8 — Narrative response to allegedly false speech before God: Job accuses the friends of speaking deceit before the Lord. At the conclusion the Lord tells Eliphaz and his companions that they spoke nothing true before Him and did not speak truth against Job. The later verdict addresses the very issue Job raises.

Job 13:10

  Job 42:7-9 — Anticipated divine reproof of the friends: Job warns that God will reprove the friends despite their position in the debate. The final narrative records that reproof and requires them to seek the offering and prayer of the man they accused.
  James 2:9 — Condemnation of partiality: Job warns that secretly admiring persons will not escape divine reproof. James likewise calls favoritism sin and says the law convicts its practitioners. The comparison concerns partiality, not an identity between the two audiences.

Job 13:11
will not His terrors whirl you about, and fear from Him fall upon you?
  Job 9:34 — Divine terror turned toward the advisers: Job asks whether divine terror will overwhelm his friends, after asking that such terror be removed from himself. The repeated terror language turns their discussion toward their own exposure to divine judgment.

Job 13:12
Your boasting will turn out like ashes, and your body will be clay.
  Job 4:19-20 — Clay mortality turned back on the adviser: Job says the friends' bodies will be clay. Eliphaz's earlier reported message described humans dwelling in houses of clay and perishing within a day. Job applies the bodily fragility they invoked to the advisers themselves.

Job 13:13
Be silent, so that I may speak and rest from anger,
  Job 32:20 — Speech sought as relief: Job asks for silence so he may speak and rest from anger. Elihu later says he will speak so he may rest, opening his lips. The repeated purpose of obtaining relief through speech belongs to different speakers.

Job 13:14
taking my flesh up in my teeth, while I will put my life in my hand.
  1 Samuel 19:5 — Shared idiom: life placed in one's hand: Job speaks of putting his life in his hand as he insists on speaking. Jonathan describes David placing his soul in his hand when striking the foreigner. The comparison is the idiom of personal exposure to danger, not an identification of the actions.

Job 13:15
If the Mighty One subdues me, as He has already begun, I will surely speak and plead before Him.
  Job 23:4-7 — Persistence in pleading despite divine pressure: Job says he will speak and plead even as the Mighty One subdues him. Later he describes presenting arguments and asking the powerful God to conclude his case. This preserves the source's resolve to plead; it does not import an alternative wording about trusting if slain.

Job 13:16
This will turn out to be deliverance for me, for deceit will not enter before Him.
  Psalms 5:5-7 — Deceit excluded from the divine presence: Job expects deliverance because deceit will not enter before God. The psalm says evildoers cannot sojourn with Him or remain before His eyes and that He destroys speakers of falsehood. Both connect access to the divine presence with exclusion of deceit.

Job 13:18
Behold, I am near my judgment; I know that I will be shown righteous.
  Job 40:8 — Expected vindication and the Lord's stated purpose: Job says he knows he will be shown righteous. The Lord later asks whether His dealing with Job had any purpose other than that Job might be shown righteous. The close verbal link does not make all of Job's preceding judgments correct.

Job 13:19
For who is there to enter judgment with me? For now I will be silent and fail.
  Isaiah 50:8-9 — Forensic challenge to an accuser: Job asks who will enter judgment with him. Isaiah's speaker, with the justifier near, likewise challenges whoever brings a case to approach. These are comparable legal challenges by distinct speakers.

Job 13:21
Keep Thy hand away from me, and let Thy terror not overwhelm me.
  Job 9:34-35 — Repeated request: remove terror before speech: Job asks that God's hand and overwhelming terror be kept away. Earlier he asked for removal of the rod and terror so he could speak without fear. Both state conditions he desires for a hearing.

Job 13:22

  Job 38:3 — Requested exchange and eventual demand to answer: Job proposes that God call or speak and he answer. The Lord later explicitly says He will question Job and Job must answer Him. The eventual encounter places the initiative with God, as this source's wording already does.

Job 13:23
How many are my sins and lawless deeds? Teach me what they are.
  Job 6:24 — Repeated request to be taught his error: Job asks to be taught his sins and lawless deeds. Earlier he told the friends to teach him, promising silence if they could tell him where he had gone astray. Both demand a stated offense rather than an unsupported accusation.

Job 13:24
Why dost Thou hide from me and regard me as opposed to Thee?
  Job 19:11 — Recurring complaint: treated as an opponent: Job asks why God regards him as opposed to Him. Later he says God dealt with him in anger and regarded him like an enemy. The latter restates the perceived hostile relationship in the complaint.

Job 13:26

  Psalms 24:7 — Sins of youth remembered or released: Job complains that the sins of youth have been put around him. The psalm asks the Lord not to remember youthful sins and acts of ignorance, but to remember the speaker according to mercy. Both explicitly concern the continuing reckoning of youthful wrongdoing.

Job 13:27
Thou didst put my foot in a restraint and watch all my works; Thou didst reach to the roots of my feet.
  Job 33:10-11 — Elihu's reprise of Job's complaint: Job describes a foot in restraint and all his works watched. Elihu later quotes a complaint that God regards the speaker as an opponent, put his foot in a wooden restraint and guarded his ways. The shared restraint and surveillance mark a specific reprise within the debate.

Job 13:28
It grows old like a wineskin, or like a moth-eaten garment.
  Isaiah 50:9 — Shared image: garment consumed by a moth: Job ends the complaint with growing old like a wineskin or a moth-eaten garment. Isaiah's speaker likewise describes opponents growing old like a garment and being devoured like a moth. The decay imagery is shared, while its application differs.

Job 14:1

  Sirach 40:1-4 — Human life from birth burdened by trouble: Job describes the mortal born of woman as short-lived and full of wrath. Sirach describes the heavy yoke from the mother's womb to the mother of all and lists anger, trouble and fear across social ranks. Both present trouble as a condition of embodied mortal life.

Job 14:2
Like a flower that blossomed, he fell away; he fled like a shadow and will not remain.
  Psalms 102:15-16 — Shared image: human life as a disappearing flower: Job compares a mortal to a flower that blossoms and falls away. The psalm likewise compares human days to a field flower that vanishes after wind passes. Both locate human transience in the flower's brief flourishing.

Job 14:4
For who will be clean from filth? No one.
  Job 25:4 — Repeated question of mortal cleansing: Job asks who can be clean from filth and answers no one. Bildad later asks how a mortal can be righteous before the Lord and how one born of woman can cleanse himself. The question is shared across opponents, not a settled verdict on Job's particular conduct.

Job 14:5
Even if his life on earth is one day, his months are numbered with Thee; Thou didst set a time, and he will certainly not go beyond it.
  Psalms 38:5-6 — The numbered limit of mortal life: Job says a person's months are numbered with God and a fixed time cannot be exceeded. The psalm asks to know the limit and number of one's days, calling them handbreadths. Both make life's bounded duration the subject of prayer.

Job 14:6
Leave him alone so that he may be quiet and take pleasure in life like a hired worker.
  Job 7:1-2 — Recurring image: life as hired labor: Job asks to be left quiet to enjoy life like a hired worker. Earlier he likened human existence to a day worker and a hired worker waiting for wages. The comparison develops his request for relief through the same laboring figure.

Job 14:7
For a tree has hope: if it is cut down, it will blossom again, and its shoot will not fail.
  Ezekiel 17:24 — Hope expressed through renewed tree growth: Job contrasts human frailty with a cut tree's hope of blossoming again. Ezekiel's tree oracle speaks of the Lord making a dry tree flourish. Both depict renewal through renewed tree growth, but Ezekiel's royal image is not identified with Job's illustrative tree.

Job 14:9
from the scent of water it will blossom and produce a harvest like a newly planted tree.
  Job 8:11-12 — Water and plant survival in opposing speeches: Job describes water bringing renewed growth even when the root grows old and the trunk dies in rock. Bildad had asked whether papyrus or rushes flourish without water and described unwatered plants withering. Their plant images agree about dependence on water but place it within different arguments about hope.

Job 14:10
But a man who has died is gone; a mortal who has fallen is no more.
  Job 14:14 — same-chapter tension: death and renewed life: Job says the dead mortal is gone and no more, then says that if a person dies he will live and that he waits to come to be again. The comparison preserves both statements and their tension, without rewriting either as a question or supplying a harmonized chronology.

Job 14:11
For in time a sea diminishes, and a river, left desolate, dries up.
  Isaiah 19:5-7 — Shared image: waters and rivers drying away: Job uses a diminishing sea and dried river in his meditation on mortality. Isaiah's Egypt oracle describes a failing river, dry channels and withering river plants. The same loss of water serves different subjects rather than identifying one event.

Job 14:12
But a person who lies down will certainly not rise until heaven is no longer stitched together; they will not wake from their sleep.
  Job 42:17a — Lament and closing notice about rising again: Job says the lying-down person will not rise until heaven is no longer stitched together or wake from sleep. The book's closing notice explicitly says Job will rise with those the Lord raises. Both are retained in their narrative positions without erasing the lament's temporal wording.

Job 14:13
If only Thou hadst guarded me in Hades and hidden me until Thy wrath ceased, and set a time for me at which Thou wouldst remember me!
  Isaiah 26:19-20 — Hidden until wrath passes, beside hope for the dead: Job wishes to be hidden in Hades until divine wrath ceases and then remembered. Isaiah places the dead rising beside an instruction to hide in inner rooms until the Lord's wrath passes. The parallel concerns protected waiting during wrath, while Hades and the inner rooms remain different locations.

Job 14:14
For if a person dies, he will live, having completed the days of his life. I will wait until I come to be again.
  Job 42:17a — Expectation of renewed life and closing notice: Job says that a person who dies will live and that he will wait until he comes to be again. The closing notice explicitly states that he will rise with those the Lord raises. The link depends on the affirmative wording actually supplied here.

Job 14:15
Then Thou wilt call, and I will answer Thee. Do not reject the works of Thy hands.
  Job 13:22 — Repeated call-and-answer hope: Job anticipates God calling and his own answer, repeating the proposed divine call and human reply of his earlier request for a hearing. Here the appeal also invokes the works of God's hands.

Job 14:16
Thou didst number my practices, and not one of my sins will pass Thee by.
  Job 31:4 — Divine counting of human conduct: Job says God numbered his practices and no sin will pass unnoticed. Later he asks whether God will see his way and count all his steps. Both present divine scrutiny through the particular image of counting one's conduct or steps.

Job 14:17
Thou didst seal my lawless deeds in a bag and mark whether I transgressed in anything unwillingly.
  Hosea 13:11-12 — Stored wrongdoing pictured as a bundle: Job describes his lawless deeds sealed in a bag. Hosea's utterance speaks of a bundle of injustice and Ephraim's hidden sin. The comparison is the packaging of wrongdoing for reckoning; it does not add a sealed bag to Hosea's wording.

Job 14:18
And indeed, a falling mountain will fall apart, and a rock will grow worn away from its place.
  Job 9:5 — Recurring image: mountains worn and overturned: Job's falling mountain and rock worn from its place return to his earlier description of God wearing mountains out and overturning them. The repeated imagery frames human endurance against even apparently durable terrain's vulnerability.

Job 14:21

  Ecclesiastes 9:5-6 — Loss of knowledge of life among the living: Job describes a departed man not knowing whether his sons become many or few. Ecclesiastes says the dead know nothing and have no further share in what is done under the sun. Both address loss of awareness or participation in earthly affairs.

Job 15:3

  Job 16:3 — A charge about words answered in kind: Eliphaz condemns speeches without benefit; Job's next reply challenges the order in words of wind and asks what troubles the friend into answering. The exchange shows the accusation of unhelpful speech returned to the accuser.

Job 15:4
Did you not also reject fear and complete such words before the Lord?
  Job 13:15 — Disputed persistence in speaking before God: Eliphaz treats Job's words before the Lord as rejection of fear. Job had declared that he would continue speaking and pleading under divine pressure. The link preserves the contrast between Job's explanation of his persistence and Eliphaz's judgment of it.

Job 15:6
May your own mouth reprove you, not I; your lips will testify against you.
  Job 9:20 — Job's feared self-condemnation used by Eliphaz: Eliphaz says Job's own mouth and lips will reprove and testify against him. Earlier Job feared that his mouth would act impiously even if he was righteous. Eliphaz turns the danger Job named into an accusation.

Job 15:7

  Proverbs 8:24-25 — Birth before the hills: challenge and Wisdom's claim: Eliphaz asks whether Job was formed before the hills. Wisdom's speech places her birth before the mountains and hills. The shared pre-mountain language contrasts the wisdom Job is challenged to possess with Wisdom's narrated priority, without identifying Job with Wisdom.

Job 15:9
For what do you know that we do not know? What do you understand that we do not also understand?
  Job 13:2 — A claim of equal knowledge returned: Eliphaz asks what Job knows that the friends do not. Job had just said he knew as much as they and was no less intelligent. The matching challenge reveals a reciprocal dispute over intellectual standing.

Job 15:10
Among us are both an elder and an ancient one, weightier in days than your father.
  Job 32:6-9 — Age offered as authority and later qualified: Eliphaz appeals to elders older than Job's father. Elihu explains that he initially deferred because the others were older, then denies that longevity guarantees wisdom or judgment. The comparison concerns the disputed authority of age.

Job 15:11
You have been scourged for few of the sins you committed; you have spoken far too much.
  Job 11:6 — Friends' claims about deserved punishment: Eliphaz says Job was scourged for few of his sins, whereas Zophar said what came from the Lord was worthy of Job's committed sins. Both presume actual offenses behind suffering, while their statements about its measure differ.

Job 15:13
that you burst out in anger before the Lord and brought such words out of your mouth?
  Job 42:7 — Reversal of an accusation about speech before God: Eliphaz accuses Job of bursting out in anger and bringing improper words before the Lord. At the end the Lord rebukes Eliphaz and his two companions for speaking nothing true before Him, as Job did. The narrative turns the issue of culpable speech back upon the accuser.

Job 15:14
For what mortal can be blameless, or one born of woman be righteous?
  Job 4:17 — Eliphaz repeats his question about mortal righteousness: Eliphaz asks how a mortal can be blameless or one born of woman righteous. His earlier reported speech asked whether a mortal can be pure before the Lord or blameless through works. Both are his arguments from human limitation.

Job 15:15
If He puts no trust in holy ones, and heaven is not pure before Him,
  Job 4:18 — Repeated distrust of exalted beings: Eliphaz says God puts no trust in holy ones and that heaven is not pure before Him. His earlier reported message said God places no trust in servants and finds crookedness in angels. The recurring argument retains the different designations and does not specify the identity of every being concerned.

Job 15:16
let alone the loathsome and unclean man, who drinks injustice as a drink.
  Job 34:7 — Shared drinking metaphor: injustice and mockery: Eliphaz describes an unclean man drinking injustice like a drink. Elihu later describes Job as drinking mockery like water. The comparison concerns the distinctive drinking metaphor, while injustice and mockery and the speakers' applications remain distinct.

Job 15:18
what wise people will say; they did not conceal their fathers.
  Job 8:8-10 — Fathers invoked alongside wisdom: Eliphaz introduces what wise people will say and says they did not conceal their fathers. Bildad explicitly directs inquiry to the former generation and the fathers for teaching. The speeches invoke fathers alongside wisdom, but Job 15:18 does not expressly say the wise received their report from those fathers.

Job 15:21
His fear is in his ears; when he thinks he is already at peace, his overthrow will come.
  Jeremiah 6:14-15 — Assumed peace followed by downfall: Eliphaz says overthrow comes when an ungodly person thinks he is at peace. Jeremiah condemns assurances of peace where there is none and announces a fall at visitation. Both contrast confidence in peace with approaching judgment, without equating the accused parties.

Job 15:22
Let him not trust that he will turn back from darkness, for he has already been assigned to the hands of iron,
  Job 18:18-21 — Friends' shared darkness imagery for the wicked: Eliphaz says the ungodly person must not expect to return from darkness. Bildad later describes removal from light into darkness and calls such places the houses of the unjust. These are parallel portrayals in the friends' speeches, not an independent identification of Job as ungodly.

Job 15:23

  Deuteronomy 28:26 — Judgment image: the dead as food for birds: Eliphaz assigns the ungodly person as food for vultures. The covenant curse says the dead will be food for heaven's birds and earthly beasts. The particular image is shared without limiting the wider bird category to vultures or claiming Job is under that covenant curse.

Job 15:25
because he raised his hands against the Lord and stiffened his neck before the Lord Almighty,
  Deuteronomy 31:27 — Stiff-necked opposition to God: Eliphaz describes the ungodly person stiffening his neck against the Lord. Moses similarly speaks of Israel's stiff neck and provocation of God. Both make bodily stiffness an image of resistance, applied to different subjects.

Job 15:27
because he covered his face with his fat and made a collar upon his thighs.
  Deuteronomy 32:15 — Bodily fatness accompanying rebellion: Eliphaz's hostile figure covers his face with fat and forms a collar on his thighs in the context of resisting God. Moses' song describes Jacob growing fat, thick and broad and forsaking his maker. Both associate luxuriant bodily fatness with rebellion in their respective portrayals.

Job 15:29

  Proverbs 13:11 — The ungodly person's wealth failing to endure: Eliphaz says the ungodly person's belongings will not remain. Proverbs says property eagerly pursued with lawlessness becomes less, contrasting it with pious increase. Both connect wealth's failure to a negative moral description, but only Proverbs here specifies the manner of acquisition; neither comparison proves Job's former wealth unjust.

Job 15:30
nor will he escape darkness. May wind wither his shoot, and may his flower fall away.
  Isaiah 40:6-8 — Shoot and flower withering: Eliphaz describes the ungodly person's shoot withered by wind and flower falling. Isaiah compares all human flesh and glory to grass and a falling flower. Both use botanical decline for transience, but Isaiah's broader human scope is not restricted to Eliphaz's ungodly figure.

Job 15:32
His cutting will be destroyed before its time, and his shoot will not grow dense.
  Job 8:12 — Plant ruined before its expected completion: Eliphaz says the cutting is destroyed before its time and its shoot will not grow dense. Bildad described an unharvested plant withering before it drinks. Both use premature plant failure in their account of an ungodly person's end.

Job 15:33

Isaiah describes clusters and vine branches removed before harvest while fruit is unripe. The shared pre-harvest destruction is specific, while Eliphaz's additional olive-blossom image is not attributed to Isaiah.

Job 15:34

  Isaiah 5:23-24 — Corrupt judgment followed by burning imagery: Eliphaz says fire will burn the houses of bribe-takers. Isaiah condemns those who justify the ungodly for gifts and then uses the image of a stalk consumed by fire for their ruin. Both connect corrupt reward with fiery judgment, without making Isaiah's stalk into a literal house.

Job 15:35
He will conceive pains in the womb; empty things will result for him, and his belly will bear deceit.”
  Psalms 7:15 — Shared pregnancy image for wrongdoing: Eliphaz describes conceiving pains and a belly bearing deceit. The psalm describes labor with injustice, conception of trouble and birth of lawlessness. Both organize wrongdoing through the linked stages of conception and birth.

Job 16:2
“I have heard many such things; you are all comforters of evils.
  Job 2:11-13 — Failed fulfillment of the friends' purpose: Job calls the companions comforters of evils. The narrative introduced their visit as an effort to comfort him and showed their initial silent presence. His criticism measures their later speeches against that stated purpose.

Job 16:3
What then? Is there any order in words of wind? What will trouble you, that you answer?
  Job 8:2 — The accusation of windy speech returned: Job challenges words of wind, while Bildad had described Job's breath as full of words. The shared speech-and-breath imagery shows Job turning a friend's charge back toward the advisers.

Job 16:4

  Psalms 21:8 — Shared mocking gesture: shaking the head: Job imagines speaking against his friends and shaking his head if their lives exchanged places. The psalm describes onlookers sneering, speaking with their lips and shaking their heads. The gesture is the comparison; Job's imagined reversal is not a report that it occurred.

Job 16:8
and Thou didst seize me; it became a testimony. My falsehood rose up within me and answered against my face.
  Job 10:17 — Affliction represented as testimony against Job: Job describes being seized and the resulting testimony and answer against his face. His earlier complaint described renewed examination against him. Both treat affliction through forensic language, without replacing this verse's reference to his falsehood with a different reading.

Job 16:9
Dealing in anger, He cast me down and gnashed His teeth at me; the arrows of His raiders fell upon me.
  Job 6:4 — Recurring image: divine arrows in Job: Job describes anger and the arrows of God's raiders falling upon him, returning to his earlier statement that the Lord's arrows are in his body and stab him when he speaks. Both portray suffering as an armed divine assault from Job's perspective, without identifying every raider.

Job 16:11

  Job 2:6-7 — Complaint and prologue: being handed over: Job says the Lord handed him to an unjust person's hands. In the prologue the Lord hands him to the devil with a limit on his life, after which the devil strikes him. The handover is a narrative comparison; the unnamed unjust person in Job's speech is not thereby identified as the devil.

Job 16:12
While I was at peace, He scattered me; He took me by the hair and tore me apart, and set me up like a target.
  Lamentations 3:12 — Shared image: sufferer made a target: Job says God set him up like a target. Lamentations likewise says He drew His bow and fixed the speaker as a missile's target. Both make the sufferer the deliberately positioned object of an attack.

Job 16:13
They surrounded me with spears, striking my kidneys without sparing; they poured my gall onto the earth.
  Lamentations 3:13 — Shared image: missiles striking the kidneys: Job describes surrounding spears striking his kidneys and gall poured out. Lamentations says arrows from the divine quiver entered the speaker's kidneys. Both use the particular internal wound, retaining the different weapons and additional details.

Job 16:16
My belly is burned up from weeping, and a shadow lies upon my eyelids.
  Psalms 6:7-8 — Weeping and bodily distress: Job describes a belly burned from weeping and shadow on his eyelids. The psalm joins copious tears with an eye troubled by anger. Both connect grief with bodily and ocular distress, without changing Job's belly to a face or equating the causes of the eye symptoms.

Job 16:17
Yet there was nothing unjust in my hands, and my prayer is pure.
  Job 1:1 — Profession of integrity and the narrator's description: Job says his hands contain nothing unjust and his prayer is pure. The opening narrative calls him true, blameless, righteous and God-fearing, providing the stated narrative characterization against which his profession is heard.
  Proverbs 15:8 — Integrity and acceptable prayer: Job joins absence of injustice in his hands with purity of prayer. Proverbs contrasts the impious person's sacrifice with acceptable prayers of those going straight. Both relate conduct and prayer, while the proverb is not an independent ruling on every claim in Job's speech.

Job 16:18
Earth, do not cover the blood of my flesh, and let there be no place for my cry!
  Genesis 4:10-11 — Blood, earth and an unsilenced cry: Job asks earth not to cover his blood or give his cry a resting place. In Genesis, Abel's blood cries to God from the earth that received it. The distinctive combination of earth, blood and audible appeal supports the comparison without identifying Job with Abel.

Job 16:20
May my supplication reach the Lord, and may my eye drip tears before Him.
  Psalms 38:13 — Prayer accompanied by tears: Job asks that his supplication reach the Lord while his eye pours tears. The psalm similarly asks the Lord to hear supplication and not remain silent at tears. Both explicitly seek a hearing for tearful prayer.

Job 16:21
May there be a hearing for a man before the Lord, and may a son of man plead for his neighbor.
  Job 9:33 — Recurring request for a hearing between human and God: Job wishes for a hearing before the Lord and for a son of man to plead for a neighbor. Earlier he wished for a mediator to hear the cause between both sides. The link compares the desired intercession without naming the unspecified advocate.

Job 16:22
The numbered years have come, and I will travel a way on which I will not return.
  Job 7:9-10 — Recurring image: a journey without return: Job says his numbered years bring him to a way on which he will not return. His earlier account of going down to Hades likewise excludes return to one's house and former place. Both belong to his anticipation of death.

Job 17:1
“I perish, carried along by wind; I need burial and do not obtain it.
  Job 3:21-22 — Longed-for death and unavailable burial: Job says he is perishing and needs burial but cannot obtain it. His first lament described people longing for death without obtaining it and rejoicing if they do. Both portray desired release as unavailable, while death and burial are not treated as identical requests.

Job 17:3
Strangers stole my belongings. Who is this one? Let him bind himself to my hand.
  Job 1:14-17 — Complaint recalls stolen possessions: Job says strangers stole his belongings. The opening reports describe raiders carrying off oxen and donkeys and horsemen carrying off camels. These losses give narrative context to the complaint without assigning an ethnicity to the unnamed strangers.

Job 17:4
For Thou didst hide their heart from understanding; therefore Thou wilt certainly not exalt them.
  Deuteronomy 29:3 — Understanding withheld from the heart: Job says God hid their heart from understanding. Moses tells Israel that the Lord had not given a heart to know, eyes to see or ears to hear. Job 23:2

Job 23:6

Job 23:7

Job 23:9

Job 23:10

Job 23:11

Job 23:15a

Job 23:17

Job 24:3

Job 24:6

  Deuteronomy 24:14-15 — Labor without wages: Job explicitly describes powerless vineyard workers without pay or food. Deuteronomy forbids withholding the poor worker's wages and requires payment that day; the norm directly addresses the economic deprivation in the complaint.

Job 24:9

  Exodus 22:21-23 — Violence against orphans and the law's protection: Job describes an orphan snatched from the breast and the humbling of one who had fallen. Exodus prohibits mistreating orphans and warns that their cries will bring wrath; the comparison does not invent a debt-enslavement mechanism for the snatching.

  Exodus 3:7-9 — A cry of oppression and divine inspection: Job asks why the Lord has not inspected expelled people and groaning infants. Job 24:19

It is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises.
  Daniel 12:2 — resurrection expectation: Job's appended unit explicitly states that he will rise with those the Lord raises. Daniel likewise describes sleepers in the earth arising, while distinguishing their outcomes; the comparison does not erase those distinctions.
  John 5:28-29 — resurrection expectation: The appendix places Job among those the Lord will raise. Jesus describes those in tombs hearing His voice and coming out to resurrection, including differing judgments; both explicitly speak of the dead being raised.

Job 42:17b
This is translated from the Syriac book: he lived in the land of Ausitis, on the borders of Idumea and Arabia, and his former name was Jobab.
  Genesis 36:33 — appended identification and royal list: The appendix explicitly gives Job the former name Jobab and places him near Idumea. Genesis lists Jobab as an Edomite king. This records the appendix's asserted identification and the matching royal entry, not independent historical verification.

Job 42:17d
These are the kings who reigned in Edom, the land he himself also ruled: first Balak son of Beor, whose city was called Dennaba; after Balak, Jobab, called Job; after him, Asom, a ruler from the land of Teman; after him, Adad son of Barad, who cut down Midian in the plain of Moab, whose city was called Geththaim.
  Genesis 36:31-35 — parallel Edomite royal sequence: The appendix's sequence Balak, Jobab, Asom and Adad corresponds to Genesis' successive Edomite rulers, including the named fathers, cities and victory over Midian. The source's spellings and explicit Jobab-Job identification remain intact.
  1 Chronicles 1:43-46 — parallel Edomite royal sequence: Chronicles repeats the appendix's four-king sequence, with corresponding fathers, cities and the defeat of Midian in Moab. The texts retain their own name spellings; the connection concerns the detailed sequence rather than an invented harmonized genealogy.

Job 42:17e
The friends who came to him were Eliphaz, from the sons of Esau, king of the Temanites; Bildad, ruler of the Shuhites; and Zophar, king of the Minaeans.
  Job 2:11 — repeated names and roles of the friends: The appendix lists Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar with the same Temanite, Shuhite and Minaean royal roles as their arrival notice. The opening notice does not independently establish the appendix's additional Esau ancestry.
