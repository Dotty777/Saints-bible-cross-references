Daniel 1:1
In the third year of King Joakim of Judea, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon arrived at Jerusalem and besieged it.
  2 Chronicles 36:5-6 — historical narrative parallel: Daniel names Joakim and Chronicles names Jehoiakim in their accounts of Nebuchadnezzar's assault upon Judah's royal house. The named ruler, Babylonian attacker and Jerusalem setting connect the narratives, while their native names and regnal dates are retained.

Daniel 1:2
The Lord handed the city into his hands, together with Joakim king of Judea and a portion of the Lord’s sacred vessels. He carried them away to Babylon and deposited them in his idol-temple.
  2 Chronicles 36:7 — historical narrative parallel: Both accounts describe vessels from the Lord's house being carried to Babylon and placed in the Babylonian king's temple. This identifies the sacred objects' removal, not merely a general exile theme.

Daniel 1:3
The king told Abiesdri, his chief eunuch, to bring him some of the sons of Israel’s magnates, of the royal family and of the chosen men:
  Isaiah 39:7 — prophetic motif and historical parallel: Isaiah warns that royal descendants will be taken into the Babylonian king's household as eunuchs; Daniel describes the selection of royal and noble Israelite youths for that court. The parallel does not establish any individual youth's descent from Hezekiah.

Daniel 1:4
young men without blemish, handsome, knowledgeable in every kind of wisdom, literate, intelligent, wise, and strong enough to be in the king’s house, and to teach them letters and the Chaldean tongue,
  Daniel 1:17 — same-chapter requirement and outcome: The king requires literate youths able to learn letters and the Chaldean language. The later account attributes the four youths' knowledge, understanding and literary skill to God, connecting the required abilities with their divine provision.
  Acts 7:21-22 — narrative parallel: The youths are to learn letters and the Chaldean language for royal service; Moses, brought up by Pharaoh's daughter, learns Egyptian wisdom. Both passages describe an Israelite figure's education within a foreign royal setting.

Daniel 1:5
and to give them a ration from the king’s house each day, from the royal table and the wine the king drank, and to educate them for three years and place some of them before the king.
  Daniel 1:18-20 — same-chapter arrangement and outcome: The king prescribes food and a period of education before court service. The later presentation and examination of the youths enact that arrangement and report its result.

Daniel 1:6
Among the lineage of Israel’s sons from Judea were Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah.
  Daniel 2:17 — character continuity: Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah are introduced together among the Judean youths; Daniel later returns to those same named companions to share the court's crisis.

Daniel 1:7
The chief eunuch assigned them names: Daniel, Baltasar; Hananiah, Sedrach; Mishael, Misach; and Azariah, Abdenago.
  Daniel 2:26 — name identification: The naming of Daniel as Baltasar is explicitly recalled when the king addresses Daniel, whose name is Baltasar. This concerns Daniel's court name, not the later king of that name.
  Daniel 3:12 — name identification and narrative continuity: The three companions receive the court names subsequently used in the accusation against the Jewish officials who refuse the image. The names link the renamed youths to the furnace narrative.

Daniel 1:8
Daniel considered in his heart how he might avoid being polluted by the king’s dinner and the wine he drank, and petitioned the chief eunuch that he might not be contaminated.
  Daniel 1:12-16 — same-chapter resolve and implementation: Daniel resolves to avoid the royal food and wine; the proposed vegetable-and-water trial and the subsequent replacement of those provisions show how that resolve is carried out.

Daniel 1:9
The Lord gave Daniel honor and favor before the chief eunuch.
  Genesis 39:2-4 — narrative parallel: God gives Daniel favor with the eunuch in a foreign household. Joseph likewise prospers through the Lord's presence and finds favor with his Egyptian master; both accounts connect divine help with favor under foreign authority.

Daniel 1:10
The chief eunuch said to Daniel, “I am anxious about my lord the king, who prescribed your food and drink, lest he see your faces drawn and weak beside the foreign youths being reared with you, and I risk my own neck.”
  Daniel 1:15 — same-chapter concern and outcome: The eunuch fears that the youths will look worse than their peers and endanger him. Their healthier appearance after the trial answers that specific concern.

Daniel 1:11
Daniel said to Abiesdri, appointed chief eunuch over Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah,
  Daniel 1:19 — same persons: Both verses name the same four young men in the same order, 'Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah': here as those under the appointed chief eunuch, and there as those found to have no equal among the wise after their test.

Daniel 1:12
“Test your servants for ten days, and let us be given legumes from the earth to eat, and water to drink.
  Daniel 1:15 — same-chapter proposal and result: Daniel proposes ten days of vegetables and water; the later examination after ten days reports the youths' better appearance. The stated trial and its measured outcome correspond.

Daniel 1:13
If our appearance looks drawn beside the other young men eating the royal dinner, then deal with your servants however you wish.”
  Daniel 1:15 — same-chapter evaluation and result: Daniel asks that their appearance be compared with the youths eating royal provisions. The later comparison reports precisely the favorable appearance on which his proposal depends.

Daniel 1:14
He dealt with them in this manner and tested them for ten days.
  Revelation 2:10 — verbal echo: Both texts speak of being 'tested' for 'ten days': Daniel and his companions are tested for ten days, while in Revelation 2:10 believers are told they will be thrown into prison 'that ye may be tested, and ye shall have affliction for ten days.'

Daniel 1:15
After ten days their appearance was good and their bodily condition better than the other young men eating the royal dinner.
  Daniel 1:12-13 — same-chapter trial and outcome: The youths' better appearance after ten days answers Daniel's proposal to test their diet for that period and compare their appearance with those receiving royal provisions.

Daniel 1:16
Abiesdri kept removing their dinner and wine and giving them legumes in exchange.
  Daniel 1:8-12 — same-chapter resolution: The continuing substitution of vegetables for the appointed food and wine implements Daniel's earlier resolve to avoid defiling provisions and his request for an alternative diet.

Daniel 1:17
The Lord gave the young men knowledge, understanding, and practical wisdom in every literary skill; to Daniel He gave understanding in every utterance, vision, dreams, and all wisdom.
  Genesis 40:8 — divine source of interpretation: God gives Daniel understanding of visions and dreams; Joseph similarly says that the explanation of dreams comes through God. Both passages locate interpretive ability in divine help.
  Daniel 2:19-23 — gift and later exercise: The gift of understanding visions and dreams is exercised when the mystery is revealed to Daniel in a night vision. His thanksgiving then credits God with the wisdom and answer received.

Daniel 1:18
After these days the king ordered them brought in, and the chief eunuch brought them before King Nebuchadnezzar.
  Daniel 1:5 — same-chapter arrangement fulfilled: The presentation of the youths after the appointed days returns to the king's earlier requirement that they be educated before entering his service.

Daniel 1:19
The king conversed with them, and among the wise no one was found like Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. They remained beside the king.
  Daniel 1:17 — same-chapter gift and recognition: The king finds none comparable to the four youths and places them in his service; the preceding account has attributed their learning and wisdom to God's gift. The same four recipients are subsequently recognized at court.

Daniel 1:20
In every matter of discourse, understanding, and education about which the king questioned them, he found them ten times wiser than the learned experts and philosophers throughout his kingdom. The king honored them, appointed them rulers, and declared them wiser than all his own men in affairs throughout his land and kingdom.
  Daniel 5:11-12 — retrospective character testimony: Daniel and his companions surpass the learned experts in the king's examination. The queen later recalls Daniel as wiser than Babylon's wise men and as an interpreter for Nebuchadnezzar, continuing that account of his exceptional understanding.

Daniel 1:21
Daniel remained until the first year of the reign of Cyrus king of the Persians.
  Ezra 1:1-3 — shared historical setting: Daniel's continuation until Cyrus's first year meets the same royal-year setting in which Ezra records Cyrus's proclamation concerning Jerusalem. The comparison identifies the setting without adding an unreported action by Daniel.

Daniel 2:1

  Genesis 41:1-8 — royal dream narrative parallel: Nebuchadnezzar's dream disturbs his sleep; Pharaoh likewise dreams and is troubled when morning comes, then seeks interpretation. Both court narratives begin with a ruler troubled by a dream.

Daniel 2:2

  Genesis 41:8 — royal consultation parallel: Nebuchadnezzar summons the court's specialists over his dream, as Pharaoh summons Egypt's interpreters and wise men. Both accounts distinguish that consultation from the later divinely enabled interpretation.

Daniel 2:3

  Genesis 41:8 — royal dream narrative parallel: The king declares his disturbance and desire to understand the dream. Pharaoh's troubled soul and unsuccessful consultation describe the corresponding difficulty in the Joseph account.

Daniel 2:4

  Daniel 2:7 — same-chapter repeated petition: The specialists ask the king to tell the dream so that they can interpret it; they repeat that request after his demand for disclosure. The repetition makes their dependence on receiving the dream explicit.

Daniel 2:5

  Daniel 2:12-13 — same-chapter threat and decree: The king threatens the specialists if they cannot disclose the dream and its interpretation. The later order to destroy Babylon's wise men turns that threat into the crisis affecting Daniel and his companions.

Daniel 2:6

  Daniel 2:48 — same-chapter promise and fulfillment: The king promises rewards and honor for disclosing the dream and its interpretation; Daniel later receives gifts and authority after doing so.

Daniel 2:7

  Daniel 2:4 — same-chapter repeated petition: The renewed request that the king first tell the dream repeats the specialists' original condition for giving an interpretation, emphasizing the unresolved demand between them and the king.

Daniel 2:8

  Daniel 2:16-19 — same-chapter contrast: The king accuses the specialists of seeking delay. Daniel also asks for time, but then calls for fasting, supplication and divine help concerning the mystery before receiving its disclosure in a night vision.

Daniel 2:9

  Daniel 2:10-11 — same-chapter disputed demand: The king demands disclosure of the dream as evidence that its interpretation can be trusted; the specialists answer that no human can meet the demand and place disclosure beyond mortals. Their reply exposes the difficulty behind the threatened judgment.

Daniel 2:10

  Daniel 2:27-28 — same-chapter human inability and divine disclosure: The specialists deny that a human can disclose the king's matter. Daniel likewise denies that the court's experts can reveal it, but points to God in heaven as the revealer of mysteries.

Daniel 2:11

  Daniel 2:27-28 — same-chapter contrast: The specialists say the matter exceeds human ability and refer to an angel whose dwelling is not with flesh. Daniel's answer instead directs the king to God in heaven who reveals mysteries; the two statements are compared without identifying the angel with God.

Daniel 2:12

  Daniel 2:24 — same-chapter reversal: The king orders the wise men brought out, followed immediately by the death decree in 2:13. Daniel later tells Arioch not to destroy them, interrupting that process by offering disclosure of the mystery.

Daniel 2:16

  Daniel 2:8-9 — same-chapter contrast: Daniel requests time before giving the interpretation; the earlier exchange shows the king accusing the specialists of buying time. The juxtaposition contrasts Daniel's request with the delay already suspected at court.

Daniel 2:17

  Daniel 1:6 — character continuity: The companions to whom Daniel reports the matter are the same Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah introduced with him among the Judean youths.

Daniel 2:18

  Daniel 2:19-23 — same-chapter petition and answer: Daniel calls for fasting, supplication and help from the Lord Most High concerning the mystery and the threatened lives of himself and his companions. The revelation and his subsequent thanksgiving record the answer to his request.

Daniel 2:19

  Genesis 40:8 — divine source of interpretation: Daniel receives the mystery in a night vision and blesses God; Joseph similarly says that dream explanations come through God. The connection concerns the divine origin of the answer.

Daniel 2:20

  Job 12:13 — shared confession of divine wisdom: Daniel blesses the Lord as the possessor of wisdom and majesty; Job assigns wisdom, power, counsel and understanding to God. Both place wisdom in God as the source of understanding, while their distinct descriptions of divine greatness are retained.

Daniel 2:21

Daniel 2:22

  Psalms 138:11-12 — divine knowledge beyond darkness: Daniel's confession that God knows what is in darkness corresponds to the psalm's declaration that darkness does not hide from God. This uses the explicit darkness and knowledge clauses, without rewriting Daniel's final dwelling phrase.

Daniel 2:23
To Thee, Lord of my fathers, I give thanks and praise, because Thou hast given me wisdom and prudence. Now Thou hast shown me everything I requested, to disclose these things to the king.”
  Daniel 2:18-19 — same-chapter thanksgiving for an answered request: Daniel thanks God for making known everything he requested. His earlier call for fasting, supplication and help, followed by the night revelation, identifies the request and its answer.

Daniel 2:24

  Daniel 2:12-13 — same-chapter reversal: Daniel's command not to destroy the wise men directly interrupts the royal decree that had ordered their destruction and threatened Daniel and his companions.

  Daniel 2:14 — same-chapter change of role: Arioch now brings the Judean captive who can disclose the mystery before the king. He had first appeared as the chief cook ordered to bring out Babylon's learned men; the same official moves from enforcing the crisis to presenting its answer.

Daniel 2:26
The king answered Daniel, who was called Baltasar in Chaldean, “Will you be able to disclose to me the vision I saw and its interpretation?”
  Daniel 1:7 — name identification: The king addresses Daniel as the one named Baltasar, explicitly recalling the court name given when the youths were renamed.

Daniel 2:29

Daniel 2:31

Daniel 2:32

  Daniel 2:37-39 — same-chapter symbolic interpretation: The statue's gold head, silver chest and arms, and bronze lower body are explained as the king and succeeding kingdoms. The explanation follows the same sequence of materials.

Daniel 2:33
its legs iron, and its feet partly iron and partly earthenware.
  Daniel 2:40-43 — same-chapter symbolic interpretation: The iron legs and mixed iron-and-clay feet are interpreted through subduing strength and a divided, imperfectly joined kingdom. The target explains those particular materials rather than assigning unnamed nations to them.

Daniel 2:34

  Daniel 2:44-45 — same-chapter symbolic interpretation: The stone cut without hands strikes the statue's feet; Daniel later relates that stone to the kingdom God establishes and the destruction of the other kingdoms.

Daniel 2:35

  Daniel 2:44-45 — same-chapter vision and interpretation: The shattered statue and the stone becoming a mountain that strikes the earth are explained through God's enduring kingdom bringing the other kingdoms to an end. The source's wording about striking the earth is retained.

Daniel 2:36

  Daniel 2:26 — same event: The king asked Daniel whether he could 'disclose to me the vision I saw and its interpretation', and this verse announces that, having stated 'the vision', Daniel will now give 'its interpretation'.
  Daniel 2:45 — verbal echo: This verse opens the interpretation of 'the vision', and the closing verse of that interpretation affirms, 'The vision is exact, and its interpretation trustworthy.'

Daniel 2:37

Daniel 2:38

Daniel 2:39

Daniel 2:41

  Daniel 2:33 — same-chapter symbolic interpretation: The divided kingdom and the mixture of pottery and iron explain the statue's feet, which were described as partly iron and partly clay.

Daniel 2:42

Daniel 2:43

  Daniel 2:33 — same-chapter symbolic interpretation: The failure of the human mixture to hold together explains the iron and clay in the statue's feet. The interpretation retains the vision's two incompatible materials.

Daniel 2:44

Daniel 2:45

Daniel 3:1

Daniel 3:2
