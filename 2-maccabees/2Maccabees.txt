2 Maccabees 1:1
To the brothers, the Jews throughout Egypt: greetings from the brothers, the Jews in Jerusalem and in the land of Judea, and good peace.
  2 Maccabees 1:10 — parallel greeting: Both letters are sent from the Jews in Jerusalem and Judea to the Jews in Egypt with a greeting. The second letter also names the council of elders, Judas and Aristobulus.

2 Maccabees 1:2
May God do good to you and remember His covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, His faithful servants.
  Exodus 2:24 — Covenant recollection: The prayer that God remember His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob repeats the three ancestors and divine remembering at the beginning of Israel's rescue from Egypt.

2 Maccabees 1:3
May He give you all a heart to revere Him and do His wishes with a generous heart and a willing soul.
  Deuteronomy 30:6-8 — Heart and obedience: The request for a heart willing to revere God and do His wishes resembles the promised divine cleansing of the heart followed by obedience to His commandments.

2 Maccabees 1:4
May He open your heart to His law and His ordinances, and make peace.
  Deuteronomy 30:6-10 — Heart and law: The prayer for an opened heart toward God's law corresponds to Deuteronomy's connection between divine heart renewal and keeping the written commandments.

2 Maccabees 1:5
May He hear your petitions, be reconciled to you, and not abandon you in an evil time.
  1 Kings 8:49-50 — Prayer for scattered Israel: The request that God hear and be reconciled to the recipients resembles Solomon's petition that God hear His displaced people from heaven and be merciful to their wrongs.

2 Maccabees 1:7
In the reign of Demetrius, in the one hundred sixty-ninth year, we Jews wrote to you in the affliction and the crisis that came upon us in those years, from the time Jason and those with him revolted from the holy land and the kingdom.
  2 Maccabees 4:7-13 — Crisis recalled: The letter traces the crisis to Jason and his associates; the narrative describes Jason's acquisition of the priesthood and his replacement of lawful civic life with Greek customs.

2 Maccabees 1:8
They burned the gateway and poured out innocent blood. We petitioned the Lord and were heard; we offered sacrifice and fine flour, lit the lamps, and set out the loaves.
  2 Maccabees 10:3 — Worship restored: The letter's sacrifices, lamps, and loaves correspond to the specific acts of restored temple worship narrated after the sanctuary's purification.
  1 Maccabees 4:50-51 — Restoration parallel: Both accounts specifically include relighting the lamps and returning the loaves to their proper place in the restored temple.

2 Maccabees 1:9
And now, see that you observe the days of the Festival of Booths in the month of Chislev. In the one hundred eighty-eighth year.
  2 Maccabees 10:5-8 — Festival invitation explained: The invitation to observe Booths in Chislev concerns the purification celebration described as eight days in the manner of Booths and decreed for annual observance.

2 Maccabees 1:10
Those in Jerusalem and those in Judea, the council of elders and Judas, to Aristobulus, teacher of King Ptolemy and a member of the family of the anointed priests, and to the Jews in Egypt: greetings and good health.
  2 Maccabees 1:1 — parallel greeting: Both letters are addressed from those in Jerusalem and Judea to the Jews in Egypt with greetings. This letter adds the council of elders, Judas, and Aristobulus, teacher of King Ptolemy.

2 Maccabees 1:12
for He Himself drove out those arrayed against us in the holy city.
  Psalms 47:4-9 — Thematic comparison of divine protection of the holy city: The letter credits God with driving out opponents arrayed against the holy city. The psalm describes God supporting His city while assembled kings are troubled and shaken. Both locate the city's security in God's action against threatening rulers; the psalm is not identified as a prediction or account of this particular expulsion.

2 Maccabees 1:13
For when the ruler came to Persia with his force, which seemed irresistible, they were cut down in the temple of Nanea, the priests of Nanea having used a deception.
  2 Maccabees 9:1-2 — related account: Both describe the ruler going into Persia and his men being defeated at a temple there. Here they are cut down in the temple of Nanea through the priests' deception; in 2 Maccabees 9:1-2 Antiochus attempts to rob the temple at Persepolis, his men are routed, and he retreats alive.

2 Maccabees 1:18
As we are about to observe the purification of the temple on the twenty-fifth of Chislev, we thought it necessary to explain this to you, so that you yourselves may observe the festival of booths and of the fire, when Nehemiah, who rebuilt the temple and the altar, offered sacrifices.
  2 Maccabees 10:5-8 — Purification anniversary: The proposed observance on the twenty-fifth of Chislev matches the later narrative's purification date and annual festival decree; the Nehemiah fire tradition is additional material in the letter.

2 Maccabees 1:19
For when our fathers were being taken to Persia, the devout priests of that time secretly took some of the altar’s fire and hid it in the hollow of a well-like place without water. They secured it there so that the place was unknown to everyone.
  2 Maccabees 2:1 — Earlier account recalled: The following chapter expressly refers back to the preservation of altar fire at the deportation, adding Jeremiah's instruction to take it.

2 Maccabees 1:20
After many years had passed, when it pleased God, Nehemiah was sent by the king of Persia. He sent the descendants of the priests who had hidden it to fetch the fire. When they reported to us that they had found no fire but thick water, he ordered them to draw some and bring it.
  Nehemiah 2:5-8 — Royal commission compared: Nehemiah's being sent by the Persian king corresponds to his commission to return to Judah and rebuild; Nehemiah's own account does not narrate recovery of thick water or altar fire.

2 Maccabees 1:21
When the sacrificial materials had been brought up, Nehemiah ordered the priests to sprinkle the water on the wood and on what lay upon it.
  1 Kings 18:33-38 — Water and altar fire compared: Sprinkling water on the offering and wood before a remarkable fire resembles Elijah's drenched offering consumed by divine fire; the liquids and narrative explanations differ.

2 Maccabees 1:22
When this was done and some time had passed, the sun, previously hidden by clouds, shone out. A great fire was kindled, so that everyone marveled.
  1 Kings 18:38-39 — Altar fire compared: The unexpected ignition and the spectators' amazement resemble the fire consuming Elijah's wet offering and the people's recognition of God, without equating the two events.

2 Maccabees 1:23
While the sacrifice was being consumed, the priests offered prayer—the priests and everyone—with Jonathan beginning and the others responding, as Nehemiah did.
  Nehemiah 8:6 — Liturgical parallel of a leader and answering assembly: Jonathan begins the prayer while the priests and others respond during the sacrifice; Esdras blesses the Lord and all the people answer Amen. Both depict public prayer with a leader and a responding congregation. The occasions remain distinct; Maccabees does not specify an Amen response, and Jonathan is not identified with Esdras.

2 Maccabees 1:24
The prayer was in this form: “Lord, Lord God, Creator of all, fearsome, strong, righteous and merciful, the only King and good,
  Nehemiah 9:32 — verbal echo: Both prayers address God with the titles 'strong' and 'fearsome': Nehemiah 9:32 calls Him 'the mighty, great, strong and fearsome One', and the source 'fearsome, strong, righteous and merciful'.
  Daniel 9:4 — verbal echo: Daniel prays to the 'Lord God' and addresses Him as 'the great, strong, and fearsome God'; the prayer here likewise addresses the 'Lord God' as 'fearsome, strong'.

2 Maccabees 1:26
accept the sacrifice on behalf of all Thy people Israel. Protect Thy portion and make it holy.
  1 Kings 8:51-53 — Chosen portion in prayer: Calling Israel God's portion and asking its sanctification resembles Solomon's prayer identifying Israel as God's people and inheritance set apart from the nations.

2 Maccabees 1:27
Gather our scattered people together. Free those enslaved among the nations. Look upon the despised and abhorred, and let the nations know that Thou art our God.
  Deuteronomy 30:3-5 — Gathering promise invoked: The request to gather the scattered people and release those among the nations corresponds to the law's promise of mercy, gathering, and return to the inherited land.
  Nehemiah 1:8-10 — Gathering prayer compared: The plea for the scattered people to be gathered resembles Nehemiah's prayer invoking Moses' promise that even the farthest dispersed will return to God's chosen place.

2 Maccabees 1:29
Plant Thy people in Thy holy place, as Moses said.”
  Exodus 15:17 — Explicit Mosaic recollection: The request to plant the people in the holy place expressly invokes Moses and corresponds to his song asking God to plant His people on the mountain of His inheritance.

2 Maccabees 1:30
The priests sang the hymns in response.
  Ezra 3:10-11 — Responsive praise at the temple: The priests sing hymns in response after the sacrificial prayer; at the foundation of the Lord's house, priests and Levites participate in answering praise and thanksgiving. The shared practice is responsive temple praise, without identifying the unnamed hymns in Maccabees as Ezra's quoted refrain or equating the ceremonies.

2 Maccabees 1:31
When the sacrificial materials had been consumed, Nehemiah ordered that the remaining water also be poured over larger stones.
  2 Maccabees 1:21 — same event: The 'remaining water' is the water Nehemiah had ordered the priests to sprinkle on the wood and the sacrificial materials in 2 Maccabees 1:21. Both verses describe Nehemiah ordering the use of this water at the same sacrifice.

2 Maccabees 1:33
When the matter became known, the king of the Persians was informed that water had appeared in the place where the deported priests had hidden the fire, and that Nehemiah and his companions had purified the sacrificial materials with it.
  2 Maccabees 1:19-22 — Report summarizes event; same-chapter: The report to the Persian king expressly recapitulates the hidden fire, recovered water, and purification described earlier in the letter.

2 Maccabees 2:1
In the records it is found that Jeremiah the prophet ordered those who were being deported to take some of the fire, as has been indicated,
  2 Maccabees 1:19 — Explicit backward reference: The words as has been indicated refer back to the deported priests' preservation of altar fire in the preceding account.

2 Maccabees 2:2
and that the prophet, after giving the law to those being deported, commanded them not to forget the Lord’s ordinances, nor to go astray in their thoughts when they saw gold and silver statues and the adornment surrounding them.
  Deuteronomy 4:9-19 — Law against forgetfulness and images: Jeremiah's reported warning not to forget the ordinances or be led astray by images parallels Moses' linked warnings against forgetting what was taught and making or worshiping visible likenesses.

2 Maccabees 2:3
Saying these and other such things, he urged them not to let the law depart from their heart.
  Deuteronomy 4:9 — Law retained in the heart: The instruction not to let the law depart from the heart resembles Moses' warning that the remembered revelation must not depart from the heart throughout life.

2 Maccabees 2:4
It was also in the writing that, after receiving an oracle, the prophet ordered the tent and the ark to accompany him, as he went out to the mountain where Moses had gone up and viewed God’s inheritance.
  Deuteronomy 34:1-4 — Explicit geographical recollection: The mountain where Moses viewed the inheritance is the setting of Moses' ascent and the Lord's display of the promised land; Deuteronomy does not recount Jeremiah's visit.

2 Maccabees 2:7
When Jeremiah learned this, he rebuked them and said, “The place will remain unknown until God gathers His people together and becomes merciful.
  Deuteronomy 30:3-5 — Gathering and mercy: Jeremiah's reported expectation that God gather His people and become merciful uses the combination of mercy and gathering promised in the law.

2 Maccabees 2:8
Then the Lord will reveal these things, and the Lord’s glory and the cloud will appear, as they were shown in the time of Moses, and as Solomon also prayed that the place might be greatly sanctified.”
  Exodus 40:34-35 — Explicit glory-cloud recollection: The expected glory and cloud expressly recall Moses' time, when the cloud covered the tent and God's glory filled it.
  2 Chronicles 5:13-14 — Explicit temple-cloud recollection: The reference to Solomon's sanctuary is illuminated by the dedication account in which a cloud of the Lord's glory fills the temple and prevents priestly service.

2 Maccabees 2:9
It was also explained how, being wise, Solomon offered a sacrifice for the dedication and completion of the temple.
  2 Chronicles 7:4-5 — Explicit dedication recollection: Solomon's dedication sacrifices are narrated with the offerings of the king and people and the dedication of God's house.

2 Maccabees 2:10
Just as Moses prayed to the Lord, and fire came down from heaven and consumed the sacrificial materials, so Solomon also prayed, and the fire came down and consumed the whole burnt offerings.
  Leviticus 9:23-24 — Explicit sacrificial-fire recollection: The recalled fire in Moses' time corresponds to Moses and Aaron's appearance before the people and the fire from the Lord consuming the altar offerings; Leviticus does not quote the prayer mentioned here.
  2 Chronicles 7:1 — Explicit sacrificial-fire recollection: Solomon finishes praying and fire descends from heaven to consume the offerings, the precise sequence recalled here.

2 Maccabees 2:11
Moses said, “Because the sin offering was not eaten, it was consumed.”
  Leviticus 10:16-18 — Mosaic episode paraphrased: The statement about the uneaten sin offering recalls Moses' discovery that the goat had been burned and his question why the priests had not eaten it; it is a compressed recollection of that exchange.

2 Maccabees 2:12
In the same way Solomon also observed the eight days.
  2 Chronicles 7:8-10 — Dedication duration compared: The recollection of Solomon's eight days corresponds to the seven-day feast with its eighth-day closing celebration; the target preserves the separate day and dismissal notices.

2 Maccabees 2:13
These same things were related in the records and in the memoirs concerning Nehemiah, and also how, in establishing a library, he gathered together the books concerning the kings and prophets, the writings of David, and kings’ letters concerning dedicated gifts.
  Ezra 6:1-5 — Archival preservation of royal provisions for sacred gifts: Nehemiah's reported library includes kings' letters about dedicated gifts. Ezra describes a treasury-library search and a preserved memorandum of Cyrus's provisions for rebuilding the Jerusalem house and returning its gold and silver vessels. Both associate collected written royal records with sacred endowments; Ezra's memorandum is not asserted to be one of Nehemiah's particular letters or in the same archive.

2 Maccabees 2:14
In the same way Judas also gathered all the writings that had been scattered because of the war that befell us, and they are with us.
  1 Maccabees 1:56-57 — Preservation after persecution: Judas's recovery of scattered writings answers the documented crisis in which books of the law were destroyed and possession of the covenant book was punished; scattering and burning are not identical losses.

2 Maccabees 2:16
As we are about to observe the purification, we have written to you. You will do well, then, to observe the days.
  2 Maccabees 1:18 — Festival invitation repeated: The renewed invitation to observe purification repeats the letter's earlier announcement of the twenty-fifth-of-Chislev observance.

2 Maccabees 2:17
Now God, who saved all His people and restored to all the inheritance, the kingship, the priesthood and the sanctification,
  Deuteronomy 30:3-5 — Restored inheritance: The statement that God restored the people's inheritance corresponds to the law's promise to gather them and return them to the land inherited by their fathers; this target does not separately specify restored kingship or priesthood.

2 Maccabees 2:18
as He promised through the law—for we hope in God that He will soon have mercy on us and gather us from under heaven into the holy place, for He rescued us from great evils and purified the place.
  Deuteronomy 30:3-5 — Explicit legal promise: The hope of gathering from under heaven to the holy place expressly appeals to the law and closely follows its promise to gather even those dispersed to heaven's farthest end.

2 Maccabees 2:19
The events concerning Judas Maccabeus and his brothers, the purification of the greatest temple and the dedication of the altar,
  2 Maccabees 10:1-3 — Narrative program fulfilled: The announced topic of Judas's purification of the temple and dedication of the altar is carried out in the subsequent account of recovery, cleansing, and a new altar.

2 Maccabees 2:20
and also the wars against Antiochus Epiphanes and his son Eupator,
  2 Maccabees 10:9-11 — Narrative program organized: The introductory naming of Epiphanes and Eupator corresponds to the later formal transition from Epiphanes' death to the wars under his son Eupator.

2 Maccabees 2:21
and the manifestations from heaven to those who zealously performed brave deeds for Judaism, so that, although few, they plundered the whole land and pursued the barbarian multitudes,
  2 Maccabees 10:29-30 — Manifestations anticipated: The introduction's heavenly manifestations are exemplified by the five mounted figures protecting Maccabeus and striking the enemy during battle.

2 Maccabees 2:22
recovered the temple renowned throughout the inhabited world, freed the city, and restored the laws that were about to be abolished, the Lord having become merciful to them with all gentleness—
  2 Maccabees 11:24-25 — Restored laws documented: The introductory claim that the ancestral laws were restored is supported by Eupator's later letter permitting ancestral customs and restoring the temple to the Jews.

2 Maccabees 2:23
these things, set forth by Jason of Cyrene in five books, we shall attempt to abridge into one composition.
  2 Maccabees 15:39 — same composition: The preface announces the abridgement of Jason's five books 'into one composition', and 2 Maccabees 15:39 closes that same composition: 'the composition. Here shall be the end.' The two passages frame the abridger's work.

2 Maccabees 2:25
we have taken thought for the pleasure of those who wish to read, for the ease of those eager to commit it to memory, and for the benefit of everyone who encounters it.
  2 Maccabees 15:38-39 — Authorial purpose and conclusion: The author's intention to benefit and delight readers is revisited in the closing assessment of the composition and its comparison of pleasing prose to mixed wine.

2 Maccabees 2:26
For us who have undertaken the hard work of abridgement, the task is not easy; it involves sweat and sleeplessness.
  Ecclesiastes 12:12 — Literary reflection on the bodily cost of study and writing: The abridger describes the work of producing the condensed history as sweat and sleeplessness. Ecclesiastes links the making of many books and much study with weariness of the flesh. Both explicitly connect literary work with bodily strain, while the abridger willingly undertakes the task and Ecclesiastes issues a caution.

2 Maccabees 2:29
For just as the architect of a new house must consider its whole foundation, while the person undertaking its encaustic work and painting must examine what is suitable for decoration, so, I think, it is with us.
  1 Corinthians 3:10-11 — Comparison of building metaphors for different responsibilities: The abridger distinguishes an architect's concern for the whole foundation from the decorator's work to explain historian and abridger roles. Paul distinguishes laying a foundation as a master builder from another person's building upon it. Both use construction roles to distinguish responsibilities; Paul's foundation is explicitly Jesus Christ, and the literary historian is not equated with that foundation.

2 Maccabees 2:31
But the person making a recasting should be permitted to pursue concise expression and set aside an exhaustive treatment of the subject.
  2 Maccabees 15:38 — Abridger's standard recalled: The stated pursuit of concise expression corresponds to the closing wish that the work be well composed and to the point.

2 Maccabees 3:1
While the holy city was inhabited in complete peace and the laws were being kept most carefully because of the high priest Onias’s piety and hatred of wickedness,
  2 Maccabees 4:1-6 — Peace disrupted: The initial peace under Onias is broken by Simon's continuing accusations and violent rivalry, which lead Onias to seek royal intervention for public peace.

2 Maccabees 3:2
even the kings themselves honoured the place and glorified the temple with their finest gifts,
  2 Maccabees 5:16 — Dedicated gifts plundered: The royal gifts honouring the temple are later explicitly identified as objects Antiochus removes with profane hands.

2 Maccabees 3:3
so that Seleucus, king of Asia, also supplied from his own revenues all the expenses required for the service of the sacrifices.
  Ezra 6:8-10 — Royal sacrifice funding compared: Seleucus's payment of sacrificial expenses resembles the Persian decree supplying temple costs and daily sacrificial materials from royal revenues; these are distinct kings and grants.

2 Maccabees 3:4
But a certain Simon, of the tribe of Benjamin, who had been appointed overseer of the temple, disagreed with the high priest over the administration of the city’s market.
  2 Maccabees 4:1-3 — Informer's hostility continued: Simon's dispute with Onias develops into the next chapter's accusations and killings, explicitly attributed to the same informer and his supporters.

2 Maccabees 3:5
Unable to overcome Onias, he went to Apollonius son of Thraseas, who at that time was governor of Coele-Syria and Phoenicia.
  2 Maccabees 4:4 — same person: Both name Apollonius as governor of Coele-Syria and Phoenicia in the same rivalry between Simon and Onias: Simon goes to him here, and in 2 Maccabees 4:4 he is increasing Simon's wickedness. The patronymic differs (son of Thraseas here, son of Menestheus there).

2 Maccabees 3:6
He reported that the treasury in Jerusalem was full of indescribable riches, so that the amount of money was beyond counting, and that it did not belong to the account for the sacrifices; these funds, he said, could fall under the king’s authority.
  2 Maccabees 3:10-12 — Accusation corrected; same-chapter: Simon's claim that the funds are available for royal seizure is answered by Onias's identification of protected private deposits and rejection of confiscation.

2 Maccabees 3:7
Apollonius met the king and informed him about the money reported to him. The king chose Heliodorus, his chief minister, and sent him with orders to remove that money.
  2 Maccabees 5:18 — Mission explicitly recalled: The later narrator explicitly remembers Heliodorus as Seleucus's agent sent to inspect the treasury, the mission initiated here.

2 Maccabees 3:8
Heliodorus immediately began his journey, ostensibly to inspect the cities of Coele-Syria and Phoenicia, but actually to carry out the king’s intention.
  2 Maccabees 5:18 — same event: 2 Maccabees 5:18 recalls this mission: Heliodorus, whom King Seleucus sent to inspect the treasury. Here he sets out ostensibly to inspect the cities but actually to carry out the king's intention.

2 Maccabees 3:10
The high priest explained that they were deposits belonging to widows and orphans,
  Exodus 22:21-23 — Protection of widows and orphans: Onias's identification of funds belonging to widows and orphans places the threatened seizure against the law's express protection of those groups and assurance that God hears their cry.

2 Maccabees 3:11
and that some also belonged to Hyrcanus son of Tobias, a man of very high standing. Thus the impious Simon had misrepresented matters. In all there were four hundred talents of silver and two hundred of gold.
  2 Maccabees 3:6 — Treasury claim corrected; same-chapter: The named private owner and counted deposits correct Simon's report of uncountable funds available to the king; the text itself calls his report a misrepresentation.

2 Maccabees 3:12
It was utterly impossible, he said, that those who had trusted in the holiness of the place and in the dignity and inviolability of the temple honoured throughout the whole world should suffer injustice.
  Leviticus 5:21-24 — Entrusted property protected: Onias's refusal to allow injustice to depositors accords with the law condemning deceit concerning deposits and requiring entrusted property to be returned.

2 Maccabees 3:13
But Heliodorus, because of his royal instructions, insisted that the money must certainly be taken into the royal treasury.
  2 Maccabees 5:18 — same event: 2 Maccabees 5:18 refers back to Heliodorus, whom King Seleucus sent concerning the treasury, and who was scourged and thrown back. Here Heliodorus insists that the money be taken into the royal treasury.

2 Maccabees 3:14
He appointed a day and went in to arrange the inspection of these things. There was no small anguish throughout the city.
  2 Maccabees 3:30 — Anguish turned to joy; same-chapter: The city's anguish at the planned treasury seizure is explicitly reversed when the narrator describes the formerly fearful temple filled with gladness after divine intervention.

2 Maccabees 3:15
The priests threw themselves down before the altar in their priestly robes, calling to heaven upon the One who had legislated about deposits, that He would keep them safe for those who had entrusted them.
  Exodus 22:6-8 — Explicit deposit-law appeal: The priests invoke the One who legislated concerning deposits; Exodus supplies laws governing entrusted money, property, dishonesty, and restitution.
  Leviticus 5:21-24 — Explicit deposit-law appeal: Leviticus likewise specifically forbids wrongdoing concerning a deposit and requires its return, supplying legal substance for the priests' prayer for depositors.

2 Maccabees 3:16
Whoever saw the high priest’s appearance was wounded in mind, for his face and altered complexion revealed his soul’s anguish.
  Nehemiah 2:2-3 — Visible facial distress revealing inner sorrow: Onias's changed face reveals the anguish of his soul to observers. The king reads the speaker's distressed face as distress of heart, and the speaker explains his grief over the devastated city. Both make facial appearance an outward sign of inward sorrow; the threatened temple deposits and the burned city gates are different causes.

2 Maccabees 3:18
People also rushed out of their houses in crowds for a public supplication, because the place was about to be treated with contempt.
  Joel 2:15-17 — Public supplication against communal disgrace: People leave their houses for public supplication because the sanctuary is threatened with contempt, while the priests plead in the surrounding scene. Joel calls the congregation together and has the priests ask that God's inheritance not be given to reproach or foreign rule. Both present communal assembly and priestly petition against disgrace, without treating Heliodorus's mission as the fulfilment of Joel's summons.

2 Maccabees 3:20
All of them stretched their hands toward heaven and offered their entreaty.
  1 Kings 8:37-39 — Temple supplication pattern: The public entreaty with hands extended during the temple crisis resembles Solomon's provision for afflicted people stretching out hands toward the house and seeking God's hearing.

2 Maccabees 3:22
So they called on the all-powerful Lord to keep the entrusted property safe and fully secure for those who had entrusted it.
  Exodus 22:6-8 — Deposit protection petition: The prayer that entrusted property remain safe addresses the specific responsibility for deposits regulated in Exodus, rather than asserting royal ownership of the funds.

2 Maccabees 3:24
When he was already there at the treasury with his bodyguards, the Sovereign of spirits and of all authority made a great manifestation. All who had dared accompany him were struck with awe at God’s power and were overcome by faintness and cowardice.
  2 Maccabees 5:18 — Intervention explicitly recalled: The later comparison with Antiochus explicitly recalls the divine scourging that stopped Heliodorus's advance against the treasury.

2 Maccabees 3:25
For there appeared to them a horse with a fearsome rider, adorned with the finest harness. Rushing forcefully forward, it struck at Heliodorus with its forehooves; its rider appeared to wear a full suit of golden armour.
  2 Maccabees 11:8 — Heavenly horseman compared: The splendid rider with golden armour resembles the later horseman bearing golden armour who leads the defenders; the first manifestation attacks Heliodorus and the second aids Judas.

2 Maccabees 3:26
Two other young men appeared to him, remarkable in strength, most beautiful in glory, and splendid in clothing. They stood on either side of him and flogged him without ceasing, raining many blows upon him.
  2 Maccabees 3:33-34 — Figures reappear; same-chapter: The two young men who scourge Heliodorus are explicitly described as the same figures when they return to explain his restored life and commission his testimony.

2 Maccabees 3:27
He suddenly fell to the ground, and deep darkness enveloped him. They picked him up and placed him on a litter.
  2 Maccabees 9:8 — Humiliation on a litter: Heliodorus's fall and removal on a litter resemble Antiochus's later public humiliation and helpless transport on a litter; the men and injuries differ.

2 Maccabees 3:28
The man who had just entered that treasury with a large retinue and all his bodyguard was carried out, unable to help himself. They plainly recognized God’s sovereignty.
  2 Maccabees 5:18 — Failed intrusion recalled: Heliodorus's helpless removal is the event later recalled when the narrator says he was scourged and thrown back from his rash advance.

2 Maccabees 3:29
Through the divine action he lay speechless, deprived of every hope and means of deliverance.
  2 Maccabees 3:33 — Life restored; same-chapter: Heliodorus's apparent loss of every hope of rescue is reversed by the messengers' declaration that the Lord has granted him life through Onias.

2 Maccabees 3:31
Some of Heliodorus’s companions quickly begged Onias to call upon the Most High to grant life to the man who lay at his last breath.
  Genesis 20:7 — Intercession for a threatened life: The request that Onias pray for Heliodorus's life resembles God's direction that Abraham pray so that the threatened Abimelech may live; the offences and intercessors differ.

2 Maccabees 3:32
The high priest, fearing that the king might form the opinion that the Jews had perpetrated some wrongdoing against Heliodorus, offered a sacrifice for the man’s deliverance.
  2 Maccabees 3:33 — Intercession answered; same-chapter: Onias's sacrifice for Heliodorus's deliverance is answered by the explicit declaration that the Lord granted life because of Onias.

2 Maccabees 3:33

  2 Maccabees 3:26 — Manifestation identified; same-chapter: The returning young men are explicitly the same two splendid figures who had scourged Heliodorus, now announcing the reason for his recovery.

2 Maccabees 3:34
You, having been scourged from heaven, proclaim to everyone God’s majestic might.” Having said this, they became invisible.

2 Maccabees 12:22

  2 Maccabees 10:20-22 — Release for kin distinguished from bribery: Timothy is released on guarantees to restore captive relatives, whereas the tower guards release enemies for money and are punished as traitors. The comparison preserves the different stated reasons for releasing opponents.

2 Maccabees 12:27

  1 Maccabees 5:46-50 — Fortified Ephron compared: Both accounts describe an attack on strongly fortified Ephron. 2 Maccabees 12:28

2 Maccabees 12:32

  Leviticus 23:15-16 — Pentecost calendar: The campaign begins after Pentecost, the festival associated with the law's count of seven weeks and fifty days; the law supplies the observance's calendar rather than a military event.

2 Maccabees 12:34

2 Maccabees 12:38

  2 Maccabees 8:26-27 — Sabbath orders campaign activity: The pause at Adullam to purify and observe the Sabbath repeats the pattern of restricting military activity before the Sabbath and devoting the day to observance in the earlier victory account.

  Joshua 7:11-12 — Forbidden spoil explains military loss: The explanation of battle deaths through concealed forbidden objects resembles the explanation of Israel's defeat through stolen banned property in Joshua. The culprits and the treatment of the dead are not identified as the same episode or procedure.

2 Maccabees 12:41

  1 Corinthians 4:5 — Hidden deeds exposed in judgment: The praise of the righteous Judge who reveals hidden things resembles Paul's expectation that the Lord will bring hidden darkness and purposes to light. The immediate discovery of idol objects differs from Paul's future horizon.

  2 Maccabees 7:9 — Resurrection expectation motivates action: The narrator explicitly grounds Judas's offering in consideration of resurrection, recalling the martyr's confidence that the King of the world will raise those who die for His laws. 2 Maccabees 12:44

2 Maccabees 12:45

  2 Maccabees 7:36 — Reward beyond death: The expected reward for those who die in piety corresponds to the brothers' promised covenant of everlasting life. The offering to release the fallen from sin is specific to this passage and is not attributed to the martyrs' speech.
