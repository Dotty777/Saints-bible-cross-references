Deuteronomy 1:1
These are the words which Moses spoke unto all Israel beyond the Jordan, in the wilderness toward the west, near the Red Sea, between Paran, Tophel, Laban, Aulon, and Katachrysea.
  Deuteronomy 1:5 — Same-chapter discourse setting: The opening locates Moses beyond the Jordan, and the later introductory notice specifies Moab as the place where he explained the law; the additional geographical names are not silently equated with other sites.

Deuteronomy 1:2
It is a journey of eleven days from Horeb by the way of Mount Seir unto Kadesh-barnea.
  Deuteronomy 1:19 — Same-chapter route and narrated arrival: The header states an eleven-day journey from Horeb by Mount Seir to Kadesh-barnea; the recital subsequently describes departure from Horeb and arrival at Kadesh-barnea, without saying the whole narrated interval lasted eleven days.

Deuteronomy 1:3
And it came to pass in the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month, that Moses spoke unto all the sons of Israel, according to all that the Lord had commanded him concerning them.
  Numbers 33:38 — Fortieth-year chronological notices: Moses speaks on the first day of the eleventh month in the fortieth year; Aaron dies on the first day of the fifth month in that year. These are distinct dated events, not identical dates.

Deuteronomy 1:4
After he had smitten Sihon king of the Amorites, who dwelt in Heshbon, and Og king of Bashan, who dwelt in Ashtaroth and in Edrei,
  Numbers 21:23-25 — Sihon conquest recalled: The discourse follows the defeat of Sihon of Heshbon; Numbers narrates Sihon refusing passage, Israel defeating him, and Israel taking the Amorite towns including Heshbon.
  Numbers 21:33-35 — Og conquest recalled: The introductory notice names the defeat of Og of Bashan associated with Ashtaroth and Edrei; Numbers narrates Og coming to battle at Edrei and the defeat of him, his sons, and his people.

Deuteronomy 1:5
beyond the Jordan, in the land of Moab, Moses began to make this law plain, saying:
  Deuteronomy 4:44-46 — Repeated law-discourse frame: Moses explains the law beyond the Jordan in Moab; the later frame again identifies Moses setting forth the law beyond the Jordan, in the ravine near the house of Peor and Sihon’s former territory.

Deuteronomy 1:6
The Lord our God spoke unto us in Horeb, saying: Let it suffice you to have dwelt in this mountain.
  Deuteronomy 1:19 — Same-chapter departure command and execution: The command says that the stay at Horeb has been sufficient; Moses subsequently recounts the actual departure from Horeb and journey through the fearful wilderness to Kadesh-barnea.

Deuteronomy 1:7
Turn and set forth, and enter the hill country of the Amorites and all the neighbouring places of Arabah, the hill country and the plain, the southwest and the seacoast, the land of the Canaanites and Anti-Lebanon, as far as the great river Euphrates.
  Deuteronomy 1:19-21 — Same-chapter commanded destination and arrival: The command directs Israel toward the Amorite hill country among the wider named regions; the recital then reports arrival at that hill country and commands possession. It does not claim every region extending to the Euphrates was already occupied.

Deuteronomy 1:8
Behold, I have delivered the land before you. Enter and inherit the land which I sware unto your fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, to give unto them and unto their seed after them.
  Exodus 6:8 — Patriarchal land oath and inheritance: Both passages name Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the Lord’s undertaking to give their land to Israel as an inheritance; Deuteronomy urges possession of the land promised in the earlier assurance.

Deuteronomy 1:9
And I spoke unto you at that time, saying: I shall not be able to bear you alone.
  Numbers 11:14 — Moses unable to bear the people alone: Moses’ recollection that he could not bear Israel alone corresponds to his explicit complaint that the people’s burden was too heavy; Numbers places that complaint in the meat-demand episode.
  Exodus 18:18 — Judicial burden requiring assistance: The recollection of inability to bear the people alone matches Jethro’s specific warning that judging the people alone would exhaust Moses and the people. The passages retain their different speakers.

Deuteronomy 1:10
The Lord your God has multiplied you, and behold, you are this day as the stars of heaven in multitude.
  Genesis 15:5 — Descendants compared with heavenly stars: Moses describes Israel’s present multiplication as the stars of heaven; the earlier promise directs Abram to count the stars and says his seed will be like them.
  Genesis 22:17 — Star-numbered descendants and blessing: Israel’s multiplication like the heavenly stars recalls the promise to bless Abraham and multiply his seed like the stars; this comparison does not claim an exact numerical census of the stars.

Deuteronomy 1:11
The Lord, the God of your fathers, make you a thousand times as many as you are, and bless you, as He has spoken unto you.
  Genesis 22:17-18 — Prayer for continued ancestral blessing: Moses asks the God of the fathers to multiply and bless Israel; the ancestral promise joins blessing with multiplication of Abraham’s seed. The requested thousandfold increase belongs to Moses’ prayer, not to an identical number in Genesis.

Deuteronomy 1:12
How shall I alone be able to bear your trouble, and your resistance, and your disputes?
  Exodus 18:18-22 — Burden of disputes shared with appointed judges: Moses asks how he can bear the people’s trouble and disputes alone; Jethro identifies the heavy judicial burden and proposes subordinate judges who will bear it with him.

Deuteronomy 1:13
Choose for yourselves wise men, men of knowledge and understanding, for your tribes, and I will appoint them to rule over you.
  Exodus 18:21 — Selection of qualified tribal leaders: Both passages require qualified men to assist government, but preserve their selection procedures: Moses asks the people to supply wise and understanding men by tribes, while Jethro tells Moses to choose able, God-fearing, righteous men.

Deuteronomy 1:14
And you answered me and said: The thing which you have spoken is good to do.
  Deuteronomy 1:13 — Same-chapter proposal and public approval: The people’s declaration that the proposed course is good answers the immediately specified proposal to supply wise and understanding tribal men for Moses to appoint as leaders.

Deuteronomy 1:15
And I took from among you wise men, men of knowledge and understanding, and appointed them to rule over you, captains of thousands, and captains of hundreds, and captains of fifties, and captains of tens, and recording officers for your judges.
  Exodus 18:25 — Matching graded leadership ranks: Both appointments specify leaders of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens; Deuteronomy additionally mentions recording officers for the judges, without inserting that additional office into Exodus.

Deuteronomy 1:16
And I commanded your judges at that time, saying: Hear the matters between your brethren, and judge justly between a man and his brother and the sojourner with him.
  Leviticus 24:22 — Impartial judgment for native and foreigner: The judges must hear brothers and judge righteously between a man, his brother, and his resident foreigner; Leviticus explicitly requires one judgment for resident foreigner and local inhabitant.

Deuteronomy 1:17
You shall not show partiality in judgment; you shall judge the small and the great alike. You shall not shrink before the face of man, for judgment belongs unto God. And you shall bring unto me the matter which is too hard for you, and I will hear it.
  Leviticus 19:15 — Judgment without social partiality: Hearing small and great alike corresponds to the law against favouring the poor or admiring the mighty in judgment; both address concrete impartiality in deciding cases.
  Exodus 18:22 — Difficult cases reserved for Moses: The instruction to bring a difficult matter to Moses matches Jethro’s arrangement that subordinate judges handle lesser judgments while weighty matters come to Moses.

Deuteronomy 1:18
And I commanded you at that time all the things which you should do.
  Deuteronomy 1:16-17 — Same-chapter judicial summary and its contents: The notice that Moses commanded the required conduct summarizes the preceding instructions to the judges about righteous hearings, equal attention to small and great, and referral of difficult cases.

Deuteronomy 1:19
And we departed from Horeb, and went through all that great and fearful wilderness which you saw, by the way of the hill country of the Amorite, as the Lord our God commanded us; and we came unto Kadesh-barnea.
  Deuteronomy 1:6-7 — Same-chapter departure follows stated route command: The report of leaving Horeb and approaching the Amorite hill country follows the command to leave the mountain and journey toward that country; Kadesh-barnea is the reported arrival point.

Deuteronomy 1:20
And I said unto you: You have come unto the hill country of the Amorite, which the Lord our God gives you.
  Deuteronomy 1:7 — Same-chapter destination identified on arrival: Moses identifies the reached country as the Amorite hill country given by God; that region had already been named explicitly in the departure command, alongside wider geographical destinations.

Deuteronomy 1:21
Behold, the Lord your God has delivered the land before you. Go up and inherit it, as the Lord, the God of your fathers, has spoken unto you; fear not, neither be dismayed.
  Numbers 14:8-9 — Exhortations to possess the land without fear: Moses commands Israel to inherit the land and not fear; Joshua and Caleb likewise urge confidence that the Lord will give the land and prohibit fear of its people. The exhortations retain their different speakers.

Deuteronomy 1:22
And you all came unto me, and said: Let us send men before us, and let them search out the land for us, and bring us word of the way by which we shall go up, and of the cities into which we shall enter.
  Numbers 13:1-3 — Differently framed commissioning of the scouts: Deuteronomy recalls the people proposing scouts to report the route and cities; Numbers gives the Lord’s command to Moses and his sending of tribal leaders. Neither account’s initiating speaker is replaced with the other’s.

Deuteronomy 1:23
And the saying pleased me; and I took twelve men from among you, one man from each tribe.
  Numbers 13:2 — One representative from each tribe: Moses recalls choosing twelve men, one from each tribe; the Lord’s scouting command requires one man from each tribe, each a leader. No individual name equivalence is inferred from this shared arrangement.

Deuteronomy 1:24
And they turned and went up into the hill country, and came unto the Valley of the Cluster, and searched it out.
  Numbers 13:23-24 — Scouting visit to the cluster-named valley: The scouts ascend and reach the Valley of the Cluster; Numbers describes their visit to the Ravine of the Cluster, its fruit, and its naming. The distinct valley and ravine renderings are retained.

Deuteronomy 1:25
And they took of the fruit of the land in their hands, and brought it down unto us, and said: The land which the Lord our God gives us is good.
  Numbers 13:26-27 — Fruit brought back and favourable land report: The men bring fruit and report that the land God gives is good; Numbers records the returned scouts displaying fruit and describing the land as flowing with milk and honey.

Deuteronomy 1:26
And you would not go up, but disobeyed the word of the Lord your God.
  Numbers 14:1-4 — Refusal following the scouting report: Moses recounts Israel refusing to ascend and resisting God’s command; Numbers gives the congregation’s weeping, grumbling, and proposal to appoint a leader and return to Egypt.

Deuteronomy 1:27
And you murmured in your tents, and said: Because the Lord hates us, He brought us forth out of the land of Egypt, to deliver us into the hands of the Amorites, to destroy us.
  Numbers 14:2-3 — Accusations about the purpose of the exodus: The people accuse the Lord of bringing them from Egypt through hatred to destruction by the Amorites; Numbers reports their complaint that entering the land will mean death in war. These remain the people’s accusations.

Deuteronomy 1:28
Whither do we go up? Your brethren have made your heart fail, saying: The nation is great and numerous and mightier than we; and the cities are great and walled up to heaven. Moreover, we have seen sons of giants there.
  Numbers 13:28-33 — Fear-producing report of strong inhabitants: The recital names stronger people, great walled cities, and giants as the discouraging report; Numbers similarly describes strongly fortified cities, Anak’s family, stronger inhabitants, and giants.

Deuteronomy 1:29
And I said unto you: Neither cower nor fear them.
  Numbers 14:9 — Appeals against fear after the scouts return: Moses tells the discouraged people not to fear the inhabitants; Joshua and Caleb likewise tell the congregation not to fear the people of the land because the Lord is among Israel.

Deuteronomy 1:30
The Lord your God, who goes before you, shall Himself fight against them with you, according to all that He did for you in the land of Egypt,
  Exodus 14:13-14 — Egyptian deliverance as assurance that God fights: Moses appeals to what the Lord did for Israel in Egypt as assurance that He will fight; the earlier sea-deliverance speech expressly promises that the Lord will fight for the frightened people.

Deuteronomy 1:31
and in this wilderness which you saw, how the Lord your God nurtured you as a man nurtures his son, in all the way which you went, until you came unto this place.
  Numbers 11:12 — Parental and nursing imagery with different agents: Moses says that God nurtured Israel like a father his son; his earlier complaint asks whether he gave birth to the people and must carry them as a nurse carries a child. The agents and metaphors remain different.

Deuteronomy 1:32
And in this matter you did not trust the Lord your God,
  Numbers 14:11 — Failure to believe despite witnessed divine acts: Moses charges Israel with not trusting the Lord despite the preceding deliverance and nurture; Numbers records the Lord asking why the people refuse belief despite the signs performed among them.

Deuteronomy 1:33
who goes before you in the way to choose a place for you, guiding you in fire by night, to show you the way wherein you should go, and in a cloud by day.
  Exodus 13:21-22 — Guidance by cloud and fire: The Lord’s going before Israel by fire at night and cloud by day recalls the continuing pillars described in Exodus; Deuteronomy additionally specifies searching out camping places.

Deuteronomy 1:34
And the Lord heard the voice of your words, and, being provoked to anger, sware, saying:
  Numbers 14:28-35 — Angry oath concerning the resistant generation: The Lord hears the people’s words and swears in anger; Numbers records the sworn announcement that the counted grumbling generation will die in the wilderness, with stated exceptions and provision for their children.

Deuteronomy 1:35
Surely none of these men shall see this good land which I sware unto their fathers,
  Numbers 14:22-23 — Provoking adults excluded from the sworn land: The excluded generation will not see the good land sworn to the fathers; Numbers likewise excludes the men who saw the signs yet repeatedly tested God from seeing that sworn land.

Deuteronomy 1:36
save Caleb son of Jephunneh. He shall see it, and I will give unto him and unto his sons the land whereon he trod, because he cleaved unto the things of the Lord.
  Numbers 14:24 — Caleb’s faithfulness and hereditary land grant: Both passages single out Caleb for following the Lord and promise that he and his seed will possess the land he entered; the shared reason is explicitly stated faithfulness.
  Joshua 14:8-14 — Caleb’s remembered promise and subsequent allotment: Caleb recalls faithfully following the Lord and Moses’ oath assigning the land he trod to him and his children; Joshua then grants Hebron. This supplies later reception and allotment of the particular Caleb promise.

Deuteronomy 1:37
And the Lord was angry with me also for your sakes, saying: You shall not enter there either.
  Numbers 20:12 — Moses excluded with differing stated explanations: Deuteronomy says the Lord was angry with Moses for the people’s sakes and barred his entry; Numbers addresses Moses and Aaron’s failure to believe and sanctify God at the water. The differing explanations and narrative settings are preserved.

Deuteronomy 1:38
Joshua son of Nun, who stands beside you, he shall enter there. Strengthen him, for he shall cause Israel to inherit it.
  Numbers 27:18-23 — Joshua commissioned as Moses’ successor: Moses must strengthen Joshua son of Nun for leading Israel into its inheritance; Numbers describes the Lord naming Joshua son of Nun and Moses publicly commissioning him before Eleazar and the congregation.
  Numbers 14:30 — Joshua named among the permitted entrants: Joshua son of Nun is designated to enter and lead Israel into inheritance; the wilderness judgment explicitly exempts Joshua son of Nun and Caleb son of Jephunneh from the adults’ exclusion.

Deuteronomy 1:39
And every young child who this day knows neither good nor evil shall enter there; and I will give it unto them, and they shall inherit it.
  Numbers 14:23 — Children without knowledge of good or evil inherit: Both passages explicitly distinguish the children who do not know good or evil and promise them the land denied to the provoking adults; this age-description is not replaced with an inferred numerical age.

Deuteronomy 1:40
But you, turn and encamp in the wilderness by the way of the Red Sea.
  Numbers 14:25 — Command to turn by the Red Sea wilderness route: Both commands redirect Israel away from the attempted entry and toward the wilderness by the Red Sea road; Numbers additionally names Amalek and the Canaanites in the valley.

Deuteronomy 1:41
And you answered me and said: We have sinned before the Lord our God. We will go up and fight, according to all that the Lord our God commanded us. And you took up, every man, his weapons of war, and gathered yourselves together, and went up into the hill country.
  Numbers 14:40 — Confession followed by untimely ascent: The people confess sin and decide to ascend for battle; Numbers likewise records their confession and determination to ascend after the judgment, rather than treating the renewed effort as approved obedience.

Deuteronomy 1:42
And the Lord said unto me: Say unto them, You shall not go up, neither shall you fight, for I am not with you; and you shall not be crushed before your enemies.
  Numbers 14:41-43 — Warning not to ascend without divine presence: Both prohibit ascent without the Lord's presence; Deuteronomy says not to ascend and not be crushed, while Numbers explicitly predicts falling before the enemies and names Amalek and the Canaanites.

Deuteronomy 1:43
And I spoke unto you, but you did not hearken unto me; and you transgressed the word of the Lord, and forced your way up into the hill country.
  Numbers 14:44 — Presumptuous ascent despite the warning: The people disregard the warning and force their way into the hill country; Numbers likewise describes forcing their way up while Moses and the ark remain in camp.

Deuteronomy 1:44
And the Amorites who dwelt in that hill country came out to meet you, and pursued you as bees do, and wounded you from Seir unto Hormah.
  Numbers 14:45 — Defeat recounted with differing enemies and endpoints: Both accounts describe defeat by mountain inhabitants after the unauthorized ascent, but Deuteronomy names Amorites pursuing like bees from Seir to Hormah, while Numbers names Amalek and Canaanites cutting them down to Erman. These names are not harmonized.

Deuteronomy 1:45
And you sat down and wept before the Lord; but the Lord did not hearken unto your voice, neither gave He heed unto you.
  Deuteronomy 1:42-44 — Same-chapter warning, defeat, and unanswered weeping: The unanswered weeping follows the specific warning that the Lord would not accompany the ascent and the narrated defeat; this link preserves that sequence without adding an unstated motive for the refusal to hear.

Deuteronomy 1:46
And you dwelt in Kadesh many days, as many days as you dwelt there.
  Deuteronomy 2:1 — Residence followed by the resumed journey: The notice of remaining many days at Kadesh closes the first recital; the next movement resumes with turning toward the wilderness and Red Sea and circling Mount Seir. No new duration is assigned to the Kadesh stay.

Deuteronomy 2:1
And we turned and departed into the wilderness by the way of the Red Sea, as the Lord had spoken unto me; and we compassed Mount Seir many days.
  Deuteronomy 1:40 — Red Sea redirection recalled as journey: The people turn toward the wilderness by the Red Sea road and circle Mount Seir; the preceding command had directed their return toward the wilderness by that Red Sea road, without specifying the later circuit’s duration.

Deuteronomy 2:2
And the Lord said unto me:
  Deuteronomy 2:3-6 — Same-chapter speech notice and specified instructions: The introductory notice that the Lord spoke is developed by the northward turn, the warning about Esau’s descendants in Seir, the prohibition on fighting them, and the instruction to purchase provisions.

Deuteronomy 2:3
Let it suffice you to have compassed this mountain; turn therefore northward.
  Deuteronomy 2:1 — Same-chapter Seir circuit and command to end it: The Lord says the circuit around the mountain has lasted sufficiently and commands a northward turn; the immediately preceding itinerary specifies the many-day circuit around Mount Seir.

Deuteronomy 2:4
And command the people, saying: You are passing through the borders of your brethren, the sons of Esau, who dwell in Seir; and they shall fear you, and be exceedingly wary of you.
  Genesis 36:6-8 — Esau’s kinship and residence in Seir: The command calls Seir’s inhabitants the sons of Esau and Israel’s brothers; Genesis identifies Esau as Jacob’s brother, records his move with his household, and explicitly states his residence in Mount Seir and his name Edom.

Deuteronomy 2:5
Do not join battle with them, for I will give you none of their land, no, not so much as a footstep; for I have given Mount Seir unto the sons of Esau for a possession.
  Numbers 20:20-21 — Respect for Esau’s territory and detour from Edom: The command forbids war over Esau’s assigned land; Numbers reports Edom’s armed refusal of passage and Israel turning away. This compares non-conquest with the detour, without converting Edom’s refusal into permission.

Deuteronomy 2:6
Buy food from them for silver, and eat; and you shall take water from them by measure for silver, and drink.
  Numbers 20:19 — Payment for water in differently addressed dealings: Israel must buy food and measured water with silver from Esau’s descendants; Numbers records an offer to pay Edom if Israel and its livestock drink water. An offer is not silently treated as an accepted sale.

Deuteronomy 2:7
For the Lord our God has blessed you in all the work of your hands. Consider how you passed through that great and fearful wilderness. Behold, these forty years the Lord your God has been with you; you have lacked nothing.
  Nehemiah 9:21 — Forty years of nourishment without lack: Moses recalls forty years of the Lord’s presence in the wilderness during which Israel lacked nothing; Nehemiah’s prayer likewise explicitly recalls forty years of wilderness nourishment and lack of nothing.

Deuteronomy 2:8
And we passed by our brethren, the sons of Esau who dwell in Seir, along the way of Arabah, from Ailon and from Ezion-geber; and we turned and passed along the wilderness way of Moab.
  Numbers 21:4 — Routes around Esau’s or Edom’s country: Deuteronomy recounts passing by Esau’s descendants in Seir and turning toward Moab’s wilderness; Numbers describes going around Edom by the Red Sea road. The accounts’ distinct named stations and stages are not collapsed.

Deuteronomy 2:9
And the Lord said unto me: Do not deal as enemies with the Moabites, neither join battle with them; for I will give you none of their land for a possession, for I have given Seir unto the sons of Lot to inherit.
  Genesis 19:36-38 — Moab’s stated descent from Lot: The prohibition on hostility toward Moab appeals to the sons of Lot; Genesis traces Moab to Lot’s older daughter and names him father of the Moabites. Deuteronomy’s actual land-name Seir is retained rather than changed to Ar.

Deuteronomy 2:10
The Emim dwelt there aforetime, a nation great and numerous and strong as the Anakim.
  Deuteronomy 2:11 — Same-chapter description and naming of the Emim: The former inhabitants are described as Emim, numerous and strong like the Anakim; the following explanation classifies them among the Rephaim and gives Emim as the Moabite designation, without equating all Anakim and Emim.

Deuteronomy 2:11
These also are reckoned as Rephaim, like the Anakim; and the Moabites call them Emim.
  Deuteronomy 2:20 — Same-chapter regional names associated with Rephaim: Moabites call the described former population Emim, while Ammonites call the population of their country Zamzummim; both notices use Rephaim. This compares regional designations without asserting the two populations were identical.

Deuteronomy 2:12
And the Horites dwelt in Seir aforetime; but the sons of Esau destroyed them, and blotted them out from before them, and dwelt in their stead, as Israel did in the land of its inheritance, which the Lord gave unto them.
  Deuteronomy 2:22 — Same-chapter repeated account of Horite displacement: Both notices describe the Horites as Seir’s earlier inhabitants and Esau’s descendants replacing them; the later notice uses that specific displacement as an analogy within the Ammonite account.

Deuteronomy 2:13
Now therefore arise, and set forth, and pass through the Ravine Zered. And we passed through the Ravine Zered.
  Numbers 21:12 — Zered itinerary notices with distinct actions: The command requires departure and crossing the ravine of Zered; Numbers reports departure and camping in that ravine. Crossing and camping are retained as distinct actions associated with the named itinerary feature.

Deuteronomy 2:14
And the time wherein we journeyed from Kadesh-barnea until we passed through the Ravine Zered was thirty and eight years, until the whole generation of men of war had fallen, dying out of the camp, as God had sworn unto them.
  Numbers 14:29-35 — Generation’s death and distinct stated durations: The recital measures thirty-eight years from Kadesh-barnea to crossing Zered until the warriors’ generation died; Numbers gives the judgment of wilderness death and forty years tied to the forty scouting days. The durations are not equated.

Deuteronomy 2:15
And the hand of God was upon them, to consume them out of the camp, until they had fallen.
  Deuteronomy 2:14 — Same-chapter divine agency and completion of judgment: The Lord’s hand consumes the warriors from the camp until they perish; the preceding chronological notice identifies that generation’s completion after thirty-eight years and connects it with the Lord’s sworn judgment.

Deuteronomy 2:16
And it came to pass, when all the men of war had fallen, dying out from among the people,
  Deuteronomy 2:14-15 — Same-chapter completed generation-loss as transition: The notice that every warrior had died marks the completed condition for the following instructions; the preceding verses specify the elapsed interval and the Lord’s consuming hand upon that generation.

Deuteronomy 2:17
that the Lord spoke unto me, saying:
  Deuteronomy 2:18-19 — Same-chapter speech introduction and border instructions: The statement that the Lord spoke introduces the particular instructions about passing Moab’s border at Seir and approaching Ammon’s descendants without provoking them or taking their assigned country.

Deuteronomy 2:18
You shall pass this day through the borders of Moab, even Seir,
  Deuteronomy 2:9 — Same-chapter repeated Moab and Seir notices: The border-crossing instruction names Moab and Seir; the earlier prohibition on hostility to Moab also actually says Seir. Both supplied readings are preserved without silently substituting another land-name.

Deuteronomy 2:19
and you shall draw near unto the sons of Ammon. Do not deal as enemies with them, neither join battle with them; for I will give you none of the land of the sons of Ammon for a possession, because I have given it unto the sons of Lot for a possession.
  Genesis 19:38 — Lot ancestry behind the Ammon prohibition: Ammon’s descendants are protected from attack because their land was assigned to Lot’s sons; Genesis records the younger daughter’s son Amman as ancestor of the Ammanites. These supplied name forms are retained.
  Deuteronomy 2:9 — Same-chapter protected lands of Lot’s descendants: The Lord separately prohibits attacking Ammon’s descendants and Moab, grounding each prohibition in a grant to Lot’s sons. The two peoples and the actual territorial wording remain distinct.

Deuteronomy 2:20
It is reckoned a land of Rephaim; for the Rephaim dwelt there aforetime, and the Ammonites call them Zamzummim,
  Deuteronomy 2:11 — Same-chapter Rephaim naming in different countries: The Ammonite notice uses Zamzummim for the former people counted as Rephaim; the Moabite notice uses Emim. The shared classification does not independently establish that the two regional populations were identical.

Deuteronomy 2:21
a nation great and numerous and mightier than you, like the Anakim. And the Lord destroyed them from before the Ammonites, who inherited the land and dwelt in their stead unto this day,
  Deuteronomy 2:22 — Same-chapter explicit analogy of displaced inhabitants: The Lord's destruction of the powerful earlier inhabitants before Ammon's descendants is followed by an explicit comparison with the Horites' displacement before Esau's descendants in Seir. The differing grammatical agents, peoples and territories remain distinct.

Deuteronomy 2:22
even as they did for the sons of Esau who dwell in Seir, when they blotted out the Horites from before them, and inherited the land, and dwelt in their stead unto this day.
  Deuteronomy 2:12 — Same-chapter Seir displacement repeated for comparison: The Horites’ destruction and Esau’s descendants inhabiting Seir repeat the earlier Seir notice; here that particular history supports the preceding comparison with the Ammonites’ inheritance.

Deuteronomy 2:23
And the Hivites who dwelt in settlements as far as Gaza, the Cappadocians who came forth from Cappadocia blotted them out, and dwelt in their stead.
  Deuteronomy 2:21-22 — Same-chapter sequence of population replacements: Cappadocians from Cappadocia destroy the Hivites near Gaza and settle in their stead; the surrounding examples also recount replacement of earlier inhabitants by Ammon’s and Esau’s descendants. The populations and locations are not identified with one another.

Deuteronomy 2:24
Now therefore arise, and set forth, and pass through the Ravine Arnon. Behold, I have delivered into your hands Sihon the Amorite, king of Heshbon, and his land. Begin to inherit it; join battle with him.
  Numbers 21:13 — Arnon crossing at the Amorite frontier: The command joins crossing the Arnon with beginning possession and war against Sihon; Numbers describes the Arnon as the frontier between Moab and the Amorites and records Israel camping beyond it.
  Numbers 21:23-24 — Commanded conquest and narrated defeat of Sihon: The Lord announces Sihon’s delivery and commands Israel to begin war and inheritance; Numbers narrates Sihon’s attack, his defeat, and Israel’s taking control of his territory from Arnon to Jabbok.

Deuteronomy 2:25
This day begin to put the dread of you and the fear of you before all the nations under heaven, who, when they hear your name, shall be troubled and shall suffer pangs before you.
  Joshua 2:9-11 — Promised terror and later report about the two kings: The Lord promises fear among nations hearing of Israel; Rahab later reports fear after hearing about the Red Sea and the destruction of Sihon and Og. Her testimony illustrates the announced terror without claiming every nation’s response is narrated there.

Deuteronomy 2:26
And I sent messengers from the wilderness of Kedemoth unto Sihon king of Heshbon with words of peace, saying:
  Numbers 21:21 — Peace envoys to Sihon: Moses recalls sending peace messages from Kedemoth to Sihon of Heshbon; Numbers likewise explicitly describes Moses sending envoys with words of peace to Sihon king of the Amorites.

Deuteronomy 2:27
I will pass through your land. I will pass along the way; I will turn neither to the right nor to the left.

  Leviticus 22:20-22 — Blemished animals excluded from sacrifice: Both reject blemished animals as offerings and explicitly include blindness; Deuteronomy additionally names lameness, while Leviticus supplies its own longer list and offering context.

Deuteronomy 15:22

  Deuteronomy 12:22 — Repeated local clean-and-unclean meat permission: Both permit clean and unclean persons alike to eat locally as gazelle and deer are eaten; the current context concerns the blemished firstborn rather than ordinary meat generally.

Deuteronomy 15:23

  Deuteronomy 12:16 — Repeated blood prohibition and earth-water disposal formula: Both prohibit eating blood and require pouring it onto the earth like water, retaining the same particular restriction alongside their respective local meat permissions.

Deuteronomy 16:1
Keep the month of new produce, and observe the Passover unto the Lord your God; for in the month of new produce you came forth out of Egypt by night.
  Exodus 34:18 — Departure month and Unleavened Bread remembrance: Both connect the prescribed observance with the month of new produce or new grain and departure from Egypt, preserving their respective month descriptions and festival emphasis.
  Exodus 12:29-31 — Nighttime Egyptian deliverance context: The Passover remembrance expressly recalls departure by night; Exodus narrates the midnight firstborn judgment and Pharaoh summoning Moses and Aaron at night to order Israel's departure.

Deuteronomy 16:2
And you shall sacrifice the Passover unto the Lord your God, sheep and cattle, in the place which the Lord your God shall choose for His name to be called upon there.
  Exodus 12:3-6 — Passover sacrifice with differing livestock and setting details: Both command the Passover sacrifice; Deuteronomy names sheep and cattle at the chosen place, while Exodus instructs Israelite households in Egypt concerning a lamb or young goat and its evening slaughter.

Deuteronomy 16:3
You shall eat no leaven with it; you shall eat unleavened bread with it for seven days, the bread of affliction, for you came forth out of Egypt in haste; that you may remember the day of your departure out of the land of Egypt all the days of your life.
  Exodus 12:39 — Unleavened bread and hasty departure: Deuteronomy calls the bread affliction and recalls departure in haste; Exodus explains the unleavened cakes by the Egyptians driving Israel out before the dough could leaven.
  Exodus 12:15-17 — Seven-day unleavened observance as departure memorial: Both prescribe seven days of unleavened bread in connection with the departure from Egypt, with Exodus also setting first- and seventh-day assemblies and work restrictions.

Deuteronomy 16:4
No leaven shall be seen with you in all your borders seven days; neither shall any of the flesh which you sacrifice in the evening of the first day remain overnight until the morning.
  Exodus 12:10 — Passover flesh not retained until morning: Both forbid leaving the sacrificed flesh until morning; Exodus additionally commands burning what remains, while Deuteronomy specifies the evening sacrifice of the first day.
  Exodus 12:19 — Seven-day absence of leaven: Both require leaven to be absent for seven days; Deuteronomy describes all the borders, while Exodus specifies houses and the consequence for eating leavened food.

Deuteronomy 16:6
but in the place which the Lord your God shall choose for His name to be called upon there, you shall sacrifice the Passover in the evening, at the setting of the sun, at the time when you came forth out of Egypt.
  Exodus 12:6 — Passover slaughter at evening: Both assign Passover slaughter to evening; Deuteronomy further specifies sunset, the departure remembrance and the chosen place, whereas Exodus supplies the fourteenth-day household arrangement.

Deuteronomy 16:7

  Exodus 12:8-9 — Passover preparation instructions with preserved difference: Deuteronomy says to boil and roast and eat at the chosen place; Exodus prescribes roasting with fire and explicitly prohibits boiling in water, without harmonizing those instructions.

Deuteronomy 16:8

  Exodus 12:15-16 — Unleavened observance and concluding work restriction: Deuteronomy specifies six days and a seventh-day concluding feast; Exodus states seven days and first- and seventh-day holiness, with both allowing work for a person's necessary needs.

  Leviticus 23:15-16 — Seven-week counting with distinct starting descriptions: Both require seven complete weeks; Deuteronomy begins with the sickle's first harvest, while Leviticus counts from the day after the Sabbath and sheaf presentation, then names fifty days.

Deuteronomy 16:10

  Exodus 34:22 — Feast of Weeks and harvest observance: Both command keeping the Feast of Weeks; Exodus explicitly names the beginning of wheat harvest, while Deuteronomy measures the observance by ability and received blessing.

Deuteronomy 16:11

  Deuteronomy 16:14 — Same-chapter repeated inclusive festival rejoicing: The Weeks rejoicing and later Tents rejoicing both name the household, slaves, Levite, sojourner, orphan and widow, keeping the two feasts distinct despite the shared participant list.

  Deuteronomy 15:15 — Egyptian household bondage remembered in communal obligations: Both command remembrance of household slavery in Egypt as motivation for the surrounding obligations; chapter15 concerns servant release, while this instruction follows inclusive festival rejoicing.

  Leviticus 23:39-43 — Seven-day ingathering feast with distinct additional details: Both connect a seven-day celebration with gathered produce; Leviticus additionally names the seventh-month date, branches, tent dwelling and the explanatory memory of wilderness tents.

Deuteronomy 16:14

  Deuteronomy 16:11 — Same-chapter shared participant list across distinct feasts: The Tents rejoicing repeats the Weeks list of household, slaves, Levite, sojourner, orphan and widow, preserving the particular inclusive pattern without merging the festivals.

Deuteronomy 16:15

  Leviticus 23:39-41 — Seven-day harvest rejoicing before the Lord: Both specify seven days of harvest celebration and rejoicing before the Lord; Deuteronomy names His chosen place and blessing, whereas Leviticus specifies the calendar and branches.
