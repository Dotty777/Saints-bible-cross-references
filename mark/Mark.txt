Mark 1:1
The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, Son of God.
  John 20:31 — thematic parallel: Mark introduces Jesus Christ as Son of God; John's written-purpose statement likewise joins Jesus, the Christ and the Son of God. John explains believing and life in His name, while Mark's verse introduces the gospel rather than stating that whole purpose.
    John 20:31 but these have been written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and so that, believing, you may have life in His name.

Mark 1:2
As it is written in Isaiah the prophet: “Look, I send My messenger before Thy face, who will prepare Thy way;
  Malachi 3:1 — quotation: The announced messenger preparing the way corresponds to Malachi's messenger clause. Mark introduces the combined citation under Isaiah's name; the following wilderness-voice verse corresponds to Isaiah 40:3. Malachi's supplied wording has the way before My face, while Mark has Thy face and Thy way; the attribution and wording are preserved rather than silently corrected.
    Malachi 3:1 “Look, I am sending out My messenger, and he will look to a way before My face. Suddenly the Lord whom you seek will come to His own sanctuary, and the messenger of the covenant whom you desire. Look, he is coming,” says the Lord Almighty.
  Exodus 23:20 — allusion: The phrase about sending a messenger before Thy face also recalls Exodus' I send My angel before your face. Exodus concerns guidance into a prepared land; Mark uses the preparatory message in its opening about John. The shared sending formula does not identify John as the angel in Exodus.
    Exodus 23:20 And behold, I send My angel before your face, to keep you in the way, that he may bring you into the land which I have prepared for you.

Mark 1:3
a voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Prepare the way of the Lord; make His paths straight.’”
  Isaiah 40:3 — quotation: Mark's wilderness voice and commands to prepare the Lord's way and straighten His paths quote the Isaiah passage named in the preceding introduction. Isaiah's supplied final phrase is the paths of our God.
    Isaiah 40:3 A voice of one crying in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord; make straight the paths of our God.

    Luke 3:2 during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas, God’s word came to John, Zechariah’s son, in the wilderness.
    Luke 3:3 He went into all the region around the Jordan, proclaiming a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins,

  Matthew 3:5–3:6 — narrative parallel: Both describe people from Jerusalem and Judea going to John and receiving baptism in the Jordan while confessing sins. Matthew also names the surrounding Jordan region.
    Matthew 3:5 Then Jerusalem, all Judea, and all the region around the Jordan went out to him,
    Matthew 3:6 and they were baptized by him in the Jordan River, confessing their sins.

  Matthew 3:4 — narrative parallel: Both describe John's camel-hair clothing, leather belt and food of locusts and wild honey.
    Matthew 3:4 John himself wore clothing of camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist; his food was locusts and wild honey.

Mark 1:8

  Luke 3:16 — narrative parallel: Both contrast John's water baptism with the coming One's baptism in the Holy Spirit. Luke adds fire; Mark's cited source verse does not contain that word.
    Luke 3:16 John answered them all, “I baptize you with water, but One stronger than I is coming, whose sandal strap I am not worthy to untie. He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire.

Mark 1:9
In those days Jesus came from Nazareth in Galilee and was baptized in the Jordan by John.
  Matthew 3:13 — narrative parallel: Both bring Jesus from Galilee to John at the Jordan for baptism. Mark specifies Nazareth and states that He was baptized; Matthew's verse narrates His arrival with the purpose of being baptized.
    Matthew 3:13 Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan, to John, to be baptized by him.

Mark 1:10
Immediately as He came up out of the water, He saw the heavens being torn open and the Spirit descending like a dove into Him.
  Matthew 3:16 — narrative parallel: Both describe Jesus emerging from the water, an opening of heaven and the Spirit descending like a dove. Mark says heaven was torn open and the Spirit descended into Him, while Matthew says the heavens opened and the Spirit came upon Him in this edition.
    Matthew 3:16 When Jesus was baptized, He immediately came up from the water. And behold, the heavens were opened, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming upon Him.

Mark 1:11
A voice came from the heavens: “Thou art My beloved Son; in Thee I am well pleased.”
  Matthew 3:17 — narrative parallel: Both record the heavenly declaration of Jesus as beloved Son and of divine pleasure in Him. Mark gives direct address, Thou art, while Matthew gives This is; the grammatical difference is retained.
    Matthew 3:17 And behold, a voice from the heavens said, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”

Mark 1:12
Immediately the Spirit drove Him out into the wilderness.
  Matthew 4:1 — narrative parallel: Both connect the Spirit with Jesus' movement into the wilderness for testing. Mark says the Spirit drove Him, while Matthew says He was led; Matthew names the devil in this verse and Mark names Satan in the following verse.
    Matthew 4:1 Then Jesus was led up into the wilderness by the Spirit to be tempted by the devil.

Mark 1:13
He was in the wilderness forty days, being tempted by Satan. He was with the wild animals, and the angels ministered to Him.
  Matthew 4:1–4:2 — narrative parallel: Both place Jesus' testing in the wilderness in a forty-day setting. Matthew explicitly describes fasting forty days and nights; Mark says He was there forty days being tempted by Satan. These verses do not supply Mark's wild animals or angelic ministry.
    Matthew 4:1 Then Jesus was led up into the wilderness by the Spirit to be tempted by the devil.
    Matthew 4:2 After fasting forty days and forty nights, He became hungry.
  Matthew 4:11 — narrative parallel: Both wilderness accounts describe angels ministering to Jesus. Matthew places their arrival after the devil leaves; Mark includes the ministry in a compressed description of the wilderness stay without that explicit timing.
    Matthew 4:11 Then the devil left Him, and behold, angels came and ministered to Him.

Mark 1:14
And after John was handed over, Jesus came into Galilee, proclaiming the gospel of God,
  Matthew 4:12 — narrative parallel: Both relate Jesus' arrival or withdrawal into Galilee to John having been handed over. Matthew says Jesus heard of it and withdrew; Mark additionally introduces His proclamation of God's gospel.
    Matthew 4:12 When He heard that John had been handed over, He withdrew to Galilee.

Mark 1:15
and saying, “The appointed time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God has drawn near; repent, and believe in the gospel.”
  Matthew 4:17 — teaching parallel: Both summarize Jesus' proclamation through the kingdom's nearness and the call to repent. Mark additionally names fulfillment of the appointed time and belief in the gospel; Matthew says kingdom of the heavens.
    Matthew 4:17 From then on Jesus began to proclaim, “Repent, for the kingdom of the heavens has drawn near.”

    Matthew 4:18 As He walked beside the Sea of Galilee, He saw two brothers, Simon called Peter and Andrew his brother, casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen.

Mark 1:17

  Matthew 4:19 — narrative parallel: Both call the fishermen to come after Jesus with the promise of making them fishers of people or men. Mark's wording includes become.
    Matthew 4:19 He said to them, “Come after Me, and I will make you fishers of people.”

Mark 1:18

  Matthew 4:20 — narrative parallel: Both describe the immediate response of leaving the nets and following Jesus.
    Matthew 4:20 Immediately they left their nets and followed Him.

Mark 1:19
Going a little farther, He saw James son of Zebedee and John his brother, who were also in the boat, putting the nets in order.
  Matthew 4:21 — narrative parallel: Both describe Jesus seeing James and John, Zebedee's sons, in the boat tending their nets. Matthew explicitly places Zebedee with them and includes Jesus' call within this verse.
    Matthew 4:21 Going on from there, He saw two other brothers, James the son of Zebedee and John his brother, in the boat with Zebedee their father, mending their nets; and He called them.

Mark 1:20
Immediately He called them. They left their father Zebedee in the boat with the hired workers and went after Him.
  Matthew 4:21–4:22 — narrative parallel: Both narrate the call of Zebedee's sons and their leaving the boat and father to follow. Mark also mentions hired workers in the boat; Matthew does not supply that detail.
    Matthew 4:21 Going on from there, He saw two other brothers, James the son of Zebedee and John his brother, in the boat with Zebedee their father, mending their nets; and He called them.
    Matthew 4:22 Immediately they left the boat and their father and followed Him.

Mark 1:21
They entered Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath He went into the synagogue and taught.
  Luke 4:31 — narrative parallel: Both place Jesus' teaching in Capernaum on the Sabbath. Mark explicitly names the synagogue here, while Luke's following narrative supplies the synagogue setting.
    Luke 4:31 He went down to Capernaum, a city of Galilee, and was teaching them on the Sabbaths.

Mark 1:22
They were astonished at His teaching, for He taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.
  Luke 4:32 — narrative parallel: Both describe astonishment at Jesus' teaching because it has authority. Mark adds the contrast with the scribes, which Luke's cited verse does not repeat.
    Luke 4:32 They were overwhelmed at His teaching, because His word was with authority.

Mark 1:23
Immediately there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit, and he cried out,
  Luke 4:33 — narrative parallel: Both introduce a man with an unclean spirit in the synagogue who cries out. Luke calls it the spirit of an unclean demon and specifies a loud voice.
    Luke 4:33 In the synagogue was a man who had the spirit of an unclean demon. He cried out with a loud voice,

Mark 1:24

  Luke 4:34 — narrative parallel: Both cries address Jesus of Nazareth, ask whether He has come to destroy them and identify Him as God's Holy One. Luke adds the opening Ah.
    Luke 4:34 “Ah! What have we to do with Thee, Jesus of Nazareth? Hast Thou come to destroy us? I know who Thou art: God’s Holy One.”

Mark 1:25
Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be muzzled, and come out of him!”
  Luke 4:35 — narrative parallel: Both give Jesus' rebuke commanding silence and departure from the man. Mark says Be muzzled, while Luke says Be silent in the supplied wording.
    Luke 4:35 Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be silent and come out of him!” The demon threw him down in their midst and came out of him without harming him.

Mark 1:26
The unclean spirit convulsed him, cried out with a loud voice, and came out of him.
  Luke 4:35 — narrative parallel: Both narrate the spirit's departure with a violent bodily movement. Mark describes convulsing and a loud cry; Luke describes the man being thrown down and expressly says he was not harmed. The movement descriptions are not treated as identical.
    Luke 4:35 Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be silent and come out of him!” The demon threw him down in their midst and came out of him without harming him.

Mark 1:27
They were all amazed, so that they discussed among themselves, saying, “What is this? A new teaching! With authority He commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey Him.”
  Luke 4:36 — narrative parallel: Both depict amazement and discussion of Jesus' authority to command unclean spirits. Mark calls it new teaching and says the spirits obey; Luke names authority and power and says they come out.
    Luke 4:36 Amazement came upon everyone, and they said to one another, “What is this word? With authority and power He commands the unclean spirits, and they come out!”

Mark 1:28
Immediately the report about Him went out everywhere through the whole surrounding region of Galilee.
  Luke 4:37 — narrative parallel: Both conclude the synagogue exorcism with the report about Jesus spreading throughout the surrounding region. Mark explicitly names Galilee.
    Luke 4:37 A report about Him went out into every place in the surrounding region.

Mark 1:29
Immediately after leaving the synagogue, they went into the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John.
  Luke 4:38 — narrative parallel: Both move from the synagogue into Simon's house. Mark adds Andrew and the accompanying James and John; Luke includes the mother-in-law's fever and request for help within the same verse.
    Luke 4:38 Leaving the synagogue, He entered Simon’s house. Simon’s mother-in-law was gripped by a high fever, and they asked Him about her.

Mark 1:30
Simon’s mother-in-law was lying down with a fever, and immediately they told Him about her.
  Matthew 8:14 — narrative parallel: Both describe Simon Peter's mother-in-law lying ill with fever. Matthew places Jesus' seeing her in Peter's house; Mark adds that they immediately told Him about her.
    Matthew 8:14 Jesus entered Peter’s house and saw his mother-in-law lying down with a fever.

Mark 1:31
He came to her, took her by the hand, and raised her up. The fever left her, and she served them.
  Matthew 8:15 — narrative parallel: Both connect Jesus' contact with her hand to the fever's departure, her rising and her service. Mark says she served them and Matthew says Him.
    Matthew 8:15 He touched her hand, and the fever left her. She arose and served Him.

Mark 1:32
When evening came, after the sun had set, they brought to Him all who were ill and those possessed by demons.
  Luke 4:40–4:41 — narrative parallel: Both describe people bringing the sick at sunset and healing accompanied by expulsions of demons. Luke additionally gives laying hands on each sick person and the demons' cries; Mark's source verse introduces the brought groups.
    Luke 4:40 As the sun was setting, everyone who had people sick with various diseases brought them to Him. He laid His hands on each one and healed them.
    Luke 4:41 Demons also came out of many, crying, “Thou art God’s Son!” He rebuked them and would not let them speak, because they knew He was the Christ.

Mark 1:33
The whole city had gathered at the door.
  Mark 2:1–2:2 — internal narrative motif: crowded house doorways: Both Markan scenes portray a crowd gathered at the door of a house where Jesus is present. The earlier evening-healing scene says the whole city; the later Capernaum teaching scene says there is no room even near the door. The repeated crowded-doorway setting is compared without identifying the occasions or asserting that whole city and many are equal numerical counts.
    Mark 2:1 When He entered Capernaum again after some days, it was heard that He was at home.
    Mark 2:2 Many gathered, so that there was no longer room, not even near the door, and He was speaking the word to them.

Mark 1:34
He healed many who were ill with various diseases and cast out many demons. He would not allow the demons to speak, because they knew Him.
  Luke 4:40–4:41 — narrative parallel: Both describe healings, expulsions and Jesus preventing demons from speaking because they know Him. Mark says He healed many; Luke says He healed each person brought and explicitly reports the demons identifying Him as God's Son and the Christ.
    Luke 4:40 As the sun was setting, everyone who had people sick with various diseases brought them to Him. He laid His hands on each one and healed them.
    Luke 4:41 Demons also came out of many, crying, “Thou art God’s Son!” He rebuked them and would not let them speak, because they knew He was the Christ.

Mark 1:35
Very early, while it was still dark, He arose, went out, and departed to a deserted place, and there He prayed.
  Luke 5:16 — thematic parallel: Both describe Jesus withdrawing to deserted places for prayer. Mark narrates a particular predawn departure, while Luke describes His repeated practice without that time notice; this is a shared practice rather than an identification of one occasion.
    Luke 5:16 But He kept withdrawing to deserted places and praying.

Mark 1:36
Simon and those with him searched for Him
  Luke 4:42 — narrative parallel: Both describe people seeking Jesus after His withdrawal to a deserted place. Mark names Simon and his companions; Luke names the crowds, so the seeking groups are not silently equated.
    Luke 4:42 When day came, He went out to a deserted place. The crowds searched for Him, came to Him, and tried to keep Him from departing from them.

Mark 1:37
and found Him. They said to Him, “Everyone is looking for Thee.”
  Luke 4:42 — narrative parallel: Both describe the searchers reaching Jesus after His withdrawal. Mark records the companions' statement that everyone is seeking Him; Luke reports the crowds trying to prevent His departure without reproducing that statement.
    Luke 4:42 When day came, He went out to a deserted place. The crowds searched for Him, came to Him, and tried to keep Him from departing from them.

Mark 1:38

  Luke 4:43 — narrative parallel: Both give Jesus' purpose of proclaiming in other towns or cities as His reason to move on. Mark says for this He came out; Luke explicitly names the good news of God's kingdom and says for this He was sent.
    Luke 4:43 But He said to them, “I must announce the good news of God’s kingdom to the other cities also, because for this I was sent.”

Mark 1:39
He went throughout all Galilee, proclaiming in their synagogues and casting out the demons.
  Matthew 4:23 — thematic parallel: Both summarize a Galilean circuit of proclamation in synagogues. Mark explicitly names casting out demons; Matthew adds teaching and healing every disease and infirmity. The summaries share the region and activity without being identical in scope.
    Matthew 4:23 He went throughout Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, proclaiming the good news of the kingdom, and healing every disease and every infirmity among the people.

Mark 1:41

  Matthew 8:3 — narrative parallel: Both describe Jesus reaching out, touching the man and declaring His willingness with the command to be cleansed. Mark explicitly gives compassion; Matthew's verse also reports the immediate cleansing.

Mark 1:42
Immediately the leprosy departed from him, and he was cleansed.

    Matthew 8:3 He stretched out His hand and touched him, saying, “I am willing; be cleansed.” Immediately his leprosy was cleansed.

Mark 1:43

  Matthew 8:4 — narrative parallel: Mark's stern warning and immediate dismissal introduce the instruction in its following verse; Matthew directly records a warning to tell no one and a command to go to the priest. Matthew does not separately describe the forceful immediate dismissal.
    Matthew 8:4 Jesus said to him, “See that you tell no one, but go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift Moses commanded as a testimony to them.”

Mark 1:44

  Matthew 8:4 — narrative parallel: Both prohibit telling others and direct the healed man to show himself to the priest and make Moses' prescribed offering as testimony. Mark explicitly calls it the offering for cleansing.
    Matthew 8:4 Jesus said to him, “See that you tell no one, but go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift Moses commanded as a testimony to them.”

Mark 1:45

Mark 2:1

Mark 2:13

This compares a specific repeated setting-and-activity pattern without identifying the scenes as one occasion or independent testimony.
    Mark 4:1 Again He began teaching beside the sea. A very great crowd gathered around Him, so that He got into a boat on the sea and sat down, while the whole crowd was on the land beside the sea.

Mark 2:14

  Luke 5:27–5:28 — narrative parallel: Both call the tax collector Levi from the tax station with Follow Me and describe his rising to follow. Mark names him son of Alphaeus; Luke adds leaving everything and does not give that patronymic.

    Luke 5:28 Leaving everything, he rose and followed Him.

Mark 2:15

  Luke 5:29 — narrative parallel: Both depict Jesus at a meal with many tax collectors and others. Luke explicitly calls it Levi's great feast in his house, while Mark uses his house and calls the other diners sinners. Those wording differences are retained.
    Luke 5:29 Levi gave a great reception for Him in his house. A large crowd of tax collectors and others was reclining with them.

Mark 2:16

  Luke 5:30 — narrative parallel: Both have Pharisees and scribes object to the disciples about eating with tax collectors and sinners. Mark asks why He eats; Luke's question is why you eat and drink, so the grammatical subjects and added drinking are not made identical.
    Luke 5:30 The Pharisees and their scribes grumbled to His disciples, saying, “Why do you eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners?”

Mark 2:17

  Luke 5:31–5:32 — narrative parallel: Both answer through the healthy-and-sick physician analogy and Jesus' mission to call sinners rather than the righteous. Luke adds to repentance, which Mark's supplied verse does not include.
