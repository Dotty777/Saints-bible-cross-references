1 Maccabees 1:1
After Alexander son of Philip, the Macedonian who came from the land of Kittim, had defeated Darius king of the Persians and Medes, he became king in his place, having first ruled over Greece.
  1 Maccabees 6:2 — Narrative connection: The later account explicitly recalls Alexander son of Philip as the Macedonian king who first ruled the Greeks, identifying the ruler introduced here.

1 Maccabees 1:10
From them came a sinful root: Antiochus Epiphanes, son of King Antiochus, who had been a hostage in Rome. He became king in the one hundred and thirty-seventh year of the kingdom of the Greeks.
  2 Maccabees 4:7 — Historical parallel: Both accounts identify the accession of Antiochus called Epiphanes; 2 Maccabees places Jason's acquisition of the high priesthood at that transition.

1 Maccabees 1:11
In those days lawless sons came out of Israel and persuaded many, saying, “Let us go and make a covenant with the nations around us, because since we separated ourselves from them, many evils have overtaken us.”
  Exodus 23:32-33 — Law and practice: The proposed covenant with surrounding nations reverses the law's prohibition of covenants with them and its warning that their worship would ensnare Israel.

1 Maccabees 1:12
The proposal was good in their eyes.
  2 Maccabees 4:10-11 — Historical parallel: The favourable reception of foreign customs corresponds to Jason's successful introduction of the Greek manner of life and displacement of lawful civic practices.

1 Maccabees 1:13
Some of the people eagerly went to the king, and he gave them authority to follow the ordinances of the nations.
  2 Maccabees 4:9-11 — Historical parallel: Both accounts connect the adoption of Greek institutions with royal permission, while 2 Maccabees names Jason and specifies his financial proposal.

1 Maccabees 1:14
They built a gymnasium in Jerusalem according to the customs of the nations
  2 Maccabees 4:12 — Historical parallel: Both accounts describe the establishment of a gymnasium in Jerusalem; 2 Maccabees locates it beneath the citadel and names Jason as its sponsor.

1 Maccabees 1:15
and made foreskins for themselves. They abandoned the holy covenant, yoked themselves to the nations, and sold themselves to do evil.
  Genesis 17:10-14 — Law and practice: The reversal of circumcision is described as abandonment of the covenant; Genesis identifies circumcision in the flesh as its enduring sign.
  2 Maccabees 4:14-16 — Historical parallel: The abandonment of ancestral covenantal identity corresponds to priests neglecting the altar and preferring Greek distinctions in the companion account.

1 Maccabees 1:16
The kingdom was established before Antiochus, and he planned to reign over the land of Egypt so that he might rule both kingdoms.
  2 Maccabees 5:1 — Historical parallel: Both accounts report Antiochus campaigning in Egypt; 2 Maccabees explicitly calls its expedition his second, so the two chronological presentations are not collapsed.

1 Maccabees 1:20
After striking Egypt, Antiochus returned in the one hundred and forty-third year. He went up against Israel and came up to Jerusalem with a heavy force.
  2 Maccabees 5:11 — Historical parallel: Both accounts connect Antiochus's movement from Egypt with an armed assault on Jerusalem; 2 Maccabees adds the report of revolt as his motive.

1 Maccabees 1:21
He arrogantly entered the sanctuary and took the golden altar, the lampstand for light and all its utensils,
  2 Maccabees 5:15-16 — Historical parallel: The king's intrusion into the sanctuary and seizure of sacred equipment correspond to the companion account of his profane entry and removal of the vessels.

1 Maccabees 1:22
the table of presentation, the libation vessels, the bowls, the golden censers, the curtain, the crowns, and the gold ornament on the front of the temple. He stripped everything off.
  2 Maccabees 5:16 — Historical parallel: The detailed list of stripped sanctuary furnishings expands the companion account's statement that Antiochus swept away sacred vessels and dedicated gifts.

1 Maccabees 1:23
He took the silver, the gold, the precious vessels, and the hidden treasures that he found.
  2 Maccabees 5:21 — Historical parallel: The seizure of temple treasure corresponds to 2 Maccabees' report that Antiochus carried away eighteen hundred talents; the latter supplies an amount absent here.

1 Maccabees 1:24
Having taken everything, he went away to his own land. He committed bloodshed and spoke with great arrogance.
  2 Maccabees 5:21 — Historical parallel: Both accounts describe Antiochus's departure with temple wealth and characterize him by arrogance.

1 Maccabees 1:25
Great mourning came upon Israel in every place where they lived.
  1 Maccabees 2:14 — Narrative connection: Mattathias and his sons' mourning gives a concrete household instance of the widespread grief following the devastation of Israel.

1 Maccabees 1:29
After two full years, the king sent a chief collector of tribute to the cities of Judah. He came to Jerusalem with a heavy force.
  2 Maccabees 5:24-26 — Historical comparison: Both narratives describe a royal agent arriving with troops before a deceptive attack on Jerusalem; the titles and troop details differ, so this comparison does not equate the unnamed tribute collector with Apollonius.

1 Maccabees 1:30
He spoke peaceful words to them deceitfully, and they trusted him. Then he suddenly fell upon the city, struck it a great blow, and destroyed many people of Israel.
  2 Maccabees 5:25-26 — Historical comparison: Both narratives describe a commander feigning peace in Jerusalem before suddenly attacking its trusting inhabitants; 2 Maccabees adds the Sabbath setting. The unnamed tribute collector is not identified with Apollonius here.

1 Maccabees 1:31
He took the spoils of the city and burned it with fire. He demolished its houses and its surrounding walls.
  2 Maccabees 8:3 — Narrative comparison: The burning and demolition of Jerusalem provide the kind of destruction recalled in the companion account's prayer for the city being destroyed and levelled.

1 Maccabees 1:32
They took the women and children captive and took possession of the livestock.
  2 Maccabees 5:24 — Historical comparison: The capture of women and children corresponds to the royal order in 2 Maccabees to sell women and younger people, alongside the killing of adult men.

1 Maccabees 1:33
They fortified the city of David with a great, strong wall and strong towers, and it became their citadel.
  1 Maccabees 6:18-20 — Narrative connection: The fortified citadel established here remains a hostile base around the sanctuary and becomes the object of Judas's later siege.

1 Maccabees 1:34
They stationed there a sinful nation, lawless men, and strengthened themselves in it.
  1 Maccabees 6:21-24 — Narrative connection: The later appeal of the citadel's defenders and their lawless Israelite allies explains the continuing royal allegiance of the hostile garrison introduced here.

1 Maccabees 1:35
They stored weapons and food, gathered the spoils of Jerusalem, and deposited them there. They became a great trap.
  1 Maccabees 6:18-19 — Narrative connection: The armed and provisioned citadel described as a trap continues to support the nations and threaten the holy places, prompting Judas to besiege it.

1 Maccabees 1:36
It became an ambush against the sanctuary and an evil adversary to Israel continually.
  1 Maccabees 6:18 — Narrative connection: The citadel's continuing hostility to Israel and the sanctuary is restated when its occupants hem Israel in around the holy places.

1 Maccabees 1:37
They poured out innocent blood around the sanctuary and defiled the sanctuary.
  2 Maccabees 8:2-4 — Narrative comparison: The companion account's prayer recalls both a profaned temple and innocent blood crying to God, the two injuries joined in this verse.

1 Maccabees 1:38
Because of them the inhabitants of Jerusalem fled, and she became a dwelling of foreigners. She became foreign to those born in her, and her children abandoned her.
  1 Maccabees 3:45 — Narrative connection: The later description of Jerusalem as deserted by its native inhabitants and occupied by foreigners resumes the displacement reported here.

1 Maccabees 1:39
Her sanctuary was laid waste like a wilderness; her festivals were turned into mourning, her sabbaths into reproach, and her honour into contempt.
  2 Maccabees 6:6 — Historical parallel: The transformation of Sabbaths and festivals into occasions of reproach corresponds to the companion account's prohibition of Sabbath keeping and ancestral festivals.

1 Maccabees 1:40
In proportion to her glory, her dishonour multiplied, and her exaltation was turned into mourning.
  1 Maccabees 4:58 — Literary contrast: Within the book, the city's honour turning into shame is reversed by the rededication's great gladness and removal of the nations' reproach.

1 Maccabees 1:41
The king wrote to his whole kingdom that all should become one people
  Daniel 3:4-7 — Thematic comparison: Antiochus's demand for uniformity across his kingdom resembles Nebuchadnezzar's universal summons to common worship; these are distinct rulers and events.

1 Maccabees 1:42
and that each should abandon his own customs. All the nations accepted the king’s command.
  2 Maccabees 6:1 — Historical parallel: The demand to abandon local customs corresponds to the royal mission to make Jews depart from ancestral and divine laws.

1 Maccabees 1:43
Many from Israel consented to his worship, sacrificed to idols, and profaned the sabbath.
  Exodus 20:8-11 — Law and practice: The Israelites' profanation of the Sabbath violates the command to remember and sanctify the seventh day.

1 Maccabees 1:44
The king sent letters by messengers to Jerusalem and the cities of Judah, ordering them to follow customs foreign to the land,
  2 Maccabees 6:1 — Historical parallel: The royal letters imposing foreign customs correspond to the companion narrative's royal agent charged with ending observance of ancestral laws.

1 Maccabees 1:45
to prevent whole burnt offerings, sacrifice, and libation in the sanctuary, to profane sabbaths and festivals,
  2 Maccabees 6:6 — Historical parallel: The ban on Sabbath and festival observance is shared explicitly by the two accounts of religious persecution.
  Daniel 3:38 — Thematic comparison: The suppression of sanctuary sacrifices corresponds to the prayer's lament that no burnt offering, sacrifice, incense, or place of offering is available; no identity of historical setting is asserted.

1 Maccabees 1:46
to defile the sanctuary and holy people,
  2 Maccabees 6:2 — Historical parallel: The command to defile the sanctuary corresponds to the companion account's dedication of Jerusalem's temple to Olympian Zeus.

1 Maccabees 1:47
to build pagan altars, sacred precincts, and shrines for idols, and to sacrifice pigs and unclean livestock,
  Leviticus 11:7-8 — Law and practice: The order to sacrifice pigs deliberately introduces an animal the law explicitly classifies as unclean and forbids Israel to eat.

1 Maccabees 1:48
to leave their sons uncircumcised, and to make themselves abominable through every uncleanness and profanation,
  Genesis 17:10-14 — Law and practice: The order to leave sons uncircumcised directly opposes the covenant requirement that every male be circumcised.

1 Maccabees 1:49
so that they would forget the law and change all the ordinances.
  2 Maccabees 6:1 — Historical parallel: The stated aim of making Israel forget the law corresponds to the companion account's policy of compelling departure from God's laws.

1 Maccabees 1:50
Whoever did not act according to the king’s command was to die.
  2 Maccabees 6:9 — Historical parallel: Both accounts specify death for those who refuse the king's imposed Greek customs.

1 Maccabees 1:51
In accordance with all these commands he wrote to his whole kingdom. He appointed overseers over all the people and commanded the cities of Judah to sacrifice, city by city.
  2 Maccabees 6:8-9 — Historical parallel: The expansion of compulsory sacrifice through royal administration corresponds to the decree extending coercion to neighbouring cities.

1 Maccabees 1:52
Many of the people gathered to them, everyone who abandoned the law, and they did evil in the land.
  1 Maccabees 2:44 — Narrative connection: Those who abandon the law and join the royal policy become the lawless opponents against whom Mattathias's movement subsequently organizes resistance.

1 Maccabees 1:53
They drove Israel into hiding in all their places of refuge.
  1 Maccabees 2:29-31 — Narrative connection: The later account identifies families seeking righteousness who withdraw into wilderness hiding places because of the persecution summarized here.

1 Maccabees 1:54
On the fifteenth day of Chislev, in the one hundred and forty-fifth year, he built an abomination of desolation upon the altar. They built pagan altars in the surrounding cities of Judah.
  1 Maccabees 6:7 — Narrative connection: The later report to Antiochus explicitly recalls the abomination he built on Jerusalem's altar and announces its removal.

1 Maccabees 1:57
Whenever a book of the covenant was found in someone’s possession, or anyone consented to the law, the king’s decree put him to death.
  2 Maccabees 6:9 — Historical comparison: The death decree against possession of the covenant book or adherence to the law corresponds to the companion account's death penalty for refusing Greek customs; it does not separately attest the book searches.

1 Maccabees 1:59
On the twenty-fifth day of the month they offered sacrifice on the pagan altar that stood upon the altar.
  1 Maccabees 4:52-54 — Narrative connection: The twenty-fifth-day sacrifice on the pagan altar supplies the desecration anniversary that the later rededication explicitly reverses.

1 Maccabees 1:60
According to the decree, they put to death the women who had circumcised their children,
  2 Maccabees 6:10 — Historical parallel: Both accounts report women being executed because they circumcised their children.

1 Maccabees 1:61
hanging the infants from their necks; they also killed their households and those who had circumcised them.
  2 Maccabees 6:10 — Historical parallel: Both accounts describe infants hung from the condemned women; 2 Maccabees specifies two women and their being thrown from the wall, whereas this account also mentions households and circumcisers.

1 Maccabees 1:62
Many in Israel stood firm and fortified themselves against eating unclean things.
  2 Maccabees 6:18-20 — Historical exemplification: Eleazar's refusal to eat pork provides a named example of the resistance to unclean food described collectively here.
  Leviticus 11:7-8 — Law and practice: The refusal of unclean food preserves the dietary boundary expressed in the law's prohibition of pork.

1 Maccabees 1:63
They chose to die so that they would not be defiled by foods or profane the holy covenant, and they died.
  2 Maccabees 7:1-2 — Historical exemplification: The seven brothers and their mother exemplify the declared choice of death rather than transgression of ancestral food laws.

1 Maccabees 1:64
Very great wrath came upon Israel.
  2 Maccabees 6:12-16 — Thematic comparison: The companion account interprets the disasters as God's disciplinary anger while also insisting that He has not abandoned His people.

1 Maccabees 2:3
Simon, called Thassi;
  1 Maccabees 2:65 — Narrative connection — same-chapter: Within the chapter, the initial naming of Simon prepares for Mattathias's later designation of this son as the brothers' counsellor and father.

1 Maccabees 2:4
Judas, called Maccabeus;
  1 Maccabees 3:1 — Narrative connection: The son introduced as Judas Maccabeus succeeds Mattathias as leader at the beginning of the next chapter.

1 Maccabees 2:5
Eleazar, called Avaran; and Jonathan, called Apphous.
  1 Maccabees 6:43-46 — Narrative connection: The later battle account identifies Eleazar by the same epithet Avaran and recounts his death beneath an elephant.

1 Maccabees 2:6
He saw the blasphemies being committed in Judah and Jerusalem
  1 Maccabees 1:46-49 — Narrative connection: The royal program of sanctuary defilement, idolatry, and suppression of the law identifies the blasphemies that prompt Mattathias's lament.

1 Maccabees 2:7
and said, “Alas for me! Why was I born to see the ruin of my people and the ruin of the holy city, and to sit there while she was given into the hand of enemies, the sanctuary into the hand of foreigners?
  1 Maccabees 1:37-39 — Narrative connection: Mattathias's lament over a ruined holy city and sanctuary in foreign hands responds to the preceding account of bloodshed, foreign occupation, and desolation.

1 Maccabees 2:8
Her temple has become like a man without honour.
  1 Maccabees 1:39-40 — Literary connection: The lament's image of a dishonoured temple resumes the preceding description of the sanctuary's honour being turned into contempt.

1 Maccabees 2:9
The vessels of her glory were carried away captive; her little children were slain in her squares, her young men by the sword of an enemy.
  1 Maccabees 1:21-23 — Narrative connection: The captive vessels of glory in Mattathias's lament are the sanctuary furnishings and treasures previously seized by Antiochus.
  2 Maccabees 5:12-13 — Historical comparison: The lament over slain children and young men corresponds to the companion account's killing of people of every age during Jerusalem's sack.

1 Maccabees 2:10
What nation has not taken possession of her royal places and seized her spoils?
  1 Maccabees 1:31-35 — Narrative connection: The complaint about the nations seizing Jerusalem's spoils recalls the plundering of the city and storage of its spoils in the hostile citadel.

1 Maccabees 2:11
All her adornment was taken away; she that was free became a slave.
  1 Maccabees 1:22 — Literary connection: The lament that the city's adornment was removed recalls the literal stripping of the temple's crowns and gold ornament.

1 Maccabees 2:12
Behold! Our holy places, our beauty, and our glory have been laid waste, and the nations have profaned them.
  1 Maccabees 1:37-39 — Narrative connection: Mattathias's cry over profaned holy places summarizes the reported defilement and desolation of the sanctuary.

1 Maccabees 2:14
Mattathias and his sons tore their garments, put on sackcloth, and mourned greatly.
  1 Maccabees 4:38-39 — Narrative connection: The sons later repeat the tearing of garments and great lament when they see the sanctuary's ruin before cleansing it.

1 Maccabees 2:15
The king’s officials who were compelling apostasy came to the city of Modein to make the people sacrifice.
  1 Maccabees 1:51 — Narrative connection: The officials' arrival at Modein implements the earlier royal policy of sending overseers to compel sacrifice city by city.

1 Maccabees 2:18
Now be the first to come forward and carry out the king’s decree, as all the nations, the men of Judah, and those left in Jerusalem have done. You and your sons shall be among the king’s Friends, and you and your sons shall be honoured with silver, gold, and many gifts.”
  2 Maccabees 7:24 — Thematic comparison: The offer of royal friendship, wealth, and honour for abandoning ancestral worship resembles Antiochus's inducements to the youngest martyr; these are separate confrontations.

1 Maccabees 2:19
Mattathias answered in a loud voice, “Even if all the nations within the king’s realm obey him, each abandoning the worship of his fathers, and have chosen his commands,
  Daniel 3:16-18 — Thematic comparison: Mattathias's refusal to conform to royal worship parallels the three men's refusal to serve the king's idol despite a universal decree.

1 Maccabees 2:20
I and my sons and my brothers will walk in the covenant of our fathers.
  Joshua 24:15 — Literary comparison: Mattathias's declaration that he and his family will remain in the ancestral covenant resembles Joshua's resolve that he and his household will serve the Lord despite others' choices.

1 Maccabees 2:21
Far be it from us to abandon the law and the ordinances!
  2 Maccabees 7:30 — Thematic comparison: Both declarations reject a king's demand on the explicit ground of obedience to the ancestral law.

1 Maccabees 2:22
We will not obey the king’s words by turning aside from our worship to the right or to the left.”
  Deuteronomy 5:32-33 — Law and practice: Mattathias's refusal to turn right or left repeats the law's instruction to remain in the commanded way without deviation.

1 Maccabees 2:23
When he finished speaking these words, a Jewish man came forward in the sight of everyone to sacrifice upon the pagan altar in Modein, according to the king’s decree.
  1 Maccabees 1:51-52 — Narrative connection: The man's public sacrifice at Modein is a concrete instance of the royal sacrifice policy and the cooperation of Israelites who abandoned the law.

1 Maccabees 2:24
Mattathias saw and burned with zeal; his inward parts trembled. He gave vent to anger according to the judgment, ran forward, and slaughtered him upon the altar.
  Numbers 25:6-8 — Explicit ancestral comparison: Mattathias's zealous intervention follows the pattern of Phinehas rising to kill an offender before the assembly, a comparison made explicit two verses later.

1 Maccabees 2:25
At that time he also killed the king’s officer who was compelling sacrifice, and he pulled down the altar.
  Deuteronomy 7:5 — Law and practice: Mattathias's demolition of the pagan altar corresponds to the command to pull down the nations' altars; this target does not prescribe the killing of the royal officer.
