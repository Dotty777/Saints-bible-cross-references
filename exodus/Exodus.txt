Exodus 1:1
These are the names of the sons of Israel who entered Egypt with Jacob their father; each came with his whole household:
  Genesis 46:8 — Explicit migration recollection: Genesis introduces the names of Israel's sons entering Egypt with Jacob; Exodus resumes that same household migration with a corresponding introductory formula.
  Acts 7:14 — Explicit retrospective account: Stephen describes Joseph summoning Jacob and his relatives into Egypt, supplying the family movement that Exodus's opening household list recalls.

Exodus 1:2
Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah;
  Genesis 35:23 — Genealogical correspondence: Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah appear together among Leah's sons, identifying the four family heads named in the opening Egyptian household list.
  Genesis 46:8-12 — Explicit migration genealogy: The earlier migration catalogue identifies Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah and their family branches, supplying the Egyptian-entry genealogy summarized by these four names.

Exodus 1:3
Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin;
  Genesis 35:23-24 — Genealogical correspondence: Issachar and Zebulun are listed as Leah's sons and Benjamin as Rachel's son, identifying the three named brothers without imposing a different ordering on Exodus.
  Genesis 46:13-14 — Genealogical continuation: The migration catalogue records Issachar's and Zebulun's sons, supplying the household branches of the first two family heads named here.
  Genesis 46:21 — Genealogical continuation: The earlier migration catalogue supplies Benjamin's descendants, continuing the household branch of the third brother named here while retaining its own expanded family wording.

Exodus 1:4
Dan and Naphtali, Gad and Asher.
  Genesis 35:25-26 — Genealogical correspondence: Dan and Naphtali are Bilhah's sons, while Gad and Asher are Zilpah's, identifying the four brothers in Exodus without confusing their mothers.
  Genesis 46:16-18 — Genealogical continuation: The migration catalogue develops Gad's and Asher's family branches and identifies Zilpah's descendants, supplying household context for these two names.
  Genesis 46:23-25 — Genealogical continuation: Dan's and Naphtali's descendants appear in the earlier migration catalogue under Bilhah's family summary, supplying the branches of the other two brothers named here.

Exodus 1:5
Now Joseph was in Egypt; and all the souls descended from Jacob were seventy-five.
  Genesis 46:27 — Explicit migration-total correspondence: Genesis gives seventy-five for Jacob's Egyptian household and identifies Joseph's Egyptian-born descendants; Exodus retains seventy-five while noting Joseph's prior presence there.
  Acts 7:14 — Explicit retrospective total: Stephen likewise gives seventy-five relatives in Joseph's summons of Jacob's family, matching the supplied Exodus total without replacing it with another edition's count.
  Deuteronomy 10:22 — Related migration account with different totals: Deuteronomy recalls the fathers entering Egypt with seventy souls, whereas this Exodus text says seventy-five; the common migration setting is compared with both stated totals preserved.

Exodus 1:6
And Joseph died, and all his brothers, and all that generation.
  Genesis 50:26 — Explicit narrative continuation: Genesis closes with Joseph's death and burial rites in Egypt; Exodus includes his death in its notice that the earlier family generation has passed away.
  Acts 7:15 — Explicit retrospective account: Stephen summarizes Jacob's descent to Egypt and the deaths of Jacob and the fathers, corresponding to the passing of the ancestral generation narrated here.

Exodus 1:7
And the sons of Israel increased and multiplied, and became numerous and exceedingly, exceedingly strong; and the land multiplied them.
  Genesis 46:3 — Promise and narrative development: God had promised to make Jacob a great nation in Egypt; the multiplication and strength of Israel's descendants develop that promise in the migration's setting.
  Psalms 104:24 — Explicit retrospective account: The psalm recounts God's greatly increasing His people and making them stronger than their enemies, recalling the population growth and strength narrated here.
  Acts 7:17 — Explicit retrospective account: Stephen expressly recalls the people's growth and multiplication in Egypt as the time of the promise approached, summarizing the development stated here.

Exodus 1:8
And another king arose over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.
  Acts 7:18 — Explicit retrospective quotation: Stephen repeats the arising of another Egyptian king who did not know Joseph, retaining the distinctive transition that introduces Israel's oppression.
  Psalms 104:25 — Related retrospective transition: The psalm moves from Israel's increase to hostility and deceit against God's servants, corresponding to the changed Egyptian treatment following the new king's appearance without supplying his historical name.

Exodus 1:9
And he said to his nation, “Behold, the people of the sons of Israel are a great multitude, and are stronger than we are.
  Exodus 1:7 — Same-chapter narrated growth and royal response: The narrator has described Israel's great increase and strength; the king now presents that population as stronger than his own people, keeping his comparative claim within his speech.
  Psalms 104:24 — Explicit retrospective population theme: The psalm's account of God's people becoming stronger than their enemies corresponds to the strength the Egyptian king invokes as grounds for alarm.

Exodus 1:10
Come, therefore, let us deal craftily with them, lest they multiply; and when war befalls us, they also join our adversaries, wage war against us, and leave the land.”
  Psalms 104:25 — Explicit retrospective hostility: The psalm describes hatred of God's people and deceitful treatment of His servants, corresponding to the king's proposed crafty policy against Israel.
  Acts 7:19 — Explicit retrospective account: Stephen recounts the king's crafty mistreatment and exposure of infants, summarizing the policy whose proposed purpose here is to prevent Israel's further growth.

Exodus 1:11
He set overseers of labour over them to afflict them with their work. They built fortified cities for Pharaoh: Pithom, Rameses, and On, which is the City of the Sun.
  Exodus 5:6-14 — Developed forced-labour account: The later taskmaster and brick-quota narrative develops the coercive work regime introduced here, while its straw restrictions are not retroactively inserted into this first construction notice.
  Deuteronomy 26:6 — Explicit retrospective oppression: The later confession says the Egyptians afflicted, humbled and imposed hard labours on Israel, recalling the overseers and afflicting work described here.

Exodus 1:12
But the more they humbled them, the more they multiplied and grew exceedingly, exceedingly strong; and the Egyptians loathed the sons of Israel.
  Exodus 1:7 — Same-chapter intensified population repetition: Israel's increase and exceptional strength repeat the earlier growth notice, now expressly continuing despite Egyptian humiliation rather than ceasing under it.
  Psalms 104:24-25 — Explicit retrospective growth-and-hostility account: The psalm joins Israel's increase and strength with Egyptian hatred and deceit, corresponding to the conjunction of continued growth and loathing narrated here.

Exodus 1:13

  Deuteronomy 26:6 — Explicit retrospective oppression: The later confession's hard labours and Egyptian humiliation recall the forcible oppression that this verse attributes to the Egyptians.
  Exodus 6:5 — Oppression and divine response: God later speaks of hearing the groaning of the Israelites whom Egypt enslaves, identifying the oppressive condition to which the renewed deliverance speech responds.

Exodus 1:14
They made their lives painful with hard labour in clay and brickmaking, and with all the work in the plains, in every task in which they enslaved them by force.
  Exodus 5:7-9 — Developed brick-labour account: The later refusal of straw, unchanged brick quotas and heavier tasks develop the brickmaking burden introduced here without making every task identical in both passages.
  Deuteronomy 26:6 — Explicit retrospective labour account: The later historical confession recalls Egyptian affliction and hard labours, summarizing the painful clay, brick and field work described here.

Exodus 1:15
The king of the Egyptians spoke to the midwives of the Hebrews, one named Sepphora and the other Puah.
  Exodus 1:16-19 — Same-chapter named participants and ensuing exchange: The following command and questioning concern the midwives introduced here, identifying the women who resist the male-child order and answer the king; no identity with Moses' later wife is inferred from similar names.

Exodus 1:16

  Exodus 1:22 — Same-chapter expanded killing order: The later command extends the male-child killing policy to all Pharaoh's people and specifies the river, preserving the distinct midwife instruction and wider decree.
  Acts 7:19 — Explicit retrospective persecution: Stephen recalls the king forcing exposure of infants so they would not survive, summarizing the lethal treatment of children developed in these royal orders.

Exodus 1:17
But the midwives feared God. They did not do as the king of Egypt had commanded them, and they kept the boys alive.
  Exodus 1:20-21 — Same-chapter fear of God and ensuing treatment: The account next reports God's good treatment of the midwives and repeats their fear of God; the house-making clause retains its supplied agency rather than being rewritten as God building their houses.
  Acts 5:29 — Related obedience principle: The apostles' declaration that God must be obeyed rather than humans provides a later expression of the priority reflected in the midwives' fear of God and refusal of the king's killing command.

Exodus 1:18
And the king of Egypt called the midwives and said to them, “Why have you done this thing, and kept the male children alive?”
  Exodus 1:17 — Same-chapter action and royal questioning: The king's accusation that boys have been kept alive responds to the midwives' previously narrated refusal to obey him.
  Exodus 1:19 — Same-chapter question and reported answer: The midwives' childbirth explanation answers the king's question about the surviving boys; their statement is retained as their reported account rather than independently verified obstetric information.

Exodus 1:19
The midwives said to Pharaoh, “The Hebrew women are not like the Egyptian women, for they give birth before the midwives come to them.” And they bore children.
  Exodus 1:18 — Same-chapter answer to royal interrogation: The claim about Hebrew women's giving birth before attendance is the midwives' response to the king's accusation that they preserved the male children.
  Exodus 1:20 — Same-chapter reported answer and narrative sequel: The narrator follows the midwives' answer with God's good treatment of them and continued population growth, without separately verifying every generalization in their childbirth claim.

Exodus 1:20
God dealt well with the midwives, and the people multiplied and became very strong.
  Exodus 1:7 — Same-chapter population repetition: The people's continued multiplication and strength repeat the opening growth notice, showing that the intervening royal policy has not stopped the narrated increase.
  Exodus 1:17 — Same-chapter conduct and ensuing treatment: God's good treatment is narrated after the midwives' fear of God and preservation of the boys, keeping the particular conduct in view without adding an unreported universal ruling about every statement they made.

Exodus 1:21
And because the midwives feared God, they made houses for themselves.
  Exodus 1:17 — Same-chapter repeated fear of God: The account repeats that the midwives feared God, recalling the reason associated with their refusal to kill; the supplied statement that they made houses for themselves remains intact.

Exodus 1:22
And Pharaoh commanded all his people, saying, “Cast every male child born to the Hebrews into the river, but keep every female child alive.”

  Acts 7:19 — Explicit retrospective persecution: Stephen's account of infants exposed so they would not be kept alive recalls the lethal royal policy, without importing a different decree's wording into Exodus.
  Hebrews 11:23 — Royal decree and parental resistance: Hebrews recalls Moses' parents hiding him without fearing the king's decree, providing a retrospective account of resistance to the danger established here.

Exodus 2:1

  Numbers 26:59 — Explicit parental genealogy: Numbers identifies Jochebed with Levi and names Aaron, Moses and Miriam as her children by Amram, corresponding to the Levite parentage introduced here without independently reconstructing its more difficult ancestry clauses.

Exodus 2:2
She conceived and bore a male child. Seeing that he was beautiful, they hid him for three months.
  Acts 7:20 — Explicit retrospective birth account: Stephen recalls Moses' birth, beauty before God and three months in his father's house, matching the child and concealment period narrated here.
  Hebrews 11:23 — Explicit retrospective faith interpretation: Hebrews recalls the beautiful child hidden for three months by his parents and interprets their action as faith, retaining that later interpretation alongside Exodus's narration.

Exodus 2:3

  Exodus 2:5 — Same-chapter placement and discovery: Pharaoh's daughter finds the basket in the marsh where the mother placed it, connecting the protective placement with the narrated discovery.

Exodus 2:4
His sister watched from a distance to learn what would happen to him.

Exodus 2:5

  Exodus 2:3 — Same-chapter discovery and earlier placement: The mother's basket placement in the marsh explains the object and location encountered by Pharaoh's daughter and her attendant.

Exodus 2:6

  Exodus 2:10 — Same-chapter compassion and adoption outcome: The child recognized as Hebrew and pitied here later becomes Pharaoh's daughter's son, carrying the rescue into an explicit adoption notice.

Exodus 2:7
And his sister said to Pharaoh’s daughter, “Do you want me to call a nursing woman from among the Hebrews, that she may nurse the child for you?”

  Exodus 2:8-9 — Same-chapter proposal and implementation: The sister calls the child's mother, and Pharaoh's daughter commissions her to nurse him and promises wages, providing the particular outcome of the nursing proposal without inventing a narrated payment.

Exodus 2:8

Exodus 2:9

Exodus 2:10

Exodus 2:12

Exodus 2:13

Exodus 2:14

Exodus 2:19
