Lamentations 1:1
After Israel was taken captive and Jerusalem laid waste, Jeremiah sat weeping and lamented this lament over Jerusalem, saying: How the city once full of peoples sat alone! She has become like a widow, she who was populous among the nations. A ruler among the regions has become subject to tribute.
  2 Kings 25:8-12 — Historical setting of Jerusalem's devastation: The lament's preface names Israel's captivity and Jerusalem's devastation; Kings narrates the burning of Jerusalem and removal of its remaining people. This supplies the historical catastrophe over which the city is personified as bereaved.

Lamentations 1:2
Weeping, she wept in the night, her tears upon her cheeks. Among all those who love her there is no one to comfort her. All her friends have dealt faithlessly with her; they have become her enemies.
  Jeremiah 37:14 — Abandonment by former friends: Jerusalem's friends act faithlessly and leave her without comfort; Jeremiah describes the afflicted people forgotten by all their friends, who will not inquire after them. Both portray stricken people abandoned by those expected to help.

Lamentations 1:3
Judea was deported because of her humiliation and her great slavery. She dwelt among the nations; she found no rest. All her pursuers overtook her among those who afflicted her.
  Deuteronomy 28:64-65 — Exile and restlessness: Judea lives among nations without finding rest; Deuteronomy warns of scattering among nations with no rest or resting place for the foot. The lament repeats the exile-and-restlessness pattern of the warning.

Lamentations 1:4
Zion’s roads mourn because no one comes at the festival. All her gates are destroyed; her priests groan. Her virgins are led away, and she is embittered within herself.
  Lamentations 2:6 — Festival life brought to an end: Zion's roads mourn because nobody comes for the festival; the next lament says the Lord destroyed His festival and forgot festival and Sabbath in Zion. Both describe the loss of the city's gathered worship.

Lamentations 1:5
Those afflicting her have become the head, and her enemies prospered, because the Lord humbled her for her many acts of ungodliness. Her infants went into captivity before the oppressor.
  Deuteronomy 28:41 — Children taken into captivity: The lament connects Judah's ungodliness with her infants going into captivity; Deuteronomy warns that sons and daughters will go into captivity rather than remain with their parents.

Lamentations 1:6
All her beauty has departed from Daughter Zion. Her rulers have become like rams finding no pasture; they went without strength before the pursuer.
  2 Kings 25:4-7 — Rulers overtaken by pursuers: Zion's rulers go weakly before the pursuer; Kings describes the fleeing king overtaken by the Chaldean army, with his force scattering away from him. The narrative supplies a concrete parallel to the lament's defeated leadership.

Lamentations 1:7
Jerusalem remembered the days of her humiliation and rejection, all her desired things from ancient days. When her people fell into the oppressor’s hands and no one helped her, her enemies saw her and laughed at her deportation.
  Psalms 78:4 — Ridicule of devastated Jerusalem: Jerusalem's enemies laugh at her deportation; the psalm of the ravaged city describes its people becoming the neighbors' sneering and ridicule. Both add humiliation by onlookers to the city's material losses.

Lamentations 1:8
Jerusalem has sinned grievously; therefore she has come into upheaval. All who glorified her humbled her, for they saw her shame. She too groaned and turned backward.
  Ezekiel 16:36-39 — Jerusalem's exposed shame: The lament links Jerusalem's sin with former admirers seeing her shame; Ezekiel portrays Jerusalem's lawlessness being exposed before her lovers, who strip her and leave her exposed. The passages share the city's personified humiliation.

Lamentations 1:9
Her uncleanness is at her feet; she did not remember her last things. He brought her down exceedingly; there is no one to comfort her. See, Lord, my humiliation, for the enemy has grown great!
  Lamentations 1:16 — Same-chapter uncomforted suffering refrain: Both verses say the comforter is absent while the enemy has grown strong. The repeated combination links the city's plea to be seen with her later explanation of her tears.

Lamentations 1:10
The oppressor spread out his hand over all her desired things. She saw nations enter her sanctuary, those whom Thou didst command not to enter Thine assembly.
  Psalms 78:1 — Nations entering the sanctuary: The lament sees foreign nations entering the sanctuary and taking desired things; the psalm likewise says nations entered God's inheritance and defiled His holy temple in Jerusalem.

Lamentations 1:11
All her people groan, seeking bread. They gave her desired things for food to restore life. See, Lord, and behold, for I have become dishonored!
  2 Kings 25:3 — Jerusalem's famine: The people seek bread and exchange desired possessions for food; Kings records famine so severe in besieged Jerusalem that no loaves remained for the people. The narrative explains the crisis behind the lament's desperate search for food.

Lamentations 1:12
Not to you, all who pass along the road! Turn and see whether there is pain like my pain, which came to be. Speaking against me, the Lord humbled me on the day of His furious anger.
  Lamentations 2:15 — Contrasting responses from passersby: The suffering city asks road-passers to turn and see her pain; later those passing along the road clap, hiss and shake their heads at Jerusalem. The repeated audience responds with mockery rather than the requested recognition of suffering.

Lamentations 1:13
From His height He sent fire; He brought it down into my bones. He spread a net for my feet; He turned me backward. He made me desolate, suffering pain all day long.
  Ezekiel 12:13 — Divine judgment as a capturing net: The Lord spreads a net for the lamenting city's feet; Ezekiel says He will spread His net over the ruler and bring him to Babylon. Both represent judgment and capture through a divine net, at different personal scales.

Lamentations 1:14
A watch was kept over my acts of ungodliness; they were intertwined in my hands and rose upon my neck. My strength weakened, because the Lord put pains into my hands; I will not be able to stand.
  Psalms 37:4-5 — Sin as an overwhelming bodily burden: The lament's acts of ungodliness rise upon the neck as strength fails; the psalm describes lawless deeds rising above the head and weighing like a heavy load. The connection preserves the lament's wording without inserting an unmentioned yoke.

Lamentations 1:15
The Lord removed all my strong men from my midst. He called a time against me to crush my chosen men. The Lord trod a winepress for the virgin Daughter Judah; for these things I weep.
  Isaiah 63:2-3 — Judgment pictured as a winepress: The Lord treads a winepress for Daughter Judah; Isaiah depicts wrathful trampling through the image of clothes from a trodden winepress. The violent judgment image is shared while the identified recipients differ.

Lamentations 1:16
My eye brought down water, for the one who comforts me, who restores my life, is far from me. My sons have been destroyed, for the enemy has grown strong.
  Jeremiah 38:15-16 — Weeping over lost children: The lamenting city's tears accompany destroyed sons and an absent comforter; Jeremiah portrays Rachel weeping over sons who are no more, followed by a promise of return from enemy territory. Both connect parental lament with the loss of children.

Lamentations 1:17
Zion spread out her hands; there is no one to comfort her. The Lord commanded concerning Jacob; around him are those who afflict him. Jerusalem has become like a woman sitting apart among them.
  Lamentations 2:19 — Hand gestures in communal lament: Zion stretches out her hands without finding comfort; the later appeal urges lifted hands before the Lord for the lives of starving infants. Both place outstretched or lifted hands within the distressed city's appeal for help.

Lamentations 1:18
The Lord is righteous, for I embittered His mouth. Hear now, all you peoples, and see my pain. My virgins and my young men went into captivity.
  Nehemiah 9:33 — Confession of divine righteousness: The lament confesses that the Lord is righteous while the speaker admits embittering His mouth; Nehemiah's communal prayer likewise confesses God's righteousness in the calamities and the people's grievous sin.

Lamentations 1:19
I called my lovers, but they deceived me. My priests and my elders failed in the city, for they sought food for themselves to restore their lives, and found none.
  Jeremiah 37:14 — Failed appeal to former allies: The city calls her lovers but is deceived; Jeremiah says the afflicted people's friends forgot them and will not inquire. Both deny the hoped-for aid from former companions while the city suffers.

Lamentations 1:20
See, Lord, how I am afflicted! My belly is troubled, and my heart has turned within me, for I have bitterly provoked. Outside the sword bereaved me, like death in the house.
  Ezekiel 7:15 — Sword outside and death inside: The lament contrasts bereavement by the sword outside with death inside the house; Ezekiel explicitly places the sword outside and famine and death inside the city. The paired inside-outside threats closely correspond.

Lamentations 1:21
Hear now that I groan; there is no one to comfort me. All my enemies heard of my calamities and rejoiced because Thou didst do it. Thou didst bring a day, Thou didst call a time, and they became like me.
  Jeremiah 37:16 — Reversal upon the oppressors: The enemies rejoice over the city's calamity but are described as becoming like her; Jeremiah declares that devourers will be devoured and plunderers become plunder. Both portray oppressors receiving the treatment they inflicted.

Lamentations 1:22
May all their wickedness come before Thee, and glean them as they made a gleaning for all my sins; for my groans are many and my heart is grieved.
  Psalms 78:11-12 — Groaning joined to a plea for repayment: The lament asks that the enemies' wickedness come before God and be repaid amid many groans; the psalm asks God to hear captives' groaning and repay the neighbors' insults. Both join distress to a petition for retribution.

Lamentations 2:1
How the Lord has darkened Daughter Zion in His anger! He hurled Israel’s glory from heaven to earth and did not remember His footstool on the day of His anger.
  1 Chronicles 28:2 — Sanctuary as the place of God's feet: The Lord does not remember His footstool in Zion's catastrophe; David describes the planned ark-house as a standing place for the Lord's feet. This supports the sanctuary imagery in the lament without describing a literal divine body.

Lamentations 2:2
The Lord drowned without sparing all Jacob’s beautiful things. In His fury He brought down Daughter Judah’s strongholds; He pressed them to the earth and profaned her king and her rulers.
  Jeremiah 52:13-14 — Destruction of the royal and fortified city: The lament portrays Judah's strongholds cast down and her king and rulers profaned; Jeremiah records the burning of the royal house and demolition of Jerusalem's wall. The narrative supplies concrete counterparts to the collapse of the fortified royal city.

Lamentations 2:3
In His furious anger He broke every horn of Israel. He turned back His right hand before the enemy and kindled a flame in Jacob like fire, consuming everything around.
  Lamentations 4:11 — Repeated divine fire against the city: The Lord's anger kindles consuming fire in Jacob; the later lament says His furious anger kindled fire in Zion that consumed her foundations. Both descriptions make the city's burning an expression of divine fury.

Lamentations 2:4
He drew His bow like an enemy and made His right hand firm like an opponent. He killed everything desired by my eyes in Daughter Zion’s tent; He poured out His fury like fire.
  Isaiah 63:10 — God portrayed as an opponent: The Lord draws His bow like an enemy and makes His right hand firm like an opponent; Isaiah says the people's disobedience caused Him to turn hostile and fight them. Both portray divine opposition toward His own people.

Lamentations 2:5
The Lord became like an enemy; He drowned Israel. He drowned all her citadels and destroyed his strongholds; He multiplied humiliation and abasement for Daughter Judah.
  Jeremiah 21:5 — The Lord fighting against the city: The lament calls the Lord an enemy who destroys Israel's citadels; Jeremiah records His declaration that He Himself will fight the people with mighty arm, fury and indignation. Both attribute the catastrophe to divine opposition.

Lamentations 2:6
He spread out His dwelling like a vine and destroyed His festival. The Lord forgot what He had made in Zion of festival and Sabbath; in His angry indignation He provoked king, priest and ruler.
  Hosea 2:13 — Festivals and Sabbaths removed: The lament says festival and Sabbath are forgotten in Zion under God's anger; Hosea announces the removal of rejoicing, festivals, new moons, Sabbaths and assemblies. Both make the loss of festal worship part of judgment.

Lamentations 2:7
The Lord rejected His altar and shook off His sanctuary. He shattered the wall of her citadels in the enemy’s hand. They raised a shout in the Lord’s house as on a festival day.
  Psalms 73:3-8 — Enemies profaning the sanctuary and feast: Enemies raise a shout in the rejected sanctuary as on a festival day; the psalm describes enemies boasting within God's feast, tearing down the sanctuary and burning it. Both invert the sacred celebration through hostile intrusion.

Lamentations 2:8
The Lord turned to destroy Daughter Zion’s wall. He stretched out a measure; He did not withdraw His hand from trampling. The outer defense mourned, and the wall weakened together with it.
  2 Kings 21:13 — A measure used for Jerusalem's judgment: The Lord stretches out a measure while destroying Zion's wall; Kings announces that He will stretch Samaria's measure over Jerusalem. Both use measurement as an image of deliberate judgment upon the city.

Lamentations 2:9
Her gates were fixed in the earth; He destroyed and broke her bars. Her king and her rulers are among the nations. There is no law; even her prophets saw no vision from the Lord.
  Psalms 73:9 — Loss of prophetic guidance: The lament says the prophets saw no vision from the Lord; the sanctuary lament in Psalms says no signs were seen and there is no prophet any longer. Both include absent prophetic guidance among the community's losses.

Lamentations 2:10
Daughter Zion’s elders sat on the ground in silence. They put dust on their heads and girded themselves with sackcloth. They brought the leading virgins in Jerusalem down to the ground.
  Job 2:12-13 — Silent mourning with dust and torn or mourning clothes: Zion's elders sit silently with dust on their heads and sackcloth; Job's friends weep, tear their robes, sprinkle earth on themselves and sit without speaking. The gestures of bodily abasement and silence express overwhelming grief in both scenes.

Lamentations 2:11
My eyes failed with tears; my heart was troubled. My glory was poured onto the ground at the shattering of the daughter of my people, as infant and nursing child failed in the city’s squares.
  Jeremiah 8:21-23 — Tears over the shattered daughter of the people: The speaker's tears and inward distress arise from the shattering of the daughter of his people; Jeremiah likewise joins that daughter's crushing with inward pain and a wish for eyes flowing with tears.

Lamentations 2:12
They said to their mothers, “Where are grain and wine?” They weakened like the wounded in the city’s squares, and their lives poured out into their mothers’ bosoms.
  Lamentations 4:4 — Children asking for unavailable food: Children ask their mothers for grain and wine as they weaken; the later lament depicts nursing children thirsty and infants asking for bread with nobody to break it. Both focus the famine through children's unmet requests.

Lamentations 2:13
What can I testify to you, or what can I liken to you, Daughter Jerusalem? Who will save you and comfort you, virgin Daughter Zion? For the cup of your crushing has grown great; who will heal you?
  Jeremiah 8:22 — Rhetorical question about healing the people: The lament asks who can heal Jerusalem's great crushing; Jeremiah asks whether there is resin or a physician and why the daughter of the people is not healed. Both express the community's apparently unrelieved injury through a healing question.

Lamentations 2:14
Your prophets saw futile things and folly for you. They did not uncover your injustice to turn back your captivity; they saw for you futile pronouncements and expulsions.
  Jeremiah 23:16-22 — False prophecy failing to turn people from evil: The prophets' futile visions failed to uncover injustice and avert captivity; Jeremiah condemns invented visions and says true hearing would have turned the people from evil practices. Both connect false prophecy with failure to confront wrongdoing.

Lamentations 2:15
All who pass along the road clapped their hands at you. They hissed and shook their heads at Daughter Jerusalem: “Is this the city they will call a crown of glory, the gladness of all the earth?”
  Psalms 47:2-3 — Jerusalem's praise turned into mockery: Passersby mock Jerusalem by asking whether this is the city they will call the gladness of all the earth. The psalm praises God's city and Zion as exultation for all the earth; that celebratory description becomes a taunt in the lament.

Lamentations 2:16
All your enemies opened their mouths against you. They hissed and gnashed their teeth; they said, “We have swallowed her! Indeed, this is the day we expected; we found it, we saw it!”
  Psalms 34:21-25 — Enemies opening their mouths and claiming to swallow: Jerusalem's enemies open their mouths and boast that they swallowed her. The psalm reports enemies opening their mouths and exulting at what they see, then prays that they not boast of swallowing the sufferer. The actual and feared taunts closely correspond.

Lamentations 2:17
The Lord did what He intended; He fulfilled His words, which He commanded from ancient days. He brought down without sparing; He made the enemy rejoice over you and exalted your oppressor’s horn.
  Jeremiah 39:23-24 — Calamity fulfilling the Lord's words: The lament says the Lord carried out His intention and fulfilled His ancient words; Jeremiah describes the city handed to Chaldeans through sword and famine and says that what God spoke happened. Both interpret the catastrophe as fulfillment of His warning.

Lamentations 2:18
Their heart cried to the Lord: Walls of Zion, bring down tears like torrents day and night! Give yourself no sobering pause; daughter, may your eye not fall silent.
  Jeremiah 8:23 — Day-and-night tears for the people: Zion is urged to let tears flow day and night without pause; Jeremiah wishes for a head of water and fountain-like eyes to weep day and night for the wounded people. The unceasing-tears imagery closely corresponds.

Lamentations 2:19
Rise, exult in the night at the beginning of your watch! Pour out your heart like water before the Lord. Lift your hands to Him for the lives of your infants, who weaken with hunger at the head of every way out.
  Psalms 61:9 — Pouring out the heart before God: The lament urges the people to pour out the heart like water before the Lord; the psalm tells the whole assembly to pour out their hearts before Him. Both describe open communal prayer through the same pouring metaphor.

Lamentations 2:20
See, Lord, and look: whom hast Thou gleaned in this way? Will women eat the fruit of their womb? A butcher made a gleaning; will infants nursing at breasts be murdered? Wilt Thou kill priest and prophet in the Lord’s sanctuary?
  Lamentations 4:10 — The horror of children becoming food: The lament asks whether women will eat the fruit of their womb; the later lament states that compassionate women's hands boiled their children for food during the people's shattering. The later statement provides the explicit counterpart to the question.

Lamentations 2:21
Child and elder lay at the way out. My virgins and my young men went into captivity. With sword and famine Thou didst kill; on the day of Thine anger Thou didst slaughter without sparing.
  2 Chronicles 36:17 — Young and old caught in Jerusalem's fall: The lament describes child and elder lying at the way out and young people taken captive; Chronicles describes young men killed, virgins shown no mercy and elders carried away. Both portray the catastrophe across age groups while preserving different details.

Lamentations 2:22
He called a festival day of my sojourning from all around. On the day of the Lord’s anger there was neither one rescued nor one left, as I prevailed and multiplied all my enemies.
  Lamentations 1:15 — A summoned occasion becomes judgment: A festival day is called, but on the Lord's day of anger nobody is rescued; the earlier lament says He called a time against Judah to crush its chosen men. Both turn the language of a summoned occasion toward the city's calamity.

Lamentations 3:1
I am the man who sees poverty under the rod of His fury against me.
  Psalms 37:2 — parallel divine discipline: The speaker experiences the rod of divine fury; the psalm likewise petitions the Lord not to discipline him in anger.

Lamentations 3:2
He took me and led me away into darkness, not light.
  Job 19:8 — shared darkness imagery: Being led into darkness rather than light parallels Job's account of God placing darkness before his face amid confinement.

Lamentations 3:3
Indeed, against me He turned His hand all day.
  Psalms 37:3 — shared oppressive-hand imagery: The divine hand repeatedly turns against the speaker; the psalm similarly describes God's hand pressing upon the sufferer.

Lamentations 3:4
He made my flesh and my skin old; He shattered my bones.
  Job 19:20 — parallel bodily deterioration: The source joins damaged flesh, skin and bones; Job likewise depicts flesh decaying within skin and bones in a disordered bodily image.

Lamentations 3:5
He built against me, surrounded my head and toiled.
  Job 19:12 — shared encirclement imagery: The source describes God building against and surrounding the speaker; Job likewise describes trials and ambushers surrounding him on his roads.

Lamentations 3:6
He made me dwell in dark places like the dead of ages.
  Psalms 142:3 — close verbal parallel: Both describe being made to dwell in dark places like people long dead, presenting present affliction through the imagery of death.

Lamentations 3:7
He built against me, and I will not go out; He made my bronze heavy.
  Psalms 87:9 — parallel confinement: The source cannot go out from the enclosure built against him; the psalm likewise says the sufferer was handed over and did not go out.

Lamentations 3:8
Indeed, I will cry and call out; He blocked my prayer.
  Psalms 21:3 — parallel unanswered cry: The source cries while God blocks prayer; the psalm similarly describes crying by day without being heard.

Lamentations 3:9
He built up my roads, blocked my paths and troubled them.
  Job 19:8 — shared blocked-way imagery: Built-up roads and blocked paths prevent movement in the source; Job likewise says he is walled in and cannot pass through.

Lamentations 3:10
He is a bear lying in wait for me, a lion in hidden places.
  Hosea 13:7-8 — shared divine predator imagery: God is pictured as a waiting bear and hidden lion in the source; Hosea likewise describes divine confrontation through predatory cats and a distressed bear.

Lamentations 3:11
He pursued me when I turned away and brought me to a stop; He made me desolate.
  Lamentations 1:13 — repeated divine desolation: Both describe the speaker as made desolate by divine action; the earlier lament also describes being turned backward amid entrapment.

Lamentations 3:12
He drew His bow and fixed me as a target for a missile.
  Job 16:12 — shared target imagery: The source is fixed as the target of a drawn bow; Job likewise says God set him up like a target after tearing him apart.

Lamentations 3:13
He drove the arrows of His quiver into my kidneys.
  Job 16:13 — shared attack on kidneys: Arrows enter the kidneys in the source; Job depicts attackers surrounding him with spears and striking his kidneys without sparing.

Lamentations 3:14
I became a laughingstock to all my people, their psalm all day.
  Psalms 68:12-13 — shared mocking-song imagery: The speaker becomes his people's laughingstock and daily psalm; the psalmist likewise becomes a proverb and the subject of wine-drinkers' songs.

Lamentations 3:15
He filled me with bitterness and made me drunk with gall.
  Jeremiah 9:14 — shared bitter nourishment imagery: God fills the speaker with bitterness and gall; Jeremiah similarly announces God feeding the people distresses and giving them gall-water to drink.

Lamentations 3:16
He knocked out my teeth with a pebble and fed me ashes.
  Psalms 101:10 — shared ashes-as-food imagery: The source is fed ashes amid bodily humiliation; the psalm likewise depicts eating ashes like bread as an expression of affliction.

Lamentations 3:17
He drove my soul away from peace; I forgot good things.
  Psalms 37:4 — parallel absence of peace: The source's soul is driven from peace; the psalm likewise says there is no peace for the sufferer's bones under divine anger.

Lamentations 3:18
I said, “My victory has perished, and my hope from the Lord.”
  Job 19:10 — parallel destroyed hope: The speaker declares his hope from the Lord perished; Job similarly describes God cutting off his hope like a tree.

Lamentations 3:19
I remembered, from my poverty and my persecution, my bitterness and gall.
  Lamentations 3:15 — same-chapter recollection of bitterness: The remembered bitterness and gall explicitly return to the bitter nourishment described earlier in the chapter.

Lamentations 3:20
My soul will remember and brood over me.
  Psalms 142:4-5 — parallel troubled remembrance: The soul remembers and broods in the source; the psalm joins a troubled heart and despondent spirit to remembering earlier days and meditating on God's works.

Lamentations 3:21
I will set this in my heart; therefore I will endure.
  Psalms 41:6 — parallel inward turn toward endurance: The speaker deliberately sets a matter in his heart and resolves to endure; the psalm similarly addresses the grieving soul with an instruction to hope in God.

Lamentations 3:25
The Lord is good to those who wait for Him, to the soul that will seek Him as good
  Psalms 26:13-14 — parallel waiting for divine goodness: The source calls the Lord good to those who wait and seek; the psalm joins confidence in seeing His good things with repeated instruction to wait for Him.

Lamentations 3:26
and will endure and be quiet for the Lord’s salvation.
  Psalms 61:2-3 — related submission and salvation: Quiet endurance for the Lord's salvation corresponds to the psalm's submitting soul that locates its salvation and support in God.

Lamentations 3:27
It is good for a man when he takes up a yoke in his youth.
  Sirach 6:18-25 — related discipline from youth: Taking a yoke in youth is called good; Sirach likewise urges youthful instruction and portrays accepting wisdom through fetters, a neck collar and a carried burden.

Lamentations 3:28
He will sit alone and be silent, because he has taken it upon himself.
  Jeremiah 15:17 — parallel solitary response to suffering: The source sits alone and silent under his burden; Jeremiah likewise sits alone before God's hand because he is filled with bitterness.

Lamentations 3:30
He will give his cheek to the one striking him; he will be filled with reproaches.
  Isaiah 50:6 — close cheek-and-reproach parallel: The source gives his cheek to the striker and receives reproach; Isaiah's speaker likewise gives his cheeks to slaps and refuses to turn from shame.

Lamentations 3:31
For the Lord will not reject for ever.
  Psalms 76:8-10 — answer to a parallel lament question: The source denies that the Lord rejects forever; the psalm asks whether rejection and the withholding of mercy can last forever.

Lamentations 3:32
For the One who humbled will have compassion according to His abundant mercy.
  Psalms 102:8-13 — parallel mercy after anger: The One who humbled will show abundant mercy in the source; the psalm likewise combines abundant mercy with the assurance that God's anger does not persist forever.

Lamentations 3:34
To humble under one’s feet all the prisoners of the earth,
  Psalms 145:7 — contrast in treatment of prisoners: Humbling prisoners underfoot belongs to the actions disavowed in the source's following lines; the psalm instead presents the Lord as releasing people in fetters.

Lamentations 3:35
to turn aside a man’s judgment before the face of the Most High,
  Exodus 23:6 — parallel rejection of perverted judgment: Turning aside a person's judgment before the Most High is condemned in this sequence; Exodus expressly forbids perverting the poor person's judgment.

Lamentations 3:36
to condemn a person when he is judged—the Lord did not say to do this.
  Exodus 23:7 — parallel rejection of unjust condemnation: The source denies divine authorization for condemning a person in the described unjust judgment; Exodus forbids unrighteous charges and killing the innocent and righteous.

Lamentations 3:38
will not calamities and good come from the mouth of the Most High?
  Job 2:10 — parallel reception of good and calamity: The source asks whether calamities and good come from the Most High; Job likewise asks whether recipients of God's good things should also bear bad things.

Lamentations 3:40
Our way was searched and examined; let us return to the Lord.
  Joel 2:12-13 — parallel call to return: Examining the people's way leads to a call to return to the Lord; Joel likewise calls the people to turn to God with their whole hearts.

Lamentations 3:41
Let us lift our hearts upon our hands to the Exalted One in heaven.
  Lamentations 2:19 — repeated heart-and-hands prayer imagery: The source raises hearts upon hands toward heaven; the earlier prayer likewise joins pouring out the heart before God with lifting hands to Him.

Lamentations 3:42
We have sinned and acted godlessly, and Thou wast not propitiated.
  Daniel 9:5-8 — parallel communal confession: Both confess communal sin and godlessness in prayer; Daniel also locates the resulting shame among Judah, Jerusalem and their leaders.

Lamentations 3:43
Thou didst cover Thyself with fury and drive us away; Thou didst kill without sparing.
  Lamentations 2:21 — repeated killing without sparing: The source accuses God of killing without sparing in fury; the earlier lament likewise describes slaughter without sparing on the day of His anger.

Lamentations 3:45
to close my eyes; Thou didst place us to be rejected among the peoples.
  Psalms 43:14-15 — parallel rejection among peoples: The source says God placed the people in rejection among other peoples; the psalm likewise describes God making His people a reproach and ridicule among nations.

Lamentations 3:46
All our enemies opened their mouths against us.
  Lamentations 2:16 — close verbal repetition: Both describe all the enemies opening their mouths against the afflicted people; the earlier passage expands the image with hissing and hostile triumph.

Lamentations 3:48
My eye will bring down outpourings of water at the shattering of the daughter of my people.
  Jeremiah 8:23 — close tears-for-the-people parallel: The eye pours water at the people's shattering; the Jeremiah passage likewise asks for eyes as a fountain of tears to mourn the wounded daughter of the people.

Lamentations 3:49
My eye was swallowed up, and I will not be silent, for there is no recovery,
  Jeremiah 14:17 — parallel unceasing lament: The speaker's eye was swallowed up, yet he will not be silent because there is no recovery; Jeremiah similarly commands unceasing tears for the people's painful crushing.

Lamentations 3:50
until the Lord bends down and sees from heaven.
