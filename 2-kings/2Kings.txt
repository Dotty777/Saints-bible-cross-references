2 Kings 1:1
Moab broke faith with Israel after Ahab died.
  2 Kings 3:4-5 — Narrative expansion: Moab breaks with Israel after Ahab dies: The opening notice places Moab’s break with Israel after Ahab’s death; the later account supplies Mesha’s preceding deliveries of lambs and rams and repeats the break after Ahab died.

2 Kings 1:2
Ahaziah fell through the lattice in his upper room in Samaria and became ill. He sent messengers and said to them, ‘Come, and inquire of Baal Fly, the god of Ekron, whether I shall recover from this illness of mine.’ And they went to inquire through him.
  2 Kings 1:16 — Same-chapter literary parallel: the inquiry to Baal Fly is named in the judgment: Ahaziah sends messengers to ask Baal Fly about recovery from his illness; Elijah’s final address expressly challenges that inquiry and announces death from the bed, linking the particular action with the oracle against it.

2 Kings 1:3
An angel of the Lord spoke to Elijah the Tishbite, saying, ‘Rise, come to meet the messengers of Ahaziah king of Samaria, and say to them: Is it because there is no God in Israel that you are going to inquire of Baal Fly, the god of Ekron?’
  2 Kings 1:6 — Same-chapter literary parallel: the challenge reaches the king through the messengers: The angel directs Elijah to challenge the messengers about seeking Baal Fly despite God in Israel; their report repeats that challenge to Ahaziah, with the death oracle additionally supplied.

2 Kings 1:4
‘It shall not be so! For thus says the Lord: The bed onto which you have gone up—you will not come down from it, for there you will surely die.’ And Elijah went and spoke to them.
  2 Kings 1:17 — Same-chapter prophecy and fulfilment: the announced death is reported: The Lord’s oracle through Elijah says Ahaziah will not descend from his bed but will die; the death notice expressly says he died according to the word Elijah had spoken.

2 Kings 1:5
The messengers returned to him, and he said to them, ‘Why have you returned?’
  2 Kings 1:3-4 — Same-chapter narrative context: the interrupted inquiry explains the early return: The king asks why his messengers have returned; the earlier account records Elijah’s interception and death oracle, providing the particular interruption behind the return without inventing a visit to Ekron.

2 Kings 1:6
They said to him, ‘A man came up to meet us and said to us: Come, return to the king who sent you and tell him, Thus says the Lord: Is it because there is no God in Israel that you are going to inquire of Baal Fly, the god of Ekron? It shall not be so! The bed onto which you have gone up—you will not come down from it, for you will surely die.’
  2 Kings 1:3-4 — Same-chapter literary parallel: the messengers repeat the divine challenge and oracle: Their report repeats both the challenge about God in Israel and Baal Fly and the bed-and-death warning given through Elijah; the connection distinguishes the original instruction from its reported delivery.

2 Kings 1:7
He spoke to them, saying, ‘What was the character of the man who came up to meet you and spoke these words to you?’
  2 Kings 1:8 — Same-chapter question and answer: identifying the messenger by appearance: Ahaziah asks what the man who delivered the words was like; the messengers describe hairiness and a leather belt, and the king identifies Elijah the Tishbite.

2 Kings 1:8
They said to him, ‘A hairy man, with a leather belt fastened around his waist.’ He said, ‘This is Elijah the Tishbite.’
  Matthew 3:4 — Descriptive parallel: hair and the leather belt in prophetic appearance: Elijah is identified as a hairy man with a leather belt around his waist; John wears camel-hair clothing and a leather belt around his waist. The shared belt and hair imagery do not make the descriptions identical.

2 Kings 1:9
He sent to him a commander, a captain of fifty, with his fifty men. He went up and came to him, and there Elijah was, sitting on the top of the mountain. The captain of fifty spoke to him and said, ‘Man of God, the king has summoned you. Come down!’
  2 Kings 1:11 — Same-chapter literary parallel: successive captains deliver the royal summons: The first captain commands the man of God to come down at the king’s summons; the next captain repeats the command with added urgency, preserving the successive groups rather than merging them.

2 Kings 1:10
Elijah answered and said to the captain of fifty, ‘If I am a man of God, fire will come down from heaven and consume you and your fifty men.’ Fire came down from heaven and consumed him and his fifty men.
  2 Kings 1:12 — Same-chapter literary parallel: repeated fire upon two separate companies: Elijah answers the first captain with the conditional man-of-God declaration and fire consumes his company; the second company receives the corresponding declaration and fire. Each company remains a separate narrated occurrence.
  Luke 9:54-55 — Verbal and narrative allusion: fire from heaven to consume people: Elijah announces heavenly fire that consumes the captain and fifty men; James and John propose commanding heavenly fire to consume the unwelcoming villagers, and Jesus rebukes them. Luke records a rejected proposal rather than another performed destruction.

2 Kings 1:11
The king again sent to him another captain of fifty with his fifty men. The captain went up and spoke to him, saying, ‘Man of God, thus says the king: Come down quickly!’
  2 Kings 1:9 — Same-chapter literary parallel: the second royal command repeats the first: The second captain tells the man of God to come down quickly; the first captain likewise delivered the king’s demand to come down, establishing the repeated command while retaining the second command’s added urgency.

2 Kings 1:12
Elijah answered and spoke to him, saying, ‘If I am a man of God, fire will come down from heaven and consume you and your fifty men.’ Fire came down from heaven and consumed him and his fifty men.
  Sirach 48:3 — Later biblical recollection: Elijah and descending fire: The second captain and his fifty are consumed by fire from heaven after Elijah speaks; Sirach recalls Elijah bringing down fire three times by the Lord’s word, without identifying every individual occurrence in that summary.

2 Kings 1:13
The king again sent a commander, a third captain of fifty, with his fifty men. The third captain of fifty came to him, bent his knees before Elijah, and pleaded with him, saying, ‘Man of God, please let my life and the lives of these fifty slaves of yours be honored in your eyes.’
  2 Kings 1:9-12 — Same-chapter narrative contrast: petition replaces the earlier captains’ commands: The third captain kneels and asks that his and his men’s lives be honored; the first two captains issued royal commands and their companies were consumed. The different forms of address and stated outcomes remain distinct.

2 Kings 1:14
‘Behold, fire came down from heaven and consumed the first two captains of fifty and their fifty men. But now please let the lives of your slaves be honored in your eyes.’
  2 Kings 1:10-12 — Same-chapter retrospective reference: the third captain recalls the earlier fire: The third captain explicitly recalls fire consuming the first two captains and their men; the preceding account narrates those two destructions separately, grounding the particular danger invoked in his plea.

2 Kings 1:15
An angel of the Lord spoke to Elijah and said, ‘Go down with him; do not be afraid of them.’ Elijah rose and went down with him to the king.
  2 Kings 1:9 — Same-chapter command contrast: the angel authorizes Elijah’s descent: The angel tells Elijah to go down without fear, and he goes to the king; the first captain had already demanded descent under royal authority. The link marks the different speakers and Elijah’s reported response to the angel.

2 Kings 1:16
Elijah spoke to him and said, ‘Thus says the Lord: Why did you send messengers to inquire of Baal Fly, the god of Ekron? It shall not be so! The bed onto which you have gone up—you will not come down from it, for you will surely die.’
  2 Kings 1:3-4 — Same-chapter literary parallel: Elijah repeats the inquiry-and-death oracle directly: Elijah’s address to the king again names the inquiry to Baal Fly and the warning that he will not descend from the bed but die, repeating the message initially given through the intercepted messengers.

2 Kings 1:17
He died according to the word of the Lord that Elijah had spoken.
  2 Kings 1:4 — Same-chapter prophecy and fulfilment: the narrator attributes death to the prior word: The death notice expressly connects Ahaziah’s death with Elijah’s word; the earlier oracle supplies the particular bed-and-death warning, without adding an independently diagnosed medical cause.
  Sirach 48:6 — Later biblical recollection: Elijah’s judgments upon kings and men in bed: Ahaziah dies according to Elijah’s word after the bed oracle; Sirach praises Elijah for bringing kings to destruction and glorious men from their bed. The thematic royal-and-bed connection does not insert Ahaziah’s name into Sirach.

2 Kings 1:18
The rest of the acts of Ahaziah, all that he did—behold, are these not written in the book of the daily records for the kings of Israel?
  1 Kings 22:52-54 — Regnal narrative framing: Ahaziah’s opening and closing notices: The closing notice refers Ahaziah’s remaining acts to Israel’s royal daily records; the earlier regnal notice supplies his parentage, Samarian rule and conduct. This links the named king’s narrative notices without claiming the cited annals were read.

2 Kings 1:18a
Jehoram son of Ahab reigns over Israel in Samaria for twelve years, beginning in the eighteenth year of Jehoshaphat king of Judah.
  2 Kings 3:1 — Repeated regnal notice: Jehoram’s parentage and twelve-year reign: Both notices name Jehoram son of Ahab ruling Israel for twelve years from Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth year. The continuation additionally names Samaria, preserving that detail without inserting it into the later notice.

2 Kings 1:18b
He did what was evil before the Lord, though not as his brothers did, nor as his mother did.
  2 Kings 3:2 — Repeated regnal assessment with a stated family-comparison difference: Both call Jehoram’s conduct evil and qualify the comparison with his mother; the continuation compares him with his brothers, whereas the later notice compares him with his father. Those different family clauses remain unresolved rather than equivalent.

2 Kings 1:18c
He removed the pillars of Baal that his father had made and shattered them. Yet he clung to the sins of the house of Jeroboam, who caused Israel to sin; he did not turn away from them.
  2 Kings 3:2-3 — Repeated regnal assessment: Baal pillars removed but Jeroboam’s sin retained: The continuation combines removal and shattering of the father’s Baal pillars with continued adherence to Jeroboam’s sins; the later notices likewise report removal and continued adherence, without adding the shattering detail to them.

2 Kings 1:18d
The Lord was stirred to anger against the house of Ahab.
  1 Kings 20:21-22 — Prophetic judgment and later dynastic assessment: Ahab’s house: The continuation states that the Lord was angered against Ahab’s house; Elijah’s earlier oracle names calamities against Ahab and his house because of the provocations and Israel’s sin, supplying the particular dynastic judgment rather than another house’s oracle.

2 Kings 2:1
When the Lord was about to take Elijah up in a tumult, as into heaven, Elijah and Elisha went from Gilgal.
  Sirach 48:12 — Later biblical recollection: Elijah’s whirlwind and Elisha’s succession: Kings introduces the Lord taking Elijah up in a tumult, as into heaven, while Elisha travels with him; Sirach recalls Elijah covered by a whirlwind and Elisha filled with his spirit, preserving each account’s wording.

2 Kings 2:2
Elijah said to Elisha, ‘Please stay here, for the Lord has sent me as far as Bethel.’ Elisha said, ‘As the Lord lives, and as you live, I will not leave you.’ So they came to Bethel.
  2 Kings 2:4 — Same-chapter literary parallel: repeated refusal to leave Elijah: At the Bethel stage Elisha swears by the Lord’s life and Elijah’s life that he will not leave; the Jericho stage repeats that pledge, with the different destinations retained.

2 Kings 2:3
The sons of the prophets who were in Bethel came to Elisha and said to him, ‘Do you know that this day the Lord is taking your master away from above your head?’ He said, ‘I too know it. Be silent.’
  2 Kings 2:5 — Same-chapter literary parallel: prophetic groups announce the same impending removal: The sons of the prophets at Bethel tell Elisha his master is being taken that day and receive his knowing reply; the Jericho group repeats the announcement and reply, without merging the two groups.

2 Kings 2:4
Elijah said to Elisha, ‘Please stay here, for the Lord has sent me to Jericho.’ Elisha said, ‘As the Lord lives, and as you live, I will not leave you.’ So they came to Jericho.
  2 Kings 2:6 — Same-chapter literary parallel: the loyalty pledge persists toward the Jordan: Elisha refuses to remain behind on the Jericho stage; he repeats the Lord-and-master life oath when Elijah names the Jordan, showing the repeated refusal at successive destinations.

2 Kings 2:5
The sons of the prophets who were in Jericho approached Elisha and said to him, ‘Do you know that this day the Lord is taking your master away from above your head?’ He said, ‘Indeed, I too know it. Be silent.’
  2 Kings 2:3 — Same-chapter literary parallel: Jericho’s warning corresponds to Bethel’s warning: The Jericho prophetic sons announce that the Lord is taking Elisha’s master away that day; the Bethel group had given the corresponding announcement, and Elisha tells both that he knows and asks silence.

2 Kings 2:6
Elijah said to him, ‘Please stay here, for the Lord has sent me as far as the Jordan.’ Elisha said, ‘As the Lord lives, and as you live, I will not leave you.’ So both of them went on.
  2 Kings 4:30 — Narrative verbal parallel: the same life oath and refusal to leave: Elisha swears by the Lord’s life and Elijah’s life that he will not leave him; the child’s mother later uses the corresponding oath and refusal toward Elisha, with different speakers and circumstances preserved.

2 Kings 2:7
Fifty men, sons of the prophets, stood opposite them at a distance, while both of them stood beside the Jordan.
  2 Kings 2:15 — Same-chapter narrative continuity: prophetic witnesses across from the Jordan: Fifty prophetic sons stand opposite at a distance while Elijah and Elisha stand by the Jordan; the later notice places Jericho’s prophetic sons opposite Elisha as they recognize Elijah’s spirit upon him, without enumerating each returning witness.

2 Kings 2:8
Elijah took his sheepskin, rolled it up, and struck the water. The water divided to this side and that, and both of them crossed over on barren ground.
  Joshua 3:15-17 — Narrative parallel: the Jordan permits a dry crossing: Elijah strikes the Jordan with his rolled sheepskin, the water divides, and the two cross on barren ground; Joshua describes the Jordan stopping and Israel crossing on dry ground with the ark’s priests. Actors and means remain different.

2 Kings 2:9
When they had crossed, Elijah said to Elisha, ‘Ask what I should do for you before I am taken up from you.’ Elisha said, ‘Please let a double portion of your spirit be upon me.’
  Deuteronomy 21:17 — Lexical and inheritance imagery: the double portion: Elisha asks for a double portion of Elijah’s spirit; Deuteronomy gives the acknowledged firstborn a double portion of possessions. The shared inheritance expression does not establish a legal adoption or quantify Elisha’s miracles.
  2 Kings 2:15 — Same-chapter request and subsequent recognition: Elijah’s spirit upon Elisha: Elisha requests a double portion of Elijah’s spirit; the prophetic sons later say Elijah’s spirit rests upon him. Their statement recognizes the spirit without explicitly measuring it as double.

2 Kings 2:10
Elijah said, ‘You have made a difficult request. If you see me being taken up from you, it will be so for you; but if not, it certainly will not happen.’
  2 Kings 2:12 — Same-chapter condition and narrated observation: seeing Elijah’s removal: Elijah makes seeing him taken away the condition attached to Elisha’s request; Elisha is then described watching and crying out before seeing him no more, connecting the stated condition with the narrated sight.

2 Kings 2:11
As they went, walking and speaking, behold—a chariot of fire and horses of fire separated the two of them, and Elijah was taken up in a tumult, as into heaven.
  Sirach 48:9 — Later biblical recollection: fiery chariot and horses at Elijah’s removal: Kings says the fiery chariot and horses separate the two men as Elijah is taken in a tumult, as into heaven; Sirach recalls his fiery whirlwind, chariot and fiery horses. It does not supply Kings with an unstated riding action.

2 Kings 2:12
Elisha was watching and crying, ‘Father, father! Israel’s chariot and its horseman!’ He saw him no more. He seized his clothes and tore them into two pieces.
  2 Kings 13:14 — Verbal narrative parallel: father and Israel’s chariot and horseman: Elisha cries Father and Israel’s chariot and horseman as Elijah is removed; Joash later uses that corresponding address while weeping over the dying Elisha, preserving the different speaker, prophet and circumstances.
  2 Kings 2:10 — Same-chapter narrative observation and earlier condition: Elisha is watching as he cries out and then sees Elijah no more; Elijah had attached the promised outcome to Elisha seeing him taken away, rather than merely hearing about it afterward.

2 Kings 2:13
He picked up Elijah’s sheepskin, which had fallen upon Elisha, and Elisha returned and stood on the edge of the Jordan.
  1 Kings 19:19 — Narrative object continuity: Elijah’s sheepskin and Elisha’s calling: Elisha picks up Elijah’s sheepskin, which the text describes as having fallen upon Elisha; the calling account had Elijah throw his sheepskin cloak over Elisha. The shared object and named relationship connect calling and succession without adding a new anointing scene.

2 Kings 2:14
He took Elijah’s sheepskin, which had fallen upon him, and struck the water, but it did not separate. He said, ‘Where is the God of Elijah—Appho?’ He struck the waters, and they split to this side and that, and Elisha crossed over.
  2 Kings 2:8 — Same-chapter narrative parallel with a different sequence: striking the Jordan: Elijah’s sheepskin strikes the water in both crossings; Elijah’s crossing follows division, whereas Elisha’s first strike does not separate the water and the later strike does. The different sequence and Appho wording are preserved.

2 Kings 2:15
The sons of the prophets in Jericho saw him from opposite him and said, ‘The spirit of Elijah has come to rest upon Elisha.’ They came to meet him and bowed before him to the ground.
  Sirach 48:12 — Later biblical recollection: Elisha filled with Elijah’s spirit: The prophetic sons say Elijah’s spirit rests upon Elisha after his return across the Jordan; Sirach likewise joins Elijah’s whirlwind departure with Elisha being filled with his spirit, without independently quantifying that filling.

2 Kings 2:16
They said to him, ‘Behold, with your servants are fifty capable men. Please let them go and search for your master, in case a spirit of the Lord has lifted him and cast him into the Jordan, or onto one of the mountains, or onto one of the hills.’ Elisha said, ‘Do not send them.’
  1 Kings 18:12 — Narrative parallel: proposed displacement of Elijah by the Lord’s spirit: The prophetic sons suggest a spirit of the Lord may have lifted Elijah and cast him elsewhere; Obadiah earlier feared the Lord’s Spirit would carry Elijah to an unknown land. Both are expressed possibilities, not narrated discoveries of a displaced prophet.

2 Kings 2:17
They pressed him until he was ashamed, and he said, ‘Send them.’ They sent fifty men, who searched for three days but did not find him.
  2 Kings 2:18 — Same-chapter narrative outcome: the unsuccessful search and Elisha’s reminder: After pressing Elisha for permission, fifty men search three days without finding Elijah; their return prompts Elisha’s reminder that he had told them not to go, keeping permission distinct from his original counsel.

2 Kings 2:18
They returned to him while he was staying in Jericho, and Elisha said, ‘Did I not tell you, Do not go?’
  2 Kings 2:16 — Same-chapter retrospective reference: Elisha recalls his refusal: Elisha asks whether he had told the returning searchers not to go; the earlier exchange records his explicit Do not send them, before their subsequent insistence and permitted search.

2 Kings 2:19
The men of the city said to Elisha, ‘Behold, the city’s settlement is good, as the lord sees, but the waters are bad and the land is bereaved of offspring.’
  2 Kings 2:21-22 — Same-chapter complaint and remedy: bad water and loss of offspring: The city’s men report bad water and a land bereaved of offspring; Elisha’s proclamation says the waters are healed and death or loss of offspring will no longer come from there, followed by the lasting-healing notice.

2 Kings 2:20
Elisha said, ‘Bring me a new small water jar and put salt in it.’ They brought it to him.
  Exodus 15:23-25 — Narrative parallel: a material cast into undrinkable water before restoration: Elisha requests a new water jar with salt before casting it at the water’s outlet; Moses casts the Lord-shown wood into Marah’s bitter water and it becomes sweet. The salt, wood and two settings remain distinct.

2 Kings 2:21
Elisha went out to the outlet of the waters, threw salt there, and said, ‘Thus says the Lord: I have healed these waters. No longer will death or loss of offspring come from there.’
  Exodus 15:25-26 — Thematic and verbal parallel: the Lord heals in a water-restoration setting: Elisha pronounces the Lord’s healing of the waters after casting salt; at Marah the restored water is followed by the Lord’s self-description as the One who heals. The promised freedom from local death or loss is not transferred into Exodus.

2 Kings 2:22
The waters have remained healed to this day, according to the word Elisha spoke.
  2 Kings 2:21 — Same-chapter proclamation and result: the healing remains: The narrator says the waters remain healed according to Elisha’s word; the preceding proclamation supplies the Lord’s explicit healing announcement and the removal of death or loss of offspring from those waters.

2 Kings 2:23
He went up from there to Bethel. As he was going up along the road, small children came out from the city and made fun of him, saying to him, ‘Go up, baldhead! Go up!’
  2 Kings 2:24 — Same-chapter provocation and response: mockery followed by curse and bears: The small children’s repeated baldhead taunt precedes Elisha turning, seeing them and cursing in the Lord’s name, followed by two bears tearing forty-two children. The link retains the source’s age wording and number.

2 Kings 2:24
He turned back toward them, saw them, and cursed them in the name of the Lord. Behold, two bears came out of the woodland and tore open forty-two of the children.
  2 Kings 2:23 — Same-chapter narrative context: the taunt preceding Elisha’s curse: The curse and bear attack follow the small children’s Go up, baldhead mockery on the road to Bethel. The connection supplies the stated provocation without inventing weapons, a planned assault or a different age group.

2 Kings 2:25
He went from there to Mount Carmel, and from there he returned to Samaria.
  2 Kings 4:25 — Narrative location continuity: Elisha’s ministry at Mount Carmel: Elisha travels from Bethel to Mount Carmel and then Samaria; the Shunammite woman later goes to the man of God at Mount Carmel, where Elisha sees her coming. The repeated named location does not establish a permanent residence.

2 Kings 3:1
Jehoram son of Ahab became king in Israel in the eighteenth year of Jehoshaphat king of Judah, and he reigned twelve years.
  2 Kings 1:18a — Repeated regnal notice: Jehoram’s twelve years and Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth year: Both notices name Ahab’s son Jehoram ruling Israel for twelve years from Jehoshaphat’s eighteenth year; the earlier continuation additionally locates his reign in Samaria, without that word being inserted here.

2 Kings 3:2
He did what was evil in the eyes of the Lord, though not as his father and not as his mother had done. He removed the pillars of Baal that his father had made.
  2 Kings 1:18b-18c — Repeated regnal assessment with differing family language: Both assessments call Jehoram evil, qualify the maternal comparison and report removal of his father’s Baal pillars. Here the comparison names his father, while the continuation names brothers; the latter also adds shattering, and those differences remain intact.

2 Kings 3:3
Yet he clung to the sin of Jeroboam son of Nebat, who caused Israel to sin; he did not turn away from it.
  2 Kings 1:18c — Repeated regnal assessment: persistence in Jeroboam’s sins: The main notice says Jehoram clings to Jeroboam son of Nebat’s sin and does not turn away; the continuation likewise says he clings to the sins of Jeroboam’s house despite removing the Baal pillars.

2 Kings 3:4
Mesha king of Moab was a noqed. At the rising, he rendered to the king of Israel a hundred thousand lambs and a hundred thousand rams in their fleeces.
  2 Kings 3:5 — Same-chapter narrative background: Mesha’s deliveries before Moab’s break: Mesha’s delivery of a hundred thousand lambs and a hundred thousand rams in their fleeces precedes the statement that Moab broke with Israel after Ahab died. Noqed and at the rising are preserved without an independently supplied translation or annual timetable.

2 Kings 3:5
After Ahab died, the king of Moab broke faith with the king of Israel.
  2 Kings 1:1 — Repeated narrative notice: Moab breaks faith after Ahab dies: Both notices place Moab’s break with Israel after Ahab’s death; the fuller campaign account names the king of Moab and the king of Israel, while the opening notice states the break briefly.

2 Kings 3:6
King Jehoram went out from Samaria that day and mustered Israel.
  2 Kings 3:9 — Same-chapter narrative continuity: Israel’s muster enters the allied expedition: Jehoram leaves Samaria and musters Israel; the subsequent circuit names the kings of Israel, Judah and Edom together. This connects the muster with the particular allied campaign without inventing an army size.

2 Kings 3:7
He went and sent word to Jehoshaphat king of Judah, saying, ‘The king of Moab has broken faith with me. Will you go with me to Moab for war?’ He said, ‘I will go up. As I am, so are you; my people are as your people, my horses as your horses.’
  1 Kings 22:4 — Verbal narrative parallel: Jehoshaphat’s alliance formula in two campaigns: Jehoshaphat promises his own participation, people and horses for Jehoram’s Moab campaign; he used the corresponding people-and-horses formula when invited against Remmath-gilead. The inviting kings and enemies remain different.

2 Kings 3:8
He said, ‘By which road shall I go up?’ He said, ‘By the wilderness road of Edom.’
  2 Kings 3:9 — Same-chapter plan and subsequent expedition: the wilderness road of Edom: The proposed route is the wilderness road of Edom; the ensuing seven-day circuit explicitly includes Edom’s king alongside Israel’s and Judah’s kings, while adding the lack of water rather than a new route instruction.

2 Kings 3:9
The king of Israel, the king of Judah, and the king of Edom went on. They made a circuit of seven days’ journey, but there was no water for the camp or the livestock at their feet.
  2 Kings 3:17 — Same-chapter distress and promised provision: camp and livestock need water: The allied camp and livestock lack water after the seven-day circuit; the Lord’s answer promises a filled torrent bed and drinking for the people, possessions and livestock, addressing the stated need without changing those categories.

2 Kings 3:10
The king of Israel said, ‘Alas! The Lord has called the three kings who are passing through to give them into the hand of Moab.’
  2 Kings 3:13 — Same-chapter literary parallel: the king repeats his feared divine intention: The king of Israel says the Lord has called the three kings to give them to Moab; he repeats that claim to Elisha. Both remain the king’s statements rather than the narrator certifying his interpretation.

2 Kings 3:11
Jehoshaphat said, ‘Is there no prophet of the Lord here, through whom we may inquire of the Lord?’ One of the attendants of the king of Israel answered, ‘Elisha son of Shaphat is here, who poured water on Elijah’s hands.’
  1 Kings 19:21 — Narrative background: Elisha ministering to Elijah: Elisha is identified as the one who poured water on Elijah’s hands; the calling account says he followed Elijah and ministered to him, supporting the service relationship without inserting the hand-washing task into that earlier sentence.
  1 Kings 22:7 — Verbal narrative parallel: Jehoshaphat asks for a prophet of the Lord: Jehoshaphat asks whether a prophet of the Lord is present through whom to inquire; his earlier wartime question uses the corresponding request before Micaiah is named, whereas this answer identifies Elisha.

2 Kings 3:12
Jehoshaphat said, ‘A word of the Lord is with him.’ The king of Israel, Jehoshaphat king of Judah, and the king of Edom went down to him.
  2 Kings 3:14 — Same-chapter narrative connection: Jehoshaphat’s confidence and Elisha’s stated regard: Jehoshaphat says a word of the Lord is with Elisha and goes to him with the other kings; Elisha subsequently states his particular regard for Jehoshaphat, rather than expressing equal approval of all three kings.

2 Kings 3:13
Elisha said to the king of Israel, ‘What have I to do with you? Go to your father’s prophets.’ The king of Israel said to him, ‘No! For the Lord has called the three kings together to hand them over into the hands of Moab.’
  2 Kings 3:14 — Same-chapter narrative contrast: rebuffing Israel while regarding Judah’s king: Elisha tells Israel’s king to go to his father’s prophets; his next oath says he would not look toward him except for regard for Jehoshaphat, explaining the different stated treatment without identifying every prophet in the rebuff.

2 Kings 3:14
Elisha said, ‘As the Lord of hosts lives, before whom I stand, if I were not showing regard for Jehoshaphat king of Judah, I would neither look toward you nor see you.’
  1 Kings 17:1 — Prophetic oath formula: the living Lord before whom the prophet stands: Elisha swears by the living Lord of hosts before whom he stands; Elijah’s drought announcement uses the corresponding life-and-standing formula. Their different audiences and announced circumstances remain separate.

2 Kings 3:15
‘But now bring me a player of strings.’ As the player plucked the strings, the hand of the Lord came upon him.
  1 Samuel 10:5-6 — Narrative parallel: instruments accompany a prophetic encounter: The hand of the Lord comes upon Elisha as a string player plays; Samuel’s sign describes instruments before prophesying prophets and the Lord’s Spirit coming upon Saul. The parallel does not equate the two expressions or claim music compels revelation.

2 Kings 3:16
He said, ‘Thus says the Lord: Make this torrent bed pit after pit.’
  2 Kings 3:20 — Same-chapter oracle and subsequent provision: the torrent-bed instruction: The Lord’s word directs pit after pit in the torrent bed; the later morning notice reports water arriving from Edom and filling the land. It records the water’s arrival without separately stating that every instructed pit was dug.

  2 Kings 7:17 — Same-chapter The gate-trampling death is explicitly recounted: The concluding notice repeats that the people trampled the officer at the gate and he died; the earlier notice had given his appointment and that death. The repetition remains one particular narrative outcome, not a new separate death.

2 Kings 8:1
Elisha had spoken to the woman whose son he had revived, saying, ‘Rise and go, you and your household, and live as a sojourner wherever you may. For the Lord has called famine upon the land, and indeed it has come upon the land for seven years.’
  2 Kings 4:32-37 — Narrative retrospective reference: the woman whose son Elisha revived: The famine instruction identifies its recipient by the son Elisha revived; the earlier account narrates the dead child, Elisha’s prayer, the opened eyes and the son returned to the Shunammite woman, grounding that particular identification.

2 Kings 8:2
The woman rose and acted according to Elisha’s word. She and her household went and lived as sojourners in the land of the foreigners for seven years.
  2 Kings 8:1 — Same-chapter instruction and compliance: the seven-year sojourn: The woman and household sojourn in the foreigners’ land for seven years according to Elisha’s word; the instruction had expressly named her household, sojourning and the seven-year famine, without prescribing that particular foreign destination.

2 Kings 8:3
After the end of seven years, the woman returned from the land of the foreigners to the city and came to cry to the king about her house and her fields.
  2 Kings 8:6 — Same-chapter appeal and royal response: recovering house and fields: After seven years the woman asks the king about her house and fields; the king subsequently orders restoration of her belongings and field produce from her departure onward, preserving the order without separately claiming its execution is narrated.

2 Kings 8:4
The king was speaking to Gehazi, the attendant of Elisha the man of God, saying, ‘Please recount to me all the great things Elisha has done.’
  2 Kings 8:5 — Same-chapter request and reported answer: Gehazi recounts the revived son: The king asks Gehazi to recount Elisha’s great works; the next notice names the revival of a dead son as the story being told when the woman appears, without claiming it is an exhaustive list of those works.

2 Kings 8:5
As he was explaining to the king how he had revived a dead son, behold, the woman whose son Elisha had revived was crying to the king about her house and her fields. Gehazi said, ‘Lord king, this is the woman, and this is her son whom Elisha revived.’
  2 Kings 4:32-37 — Narrative retelling: the revived child identified before the king: Gehazi identifies the woman and son while recounting Elisha’s revival of a dead son; the earlier account supplies the dead child, prayer, opened eyes and return to the mother, rather than a different revival narrative.

2 Kings 8:6
The king questioned the woman, and she recounted it to him. The king assigned her one eunuch, saying, ‘Restore all her belongings and all the produce of her field from the day she left the land until now.’
  2 Kings 4:13 — Narrative contrast: an earlier royal-help question and the later restoration order: Elisha earlier asks whether the woman has a matter for the king or commander, and she answers that she lives among her own people; here the king orders restoration after her own appeal, linking the different stated situations.

2 Kings 8:7
Elisha came to Damascus, and the son of Ader, king of Syria, was ill. They reported to him, saying, ‘The man of God has come here.’
  2 Kings 8:9 — Same-chapter narrative continuity: the ill Syrian king sends to the newly arrived prophet: The son of Ader is ill when Elisha reaches Damascus and is reported there; Hazael then approaches Elisha with that king’s recovery inquiry, identifying the particular illness behind the visit.

2 Kings 8:8
The king said to Hazael, ‘Take a manaa in your hand and go to meet the man of God. Inquire of the Lord through him, saying, Will I recover from this illness of mine?’
  2 Kings 1:2-4 — Narrative contrast: two ill kings seek an oracle from different sources: The Syrian king directs an inquiry of the Lord through the man of God; Ahaziah had directed an inquiry of Baal Fly and received Elijah’s challenge about God in Israel. Their messengers, recipients and resulting words are kept distinct.

2 Kings 8:9
Hazael went to meet him, taking a manaa in his hand and all the good things of Damascus, a load for forty camels. He came and stood before him and said to Elisha, ‘Your son, the son of Ader, king of Syria, sent me to you, saying, Will I recover from this illness of mine?’
  2 Kings 8:8 — Same-chapter instruction and reported execution: Hazael’s recovery inquiry: Hazael brings the king’s recovery question to Elisha after being directed to inquire through the man of God; his reported load of Damascus goods and forty camels remains additional to the original manaa instruction.

2 Kings 8:10
Elisha said, ‘Go, tell him: You will surely live. And the Lord has shown me that you will surely die.’
  2 Kings 8:14-15 — Same-chapter oracle report and subsequent death: both clauses remain visible: Elisha gives the You will surely live message and says the Lord has shown certain death; Hazael reports the live clause, while the next-day account reports death and Hazael becoming king. The two oracle clauses are not silently harmonized.

2 Kings 8:11
He stood before his face and held it until shame, and the man of God wept.
  2 Kings 8:12 — Same-chapter emotional response and explanation: the man of God weeps: The verse ends with the man of God weeping; Hazael asks why, and Elisha names the evils he knows Hazael will do to Israel. The link does not assign the earlier ambiguous standing-and-held-face pronouns independently.

2 Kings 8:12
Hazael said, ‘Why does my lord weep?’ He said, ‘Because I know all the evils you will do to the sons of Israel: you will send their strongholds into fire, kill their chosen men with the sword, dash down their infants, and tear open their pregnant women.’
  2 Kings 13:3-4 — Prophetic warning and later oppression: Hazael afflicts Israel: Elisha foresees Hazael’s violence against Israel; the later reign account says Israel is given into Hazael’s hand and describes Syrian affliction. It supports the named oppression without separately verifying every foretold atrocity in that later passage.

2 Kings 8:13
Hazael said, ‘Who is your slave, a dead dog, that he should do this thing?’ Elisha said, ‘The Lord has shown me you reigning over Syria.’
  1 Kings 19:15 — Prophetic succession connection: Hazael named for Syria’s kingship: Elisha tells Hazael that the Lord has shown him reigning over Syria; the earlier word to Elijah directs the anointing of Hazael as Syria’s king. This links the named kingship without claiming an anointing ceremony is narrated here.

2 Kings 8:14
He went away from Elisha and entered his master’s presence. He said to him, ‘What did Elisha say to you?’ He said, ‘He told me, You will surely live.’
  2 Kings 8:10 — Same-chapter selective report: the live clause repeated and the death clause omitted: Hazael reports Elisha saying You will surely live; Elisha’s recorded words also include the Lord showing certain death. The connection marks the specific reported clause without treating it as the complete oracle.

2 Kings 8:15
The next day he took the machma, dipped it in the water, and wrapped it over his face. He died, and Hazael became king in his place.
  2 Kings 8:13 — Same-chapter prediction and royal succession: Hazael becomes king: The next-day account reports the machma dipped in water, the death and Hazael becoming king in his place; Elisha had expressly announced seeing Hazael reigning over Syria. The object and face pronouns are preserved rather than independently translated or reassigned.

2 Kings 8:16
In the fifth year of Joram son of Ahab king of Israel, Joram son of Jehoshaphat became king of Judah.
  2 Chronicles 21:1 — Regnal narrative parallel: Joram succeeds Jehoshaphat in Judah: Kings distinguishes Joram son of Ahab in Israel from the newly reigning Joram son of Jehoshaphat in Judah; Chronicles names Jehoshaphat’s death and his son Joram’s succession, without supplying Kings’ fifth-year synchronism.

2 Kings 8:17
He was thirty-two years old when he became king, and he reigned eight years in Jerusalem.
  2 Chronicles 21:5 — Regnal narrative parallel: Joram’s accession age and reign length: Both give Joram an accession age of thirty-two and eight years reigning in Jerusalem, matching the stated figures and city rather than importing an additional chronology.

2 Kings 8:18
He walked in the way of the kings of Israel, as the house of Ahab had done, for Ahab’s daughter was his wife. He did what was evil before the Lord.
  2 Chronicles 21:6 — Regnal narrative parallel: Ahab’s daughter and Joram’s conduct: Both connect Joram’s marriage to Ahab’s daughter with walking in the way of Israel’s kings and Ahab’s house and doing evil before the Lord; neither passage is changed into an additional maternal genealogy.

2 Kings 8:19
Yet the Lord was unwilling to destroy Judah because of David His slave, as He had said He would give a lamp to him and to his sons all the days.
  2 Chronicles 21:7 — Covenantal narrative parallel: David and the continuing lamp: Kings says the Lord would not destroy Judah for David’s sake and the promised lamp; Chronicles names David’s house, covenant and lamp for his sons. The shared promise is retained with the different objects Judah and David’s house.

2 Kings 8:20
In his days Edom broke away from beneath Judah’s hand and made a king over themselves.
  2 Chronicles 21:8 — Historical narrative parallel: Edom separates and appoints its own king: Both state that Edom breaks away from Judah and makes a king over itself in Joram’s days, matching the particular political separation rather than another revolt.

2 Kings 8:21
Joram went up to Sior with all the chariots. He rose and struck the Edomites who had encircled him and the chariot commanders, and the people fled to their dwellings.
  2 Chronicles 21:9 — Historical narrative parallel: Joram strikes encircling Edom and the people flee: Both describe Joram going out, striking encircling Edom and chariot commanders, and the people fleeing. Kings names Sior and Chronicles adds by night; those details are not inserted into the other account.

2 Kings 8:22
Edom has remained broken away from beneath Judah’s hand to this day. Lobena also broke away at that time.
  2 Chronicles 21:10 — Historical narrative parallel with preserved place forms: Edom’s continuing separation: Both state Edom remains separated from Judah and name an additional rebellion at that time. Kings has Lobena, Chronicles Lomna and an added abandonment-of-the-Lord explanation; the distinct forms and additional explanation remain visible.

2 Kings 8:23
The rest of the acts of Joram, and everything he did—behold, are these not written in the book of the daily records for the kings of Judah?
  1 Kings 22:46 — Literary regnal formula: the remaining acts in Judah’s royal records: Joram’s closing formula refers his remaining acts to Judah’s royal daily records; Jehoshaphat’s closing formula similarly directs the reader to Judah’s royal annals. These are different kings’ notices, and the unavailable annals are not claimed as read.

2 Kings 8:24
Joram lay down with his fathers and was buried with his fathers in the city of David his father. Ahaziah his son became king in his place.
  2 Chronicles 22:1 — Regnal narrative parallel: Ahaziah succeeds his father Joram: Kings reports Joram’s death and burial followed by his son Ahaziah reigning; Chronicles likewise names Ahaziah son of Joram installed in his father’s place, adding the account of the older sons without equating any burial wording.

2 Kings 8:25
In the twelfth year of Joram son of Ahab king of Israel, Ahaziah son of Joram became king.
  2 Kings 9:29 — Repeated regnal identity with an unresolved synchronism difference: Both name Ahaziah becoming king in Judah during Joram of Israel’s reign; this notice gives the twelfth year, whereas the later notice gives the eleventh. The named succession is matched while the year difference remains unresolved.

2 Kings 8:26
Ahaziah was twenty-two years old when he became king, and he reigned one year in Jerusalem. His mother’s name was Athaliah, daughter of Omri king of Israel.
  2 Chronicles 22:2 — Regnal narrative parallel with an unresolved accession-age difference: Both give Ahaziah one year reigning in Jerusalem and name his mother Athaliah daughter of Omri; Kings says he was twenty-two at accession, while Chronicles says twenty. The shared reign and maternal notice do not resolve that age difference.

2 Kings 8:27
He walked in the way of the house of Ahab and did what was evil before the Lord, just as the house of Ahab did.
  2 Chronicles 22:3-4 — Regnal assessment parallel: Ahaziah’s conduct follows Ahab’s house: Kings says Ahaziah walks in Ahab’s house’s way and does evil as that house did; Chronicles repeats that pattern while adding his mother’s and other counselors’ influence, retaining those added explanations in Chronicles.

2 Kings 8:28
He went with Joram son of Ahab to war against Hazael king of the foreigners at Remmoth-gilead. The Syrians struck Joram.
  2 Chronicles 22:5 — Historical narrative parallel: allied war against Hazael and Joram’s wounds: Both describe Ahaziah joining Joram son of Ahab against Hazael and Joram being struck; Kings names Remmoth-gilead and Syrians, while Chronicles has Rama-galaad and archers. The forms and different actor descriptions remain preserved.

2 Kings 8:29
King Joram returned to Jezreel to be treated for the wounds they had inflicted on him at Remmoth when he fought Hazael king of Syria. Ahaziah son of Joram went down to see Joram son of Ahab at Jezreel, because he was ill.
  2 Chronicles 22:6 — Historical narrative parallel: Joram’s treatment and Ahaziah’s visit: Both place wounded Joram’s return for treatment at Jezreel or Jezrael and Ahaziah son of Joram’s visit because he is ill, retaining the distinct place forms and the two Joram parentages rather than merging the kings.
