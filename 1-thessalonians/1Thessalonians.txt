1 Thessalonians 1:1
Paul and Silvanus and Timothy, to the Church of the Thessalonians in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ: grace to you and peace.
  2 Thessalonians 1:1 — thematic parallel: Both openings name Paul, Silvanus and Timothy and address the Thessalonian Church in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Second Thessalonians says our Father and places its grace-and-peace wording in the next unit; the actual opening divisions are preserved.
    2 Thessalonians 1:1 Paul and Silvanus and Timothy, to the Church of the Thessalonians in God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ:

1 Thessalonians 1:2
We always give thanks to God for all of you, mentioning you in our prayers, unceasingly
  Philippians 1:3–1:4 — thematic parallel: Both thank God for the recipients and remember them in continuing prayer. Philippians uses the singular I and expressly adds joy; Thessalonians speaks as we and continues its remembrance into the following unit.
    Philippians 1:3 I thank my God every time I remember you,
    Philippians 1:4 always in every petition of mine for all of you, making my petition with joy,

1 Thessalonians 1:3
remembering your work of faith and labor of love and endurance of hope in our Lord Jesus Christ before our God and Father,
  1 Corinthians 13:13 — thematic parallel: Both bring faith, hope and love together. Thessalonians describes faith's work, love's labor and hope's endurance before God; Corinthians says the three remain and calls love greatest. Their order and descriptions differ.
    1 Corinthians 13:13 And now faith, hope, and love remain, these three; but the greatest of these is love.

1 Thessalonians 1:4
knowing, brothers beloved by God, your election,
  Ephesians 1:4 — thematic parallel: Knowing the beloved brothers' election corresponds to Ephesians saying God chose us in Christ. Ephesians adds before the world's foundation and the purpose of holiness without blemish; those details are not supplied as wording of this shorter election statement.
    Ephesians 1:4 just as He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world, so that we should be holy and without blemish before Him in love,

1 Thessalonians 1:5
because our gospel did not come to you in word alone, but also in power and in the Holy Spirit and in great assurance, just as you know what kind of people we became among you for your sake.
  1 Corinthians 2:4–2:5 — thematic parallel: Both contrast proclamation as words alone or persuasive wisdom with Spirit and power. Corinthians explains faith resting in God's power; Thessalonians also names great assurance and the missionaries' conduct among the recipients. The settings and additional descriptions remain distinct.
    1 Corinthians 2:4 My speech and my proclamation were not in persuasive words of wisdom, but in a demonstration of Spirit and power,
    1 Corinthians 2:5 so that your faith might rest in God’s power rather than human wisdom.

1 Thessalonians 1:6
And you became imitators of us and of the Lord, receiving the word in great affliction with joy from the Holy Spirit,
  Acts 13:50–13:52 — narrative parallel: Acts places disciples filled with joy and the Holy Spirit after persecution and expulsion of Paul and Barnabas. Thessalonians describes receiving the word in affliction with joy from the Holy Spirit and becoming imitators. This compares the joy-and-Spirit response amid missionary hardship in distinct settings, preserving Acts' conjunction and Thessalonians' from wording.
    Acts 13:50 But the Jews stirred up the devout women of high standing and the leading men of the city, raised persecution against Paul and Barnabas, and drove them out of their territory.
    Acts 13:51 They shook the dust from their feet against them and went to Iconium.
    Acts 13:52 The disciples were filled with joy and the Holy Spirit.

1 Thessalonians 1:7
so that you became an example to all who believe in Macedonia and in Achaia.
  Philippians 3:17 — thematic parallel: The Thessalonians become an example to believers in Macedonia and Achaia. Philippians asks readers to imitate Paul and observe others walking by the shared pattern. Both depict believers as examples for others, with different people and geographic scope.
    Philippians 3:17 Join together in imitating me, brothers, and observe those who walk in this way, just as you have us as a pattern.

1 Thessalonians 1:8
For from you the word of the Lord has sounded out, not only in Macedonia and Achaia, but your faith toward God has gone out in every place, so that we have no need to say anything.
  Romans 1:8 — thematic parallel: Both report the recipients' faith becoming widely known: every place in Thessalonians and the whole world in Romans. Thessalonians additionally describes the Lord's word sounding out from them and names Macedonia and Achaia; neither passage gives an exhaustive list of individual hearers.
    Romans 1:8 First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for all of you, because your faith is proclaimed in the whole world.

1 Thessalonians 1:9
For they themselves report about us what our coming to you was like, and how you turned to God from idols, to serve the living and true God
  Acts 14:15 — narrative parallel: Turning from idols to serve the living and true God parallels the Acts appeal to turn from worthless things to the living Creator. Acts addresses a crowd attempting sacrifice to Paul and Barnabas, whereas Thessalonians reports the recipients' turn already made; the occasions are distinct.
    Acts 14:15 and saying, “Men, why are you doing these things? We too are human beings, of the same nature as you. We proclaim good news to you: turn from these worthless things to the living God, who made heaven and earth and the sea and everything in them.

1 Thessalonians 1:10
and to await His Son from the heavens, whom He raised from the dead—Jesus, who rescues us from the coming wrath.
  Philippians 3:20 — thematic parallel: Both await Jesus from the heavens. Philippians calls Him Savior and Lord in its heavenly-citizenship statement; Thessalonians calls Him God's raised Son and the rescuer from coming wrath. The additional titles and stated effects are preserved.
    Philippians 3:20 For our citizenship is in the heavens, from which we also eagerly await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ,

1 Thessalonians 2:1
For you yourselves know, brothers, that our coming to you has not been in vain.
  1 Thessalonians 1:5 — internal explanation: The earlier description of the gospel's coming in power, the Holy Spirit and assurance, together with known missionary conduct, gives concrete context for the claim that their coming was not in vain. This is an internal explanation of the same visit, not another independent account.
    1 Thessalonians 1:5 because our gospel did not come to you in word alone, but also in power and in the Holy Spirit and in great assurance, just as you know what kind of people we became among you for your sake.

1 Thessalonians 2:2
But after we had already suffered and been shamefully treated in Philippi, as you know, we were bold in our God to speak to you the gospel of God amid much struggle.
  Acts 16:22–16:24 — narrative parallel: Acts reports Paul and Silas being attacked, beaten, imprisoned and placed in stocks in Philippi. That supplies concrete narrative background for the letter's reference to prior shameful treatment there before bold gospel speech to the Thessalonians. The later Thessalonian struggle is not made the same event as the Philippian imprisonment.
    Acts 16:22 The crowd joined the attack against them. The magistrates tore off their garments and ordered them to be beaten with rods.
    Acts 16:23 After inflicting many blows on them, they threw them into prison and ordered the jailer to keep them securely.
    Acts 16:24 Having received such an order, he put them in the inner prison and secured their feet in the stocks.

1 Thessalonians 2:3
For our appeal does not come from error or impurity, nor is it made in deceit.
  2 Corinthians 4:2 — thematic parallel: Both defend the integrity of ministry by rejecting deceit or cunning. Corinthians also rejects tampering with God's word and commends truth before consciences; Thessalonians denies error and impurity as well as deceit in the appeal. The particular descriptions differ.
    2 Corinthians 4:2 But we have renounced the secret and shameful things, not walking in cunning or tampering with the word of God, but commending ourselves to every human conscience before God by making the truth known.

1 Thessalonians 2:4
But just as we have been approved by God to be entrusted with the gospel, so we speak, pleasing not people but God, who tests our hearts.
  Galatians 1:10 — thematic parallel: Both oppose pleasing people to pleasing God in gospel service. Galatians frames the question through being Christ's slave; Thessalonians adds God's approval, entrusting of the gospel and testing of hearts. These are related arguments with distinct stated grounds.
    Galatians 1:10 For am I now seeking the approval of human beings, or of God? Or am I seeking to please human beings? If I were still pleasing human beings, I would not be Christ’s slave.

1 Thessalonians 2:5
For we never came with a word of flattery, as you know, nor with a pretext for greed—God is witness—
  Acts 20:33–20:35 — narrative parallel: Paul's denial of coveting silver, gold or clothing in Acts gives a concrete counterpart to the denial of a pretext for greed. Acts adds labor supporting his companions and the weak; Thessalonians separately denies flattery and invokes God as witness. The selected Acts account does not independently repeat every denial.
    Acts 20:33 I coveted no one’s silver or gold or clothing.
    Acts 20:34 You yourselves know that these hands served my needs and those of the people with me.
    Acts 20:35 In everything I showed you that by working in this way we must support the weak and remember the words of the Lord Jesus, that He Himself said, ‘It is more blessed to give than to receive.’”

1 Thessalonians 2:6
nor seeking glory from people, either from you or from others,
  John 5:41 — thematic parallel: The missionaries' refusal to seek glory from people parallels Jesus' statement that He does not receive glory from people. The seeking and receiving formulations have different speakers and settings; the comparison concerns human-sourced glory, not an identity of authority.
    John 5:41 I do not receive glory from people,

1 Thessalonians 2:7
though we could have carried weight as apostles of Christ. But we became little children among you. As a nursing mother would cherish her own children,
  Galatians 4:19 — thematic parallel: The nursing mother cherishing her own children and Paul's labor until Christ is formed in his children are distinct maternal images of ministry. Thessalonians also prints little children among you after the apostles' possible weight; that wording is retained rather than replaced with gentle, and neither maternal comparison is biological parenthood.
    Galatians 4:19 my children, for whom I am again in labor until Christ is formed in you.

1 Thessalonians 2:8
so, longing for you, we were pleased to share with you not only the gospel of God but also our own lives, because you had become beloved to us.
  2 Corinthians 12:15 — thematic parallel: Sharing their own lives with beloved recipients parallels Paul's willingness to spend and be spent for their souls in Corinthians. Both express costly personal love in ministry; Corinthians also asks about being loved less, while Thessalonians joins sharing life to sharing God's gospel.
    2 Corinthians 12:15 And I will most gladly spend and be spent for your souls. If I love you more abundantly, am I loved less?

1 Thessalonians 2:9
For you remember, brothers, our labor and toil: working night and day so as not to burden any of you, we proclaimed to you the gospel of God.
  2 Thessalonians 3:7–3:9 — thematic parallel: Both describe labor and toil, working night and day so as not to burden the Thessalonians. Second Thessalonians also names bread without payment and the intended example despite a right; First Thessalonians expressly joins the work to proclaiming God's gospel. The repeated account is retained without inventing work hours or wages.
    2 Thessalonians 3:7 For you yourselves know how you ought to imitate us, because we did not behave in a disorderly way among you,
    2 Thessalonians 3:8 nor did we eat anyone’s bread without payment, but with labor and toil we worked night and day so as not to burden any of you.
    2 Thessalonians 3:9 This was not because we lack the right, but so that we might give ourselves to you as an example for you to imitate.

1 Thessalonians 2:10
You are witnesses, and God also, how devoutly and justly and blamelessly we conducted ourselves toward you who believe,
  2 Corinthians 1:12 — thematic parallel: Both appeal to the quality of the missionaries' conduct toward the recipients. Corinthians invokes conscience and conduct in God's sincerity and grace; Thessalonians calls the believers and God witnesses to devout, just and blameless conduct. Their witness and qualification formulas remain distinct.
    2 Corinthians 1:12 For our boast is this, the testimony of our conscience: that in simplicity and sincerity from God, and not in fleshly wisdom but in the grace of God, we conducted ourselves in the world, and especially toward you.

1 Thessalonians 2:11
just as you know how we dealt with each one of you, as a father with his own children,
  1 Corinthians 4:14–4:16 — thematic parallel: Both use father-and-children language for pastoral dealings. Corinthians explicitly connects Paul's fatherhood to the gospel and admonishes beloved children; Thessalonians describes dealing with each recipient as a father with his children. The relationship is a ministry comparison, not a biological genealogy.
    1 Corinthians 4:14 I write these things not to shame you, but to admonish you as my beloved children.
    1 Corinthians 4:15 For even if you have ten thousand guardians in Christ, you do not have many fathers; for in Christ Jesus I became your father through the good news.
    1 Corinthians 4:16 Therefore I appeal to you: imitate me.

1 Thessalonians 2:12
encouraging you and comforting you and solemnly urging you to walk worthily of God, who calls you into His own kingdom and glory.
  Ephesians 4:1–4:3 — thematic parallel: Both urge a worthy walk connected to God's calling. Thessalonians names His kingdom and glory and describes encouragement, comfort and solemn urging; Ephesians develops a worthy walk through humility, patience, love and unity. Those respective details are retained.
    Ephesians 4:1 Therefore I, the prisoner in the Lord, urge you to walk worthily of the calling with which you were called,
    Ephesians 4:2 with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love,
    Ephesians 4:3 making every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace:

1 Thessalonians 2:13
And for this reason we also thank God unceasingly: when you received the word of God that you heard from us, you accepted it as no human word, but as it truly is, the word of God, which also works in you who believe.
  Galatians 1:11–1:12 — thematic parallel: Both distinguish the gospel's divine source from a merely human word or origin. Galatians describes Paul's receiving it through Jesus Christ's revelation; Thessalonians describes the community hearing it from human missionaries but accepting God's word at work in believers. Origin and human delivery are kept distinct.
    Galatians 1:11 For I make known to you, brothers, that the gospel proclaimed by me is not of human origin.
    Galatians 1:12 For I neither received it from a human being nor was taught it, but received it through a revelation of Jesus Christ.

1 Thessalonians 2:14
For you, brothers, became imitators of the Churches of God in Christ Jesus that are in Judea, because you also suffered the same things from your own countrymen as they did from the Jews
  Acts 17:5–17:9 — narrative parallel: Acts records an attack on Jason's house and the dragging of Jason and brothers before Thessalonian officials, providing a concrete example of suffering in that community. The letter compares their suffering with Judean Churches. Acts' jealous instigators, mob and officials are not collapsed into one actor or made a complete description of every countryman.
    Acts 17:5 But the Jews became jealous and took some wicked men from the marketplace, gathered a mob, and threw the city into turmoil. They attacked Jason’s house and sought to bring Paul and Silas out to the populace.
    Acts 17:6 When they did not find them, they dragged Jason and some brothers before the city officials, shouting, “These men who have upset the inhabited world have come here too,
    Acts 17:7 and Jason has welcomed them. They all act against Caesar’s decrees, saying that there is another king, Jesus.”
    Acts 17:8 They disturbed the crowd and the city officials who heard these things.
    Acts 17:9 After taking security from Jason and the others, the officials released them.

1 Thessalonians 2:15
who both killed the Lord Jesus and the prophets and drove us out, and do not please God and are opposed to all people,
  Acts 7:52 — narrative parallel: Both charges associate killing prophets with the death of the Lord or the Righteous One. Acts distinguishes fathers who killed prophetic forerunners from the addressed betrayers and murderers; Thessalonians also names driving out the missionaries. The described groups and generations retain their separate settings.
    Acts 7:52 Which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute? They killed those who announced beforehand the coming of the Righteous One, whose betrayers and murderers you have now become—

1 Thessalonians 2:16
hindering us from speaking to the nations so that they might be saved, thus always filling up their sins. But wrath has overtaken them to the end.
  Acts 13:45–13:48 — narrative parallel: Acts describes opposition to Paul's speech followed by Paul and Barnabas turning to Gentiles with a salvation proclamation. This relates to the letter's charge of hindering speech to the nations for their salvation. Acts does not repeat the letter's wrath statement or establish a date or event for it.
    Acts 13:45 When the Jews saw the crowds, they were filled with jealousy and contradicted what Paul was saying, speaking abusively.
    Acts 13:46 Paul and Barnabas spoke boldly and said, “It was necessary for God’s word to be spoken to you first. Since you reject it and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life, look—we are turning to the Gentiles.
    Acts 13:47 For the Lord has commanded us in this way: ‘I have appointed you as a light for Gentiles, so that you may bring salvation to the end of the earth.’”
    Acts 13:48 When the Gentiles heard this, they rejoiced and glorified the word of the Lord, and as many as had been appointed to eternal life believed.

1 Thessalonians 2:17
But we, brothers, having been bereaved of you for a short time—in face, not in heart—were all the more eager, with great longing, to see your face.
  Colossians 2:5 — thematic parallel: Both describe bodily absence alongside continuing attachment to the recipients. Thessalonians says in face, not in heart, with longing to see them; Colossians says absent in flesh but with them in spirit, rejoicing in their order and faith. The images and immediate circumstances differ.
    Colossians 2:5 For even if I am absent in the flesh, yet in spirit I am with you, rejoicing and seeing your order and the firmness of your faith in Christ.

1 Thessalonians 2:18
For we wanted to come to you—I, Paul, both once and twice—and Satan hindered us.
  Romans 1:13 — thematic parallel: Both describe Paul wanting or planning to visit and being hindered. Thessalonians explicitly attributes its hindrance to Satan and says once and twice; Romans leaves the hindering agent unnamed while describing repeated plans. The Roman obstacle is not assigned the Thessalonian explanation.
    Romans 1:13 And I do not want you to be unaware, brothers, that I often planned to come to you—and was hindered until now—so that I might have some fruit among you also, just as among the other nations.

1 Thessalonians 2:19
For what is our hope or joy or crown of boasting before our Lord Jesus at His coming? Is it not also you?
  Philippians 4:1 — thematic parallel: Both describe the recipients themselves as joy and crown. Thessalonians asks about our hope, joy and crown of boasting before Jesus at His coming; Philippians uses my joy and crown within its call to stand firm. The pronouns and stated future setting differ.
    Philippians 4:1 So then, my beloved and longed-for brothers, my joy and crown, stand firm in the Lord in this way, beloved.

1 Thessalonians 2:20
For you are our glory and joy.
  1 Thessalonians 2:19 — internal rhetorical question and answer: The preceding question asks whether the recipients are the speakers' hope, joy or crown before Jesus; the following assertion calls them our glory and joy. This direct internal answer identifies the recipients' place in the rhetorical question rather than supplying independent testimony.
    1 Thessalonians 2:19 For what is our hope or joy or crown of boasting before our Lord Jesus at His coming? Is it not also you?

1 Thessalonians 3:1
Therefore, when we could bear it no longer, we were willing to be left alone in Athens,
  Acts 17:15 — narrative parallel: Acts explicitly places Paul in Athens and records arrangements for Silas and Timothy to come to him. Thessalonians speaks of accepting being left alone in Athens before Timothy's sending in the next unit. The shared travel setting does not establish every intermediate movement or resolve the letter's plural we into a reconstructed itinerary.
    Acts 17:15 Those escorting Paul brought him as far as Athens. After receiving an order for Silas and Timothy to come to him as quickly as possible, they departed.

1 Thessalonians 3:2
and sent Timothy, our brother and God’s fellow worker in the gospel of Christ, to strengthen you and encourage you concerning your faith,
  Philippians 2:19–2:22 — thematic parallel: Both describe Timothy being sent or expected to be sent in gospel service for a community's good. Thessalonians calls him God's fellow worker and states strengthening and encouraging faith as his mission; Philippians commends his genuine concern and service while anticipating news that will encourage Paul. The missions and stages differ.
    Philippians 2:19 Now I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you soon, so that I too may be encouraged when I know about your circumstances.
    Philippians 2:20 For I have no one of like mind who will sincerely care about your circumstances,
    Philippians 2:21 for all seek their own interests, not those of Jesus Christ.
    Philippians 2:22 But you know his proven character, that as a child with a father he served with me for the gospel.

1 Thessalonians 3:3
so that no one would be shaken by these afflictions. For you yourselves know that we are appointed to this.
  Acts 14:22 — thematic parallel: Both encourage steadfast faith despite afflictions. Acts states that entry into God's kingdom is through many afflictions; Thessalonians says not to be shaken by the afflictions to which we are appointed. The letter's appointment wording and Acts' kingdom statement remain distinct.
    Acts 14:22 strengthening the souls of the disciples, encouraging them to remain in the faith, and saying, “Through many afflictions we must enter the kingdom of God.”

1 Thessalonians 3:4
For even when we were with you, we told you beforehand that we were going to suffer affliction, just as it also happened, as you know.
  John 15:20 — thematic parallel: Both recall advance warning of suffering or persecution. Jesus' slave-and-master saying warns His followers about persecution; the letter says its prior prediction of affliction happened as the recipients know. The speakers, settings and fulfillment statement are not made identical.
    John 15:20 Remember the word that I said to you: ‘A slave is not greater than his master.’ If they persecuted Me, they will also persecute you; if they kept My word, they will also keep yours.

1 Thessalonians 3:5
For this reason, when I too could bear it no longer, I sent to learn of your faith, for fear that the tempter had tempted you and our labor might be in vain.
  Philippians 2:16 — thematic parallel: Both connect the recipients' faithfulness with concern that gospel labor not prove vain. Philippians links holding fast the word of life with Paul's future boast about not running or laboring in vain; Thessalonians describes sending to learn their faith and fearing the tempter's activity. That temptation detail is not inserted into Philippians.
    Philippians 2:16 holding fast the word of life, so that I may boast in the day of Christ that I did not run in vain or labor in vain.

1 Thessalonians 3:6
But now Timothy has come to us from you and brought us good news of your faith and love, and that you always remember us kindly, longing to see us just as we also long to see you.
  Philippians 2:19 — thematic parallel: Timothy's actual return with news of the Thessalonians' faith, love and mutual longing gives a concrete counterpart to Philippians' anticipated sending of Timothy so Paul can learn their circumstances. The reported return and projected mission concern different communities and stages.
    Philippians 2:19 Now I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you soon, so that I too may be encouraged when I know about your circumstances.

1 Thessalonians 3:7
For this reason, brothers, in all our distress and affliction we have been comforted about you through your faith,
  2 Corinthians 7:6–7:7 — thematic parallel: Both describe comfort for distressed missionaries through a community-related report. Corinthians names God's comfort through Titus' arrival and his report of longing, mourning and zeal; Thessalonians names comfort through the readers' faith after Timothy's news. Titus and Timothy and the reported qualities remain distinct.
    2 Corinthians 7:6 But God, who comforts the lowly, comforted us by the arrival of Titus,
    2 Corinthians 7:7 and not only by his arrival, but also by the comfort with which he was comforted because of you. He reported to us your longing, your mourning, your zeal on my behalf, so that I rejoiced still more.

1 Thessalonians 3:8
because now we live, if you stand firm in the Lord.
  Philippians 4:1 — thematic parallel: Both explicitly connect the community to standing firm in the Lord. Philippians commands beloved recipients to stand firm; Thessalonians expresses the missionaries' renewed life conditionally upon the readers' steadfastness. It is not a separate account of bodily resurrection.
    Philippians 4:1 So then, my beloved and longed-for brothers, my joy and crown, stand firm in the Lord in this way, beloved.

1 Thessalonians 3:9
For what thanksgiving can we return to God for you, for all the joy with which we rejoice because of you before our God,
  Philippians 1:3–1:4 — thematic parallel: Both join thanksgiving to God for recipients with joy concerning them. Philippians describes thankful remembrance and joyful petitions; Thessalonians asks what thanksgiving can be returned for all the joy before God. The rhetorical question and direct prayer account retain their forms.
    Philippians 1:3 I thank my God every time I remember you,
    Philippians 1:4 always in every petition of mine for all of you, making my petition with joy,

1 Thessalonians 3:10
as night and day we pray most earnestly to see your face and supply what is lacking in your faith?
  Romans 1:10–1:12 — thematic parallel: Both describe earnest prayer or longing to visit recipients for their faith's benefit. Romans names a spiritual gift for strengthening and mutual encouragement through faith; Thessalonians names seeing their face and supplying what is lacking in faith. It does not say that their already-reported faith is wholly absent.
    Romans 1:10 always in my prayers asking whether somehow now at last I may be granted a way, by the will of God, to come to you.
    Romans 1:11 For I long to see you, to share with you some spiritual gift so that you may be strengthened,
    Romans 1:12 that is, to be encouraged together among you through one another’s faith, both yours and mine.

1 Thessalonians 3:11
Now may our God and Father Himself, and our Lord Jesus, direct our way to you.
  Romans 1:10 — thematic parallel: Both pray for a way to reach the recipients under divine direction. Thessalonians expressly invokes God our Father and our Lord Jesus to direct the way; Romans asks for a way to come by God's will. Their named agents and formulations are preserved.
    Romans 1:10 always in my prayers asking whether somehow now at last I may be granted a way, by the will of God, to come to you.

1 Thessalonians 3:12
And may the Lord make you increase and overflow in love for one another and for all, just as we also do for you,
  Philippians 1:9 — thematic parallel: Both intercede for increasing love. Thessalonians asks the Lord for love overflowing toward one another and all, with the missionaries as an example; Philippians asks love to abound in knowledge and discernment. Neither passage's additional qualities replace the other's.
    Philippians 1:9 And this I pray: that your love may abound still more and more in knowledge and every kind of discernment,

1 Thessalonians 3:13
so as to establish your hearts blameless in holiness before our God and Father at the coming of our Lord Jesus with all His saints. Amen.
  1 Thessalonians 5:23 — internal prayer parallel: The two prayers within the letter connect holiness and blamelessness with the Lord Jesus' coming. This prayer concerns hearts established blameless in holiness before God the Father at the Lord Jesus' coming with all His saints; the later one asks complete sanctification and preservation of spirit, soul and body. This is an internal prayer parallel, not independent testimony or a definition of every saint's identity.
    1 Thessalonians 5:23 Now may the God of peace Himself sanctify you completely, and may your spirit and soul and body be kept whole, blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.

1 Thessalonians 4:1
Finally then, brothers, we ask and urge you in the Lord Jesus that, as you received from us how you ought to walk and please God—just as you are indeed walking—you may abound still more.
  Colossians 1:10 — thematic parallel: Both describe a walk pleasing to God or the Lord. Colossians prays for a worthy walk bearing good-work fruit and growing in God's knowledge; Thessalonians acknowledges that the recipients already walk as instructed and urges further increase. Their present practice is not replaced with a claim of total failure.
    Colossians 1:10 to walk worthily of the Lord, pleasing Him in every way, bearing fruit in every good work and growing in the knowledge of God,

1 Thessalonians 4:2
For you know what instructions we gave you through the Lord Jesus.
  1 Corinthians 14:37 — thematic parallel: Both locate the authority of apostolic instructions with the Lord. Corinthians asks the one claiming prophecy or spirituality to recognize the written material as the Lord's command; Thessalonians recalls known instructions through the Lord Jesus. The instructions' particular contexts are not identified as the same material.
    1 Corinthians 14:37 If anyone thinks he is a prophet or spiritual, let him recognize that what I write to you is the Lord’s command.

1 Thessalonians 4:3
For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from sexual immorality,
  1 Corinthians 6:18 — thematic parallel: Both direct readers away from sexual immorality. Thessalonians names abstaining within God's will for sanctification; Corinthians commands fleeing it and develops a body-related reason. The statement of God's will and the imperative retain their different forms and additional rationale.
    1 Corinthians 6:18 Flee sexual immorality. Every sin that a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body.
