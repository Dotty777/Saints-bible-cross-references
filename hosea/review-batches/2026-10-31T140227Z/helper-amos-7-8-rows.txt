7:1|Joel 1:4|locust and bruchus imagery in agricultural devastation|Amos's vision presents a brood of locusts and a bruchus locust, followed in the next verse by devouring the land's grass. Joel likewise names locust and bruchus among successive devourers of what remains. This connection concerns the insects and their devastation, without identifying Amos's Gog label with another figure.
7:2|Joel 2:17-18|intercession for the people amid threatened devastation|Amos pleads for mercy for Jacob, who is so few, as the locust vision reaches the land's grass. Joel's priests likewise ask the Lord to spare His people, followed by His sparing them. Both put an appeal for the people's preservation before the Lord.
7:3|Exodus 32:12-14|appeal for mercy followed by withdrawal of announced calamity|Amos's plea that the Lord relent is answered with the declaration that this will not happen. Moses likewise asks the Lord to cease from fierce anger and be merciful, after which the Lord is appeased concerning the calamity announced against His people.
7:4|Deuteronomy 32:22|divine judgment by fire reaching the depths|Amos sees judgment by fire devouring the great abyss and the portion. Deuteronomy likewise describes fire kindled from divine wrath, burning to Hades beneath and devouring the earth and its produce. The connection is the fire's destructive reach, without equating the abyss with Hades.
7:5|Amos 7:2|same-chapter repeated plea for Jacob's preservation|Both appeals address the Lord and ask who will raise Jacob because he is so few. The first asks for mercy after the locust vision; the second asks Him to cease after the fire vision, repeating the reason for intercession.
7:6|Amos 7:3|same-chapter repeated answer to intercession|Both units follow a plea to relent with the Lord's declaration that the threatened event will not happen. The repeated answer links the locust and fire visions while preserving the second answer's emphatic wording.
7:8|Amos 8:2|same-book vision question and repeated judgment refrain|Both visions ask Amos what he sees, receive his answer naming the object, and end with the Lord declaring that He will no longer pass His people by. The objects remain distinct: adamant in chapter 7 and a fowler's vessel in chapter 8.
7:9|Hosea 10:8|judgment on Israel's altars and rites|Amos announces that the altars of laughter will disappear and Israel's rites be laid waste. Hosea likewise announces removal of the shrines of On, described as Israel's sins, and thorns and thistles upon their altars. Both announce the ruin of places of worship without equating their different labels.
7:10|Jeremiah 33:8-11|religious officials accuse a prophet before political authorities|Amaziah the priest reports Amos to Israel's king, alleging conspiracy and intolerable words. In Jeremiah, priests and false prophets likewise complain to the rulers about his prophecy against the city and demand death. Both accounts show opposition to a prophet's message being brought before governing authorities.
7:11|Amos 7:17|same-chapter recurrence of Israel's captivity prediction|Amaziah's report attributes to Amos the prediction that Israel will be led captive away from its land. The later word addressed to Amaziah ends with that same prediction. This link concerns the captivity clause, without treating Amaziah's separate claim about Jeroboam's individual death as independently verified.
7:12|Luke 13:31-33|pressure on a prophetic speaker to leave|Amaziah tells Amos to withdraw to Judah, where he may make his living and prophesy. In Luke, Jesus is urged to leave because Herod wants to kill Him, and He answers in terms of a prophet's journey toward Jerusalem. Both passages place a demand to leave beside the prophetic mission, with different stated reasons.
7:13|Acts 4:18-20|prohibition of public proclamation met by duty to God|Amaziah prohibits Amos from prophesying at the royal sanctuary of Bethel. The council similarly prohibits Peter and John from speaking or teaching in Jesus' name, while they answer that they must speak what they have seen and heard. Amos's following reply likewise grounds his mission in the Lord's command.
7:14|Zechariah 13:4-5|contrasting uses of a denial of prophetic status and an agricultural occupation|Amos says he was no prophet or prophet's son but a goat herder and sycamore-fig worker, before recounting the Lord's call. Zechariah's speaker likewise denies being a prophet and names work on the earth, in a context of prophets ashamed of lying. The verbal pattern is shared while the contexts and purposes differ.
7:15|2 Samuel 7:8|the Lord takes a person from tending sheep to service over Israel|Amos says the Lord took him from the sheep and commanded him to prophesy to His people Israel. The word to David likewise recalls the Lord taking him from the sheepfold to be ruler over Israel. The call from the flock is shared, while prophecy and kingship remain distinct tasks.
7:16|Isaiah 30:9-11|opposition to unwelcome prophetic speech|Amos repeats the prohibition on prophesying against Israel and stirring up a crowd against Jacob's house. Isaiah likewise describes a disobedient people telling prophets and visionaries not to announce or speak, asking instead for deception. Both passages report attempts to suppress unwelcome prophetic speech.
7:17|Jeremiah 20:4-6|a priest opposing a prophet is warned of sword, captivity and death abroad|Amos's response to Amaziah announces deaths by sword, captivity and his death in an unclean land. Jeremiah's response to Pashhur likewise announces sword deaths among his associates and captivity ending in his death in Babylon. The surrounding accounts identify each opponent as a priest who acts against the prophet.
8:2|Ezekiel 7:2-4|the announced arrival of an end upon Israel|Amos's vision is explained by the declaration that the end has arrived upon the Lord's people Israel. Ezekiel likewise addresses Israel's land with the repeated declaration that an end has come upon it and its inhabitants. Both declare the arrival of an end in a judgment oracle.
8:4|Proverbs 22:22-23|oppression of the needy opposed to the prohibition of force|Amos addresses those who wear out the needy and oppress the poor out of the land. Proverbs explicitly forbids force against the needy and dishonoring the weak, adding that the Lord will judge the poor person's case.
8:5|Deuteronomy 25:13-16|unjust commercial measurements opposed to the legal standard|Amos reports traders planning a small measure, great weights and an unjust balance. Deuteronomy forbids differing weights and measures, requires true and just ones, and condemns those who practice that injustice.
8:6|Amos 2:6|same-book exchange of vulnerable people for silver and sandals|Amos 8 reports an intention to acquire poor people for silver and a lowly person for sandals. Amos 2 condemns selling a righteous person for silver and a poor person for sandals. The repeated prices connect buying and selling within the book's accusations of exploitation.
8:7|Hosea 7:2|wrongdoing remembered before God|Following the charges of oppression and unjust trade, the oath in Amos declares that the people's works will never be forgotten. Hosea likewise speaks of all their evils remembered and their schemes coming before the Lord's face. Amos's oath by Jacob's arrogance is preserved without changing its stated object.
8:8|Amos 9:5|same-book recurrence of earth's disturbance and Egypt's river|Both passages join the disturbed or shaken earth with its inhabitants mourning and a consummation rising like a river and descending like Egypt's river. Amos 9 explicitly introduces the Lord touching the earth within this repeated description.
8:9|Jeremiah 15:9|sunset during the middle of the day as judgment imagery|Amos announces the sun setting at midday and daylight growing dark upon the earth. Jeremiah likewise describes the sun of the woman who bore seven setting while the day is still at its middle, beside shame and threatened sword deaths. Both use the unexpected midday sunset in a judgment setting.
8:10|Jeremiah 6:26|sackcloth and mourning for a beloved one|Amos joins sackcloth with lamentation and mourning for a beloved one. Jeremiah likewise tells the daughter of the people to wear sackcloth and make a pitiful lament for a beloved one because misery is coming.
8:10|Ezekiel 7:18|shared public mourning signs of sackcloth and baldness|Amos announces sackcloth on every waist and baldness on every head. Ezekiel likewise describes people girding on sackcloth, shame upon every face and baldness upon every head. Both extend these mourning signs across the judged population.
8:11|Deuteronomy 8:3|bread and the Lord's word distinguished as human necessities|Amos announces a famine for hearing the Lord's word rather than for bread or water. Deuteronomy recounts hunger and manna to explain that a person lives by every word from God's mouth and not by bread alone. Both distinguish dependence on God's word from physical bread.
8:12|1 Samuel 28:5-6|seeking divine communication without receiving an answer|Amos describes people running about seeking the Lord's word but not finding it. Samuel's account describes Saul inquiring through the Lord while receiving no answer through dreams, clear signs or prophets. Both describe an unsuccessful search for divine communication, in different settings.
8:13|Lamentations 4:4|young people pictured with thirst in calamity|Amos foretells young women and men fainting from thirst. Lamentations portrays a nursing child's tongue clinging to its throat with thirst, alongside infants asking for bread. Both use images of bodily thirst among the young, while the age groups and exact effects differ.
8:14|1 Kings 12:28-30|the condemned cult of Dan and its golden heifer|Amos condemns people who declare that Dan's god lives. Kings records one of the two golden heifers being placed in Dan and calls the resulting practice a sin. The connection concerns Dan's cult; Amos's separate wording about Samaria's propitiation and Beersheba remains unchanged.
