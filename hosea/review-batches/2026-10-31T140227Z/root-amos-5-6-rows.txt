5:1|Ezekiel 19:1|prophetic lament taken up over Israel|Amos introduces the word taken up over Israel's house as a lament. Ezekiel likewise receives a command to take up a lament over Israel's ruler. Both explicitly frame the prophetic address as lament, with different stated subjects.
5:2|Jeremiah 14:17-18|the people pictured as a devastated daughter|Amos laments fallen Virgin Israel, who has no one to raise her. Jeremiah calls for continuing tears over the crushed daughter of the people and describes sword and famine. Both use a female figure for the people within a lament over national devastation.
5:3|Deuteronomy 28:62|a greatly reduced remnant of Israel|Amos predicts that only a hundred will remain from a city where a thousand went out, and ten from a hundred. Deuteronomy likewise warns that the people will be left few in number after their former multitude, linking their reduction to disobedience.
5:4|Isaiah 55:3-6|seeking and hearing God associated with life|Amos commands Israel to seek the Lord and live. Isaiah likewise calls for listening to God so that the soul will live and then commands seeking God. Both connect turning toward God with life.
5:5|Hosea 4:15|shared prohibition on entering Gilgal|Amos tells the people not to enter Gilgal while rejecting their resort to named sanctuaries. Hosea likewise explicitly tells Judah not to enter Gilgal. The connection rests on the identical place and prohibition, without equating the passages' other place names.
5:6|Jeremiah 21:12|warning of fire with no one to quench it|Amos calls for seeking the Lord so that Joseph's house does not flare up in an unquenchable fire. Jeremiah likewise calls David's house to just action so that the Lord's anger is not kindled like fire with no one to quench it. The warnings address different houses.
5:7|Psalms 96:2|the Lord's rule described through righteousness and judgment|Amos positively describes the Lord making judgment on high and placing righteousness upon the earth. The psalm likewise names righteousness and judgment as the establishment of His throne. This connection preserves Amos's positive native wording.
5:8|Amos 9:6|same-book repeated description of the Lord's power over waters|Both passages describe the Lord calling for the sea's water and pouring it upon the earth's surface, then name Him the Lord God Almighty. The repeated description connects the book's presentations of His power.
5:9|Isaiah 25:12|fortified strength brought down|Amos describes shattering directed against strength and suffering brought upon a fortress. Isaiah likewise announces the height of a wall's refuge being brought low to the ground, sharing the overthrow of fortified strength.
5:10|Proverbs 9:7-8|hatred directed at one who reproves wrongdoing|Amos accuses the people of hating one who reproves at the gates. Proverbs likewise warns that reproving evil people brings dishonor and hatred, describing the hostile reception of correction.
5:11|Deuteronomy 28:30|houses and vineyards whose benefits are lost|Amos warns those who exploit the poor that they will not inhabit their stone houses or drink their vineyards' wine. Deuteronomy's curse likewise describes a house built but not inhabited and a vineyard planted without gathering its fruit.
5:12|Exodus 23:6-8|payments and injustice toward the poor and righteous|Amos condemns trampling the righteous, taking payments and turning the poor aside at the gates. Exodus forbids perverting a poor person's judgment, killing the innocent or righteous, and accepting gifts that corrupt righteous words.
5:14|Romans 12:9|the ethical contrast of good and evil|Amos commands seeking good rather than evil. Romans likewise commands abhorring evil and clinging to good, presenting the same opposed directions of conduct.
5:15|Deuteronomy 16:18-20|restoration of just judgment in the community|Amos commands restoring judgment at the gates so that the Lord may have mercy on Joseph's remaining ones. Deuteronomy requires appointed judges to judge righteously in the cities, forbids perverted judgment and commands pursuit of justice. The link concerns the required public administration of justice.
5:16|Jeremiah 9:16-19|summoning skilled mourners for a public lament|Amos announces mourning in squares and roads and a call involving those skilled in wailing. Jeremiah likewise summons skilled women who lament to take up a dirge and teach lamentation. Both explicitly describe organized public mourning.
5:17|Exodus 12:12|the Lord passing through a people in judgment|Amos announces mourning because the Lord will pass through the people's midst. Exodus likewise announces His passage through Egypt to strike the firstborn and execute vengeance. The shared passage-and-judgment pattern is applied to different peoples.
5:18|Joel 2:1-2|the Lord's day described as darkness|Amos warns those who desire the Lord's day that it is darkness rather than light. Joel likewise describes the near Lord's day as darkness and gloom, with turmoil among the land's inhabitants.
5:19|Isaiah 24:17-18|escape from one danger leads into another|Amos depicts fleeing a lion only to meet a bear, followed by a snake within a house. Isaiah similarly depicts fleeing fear into a pit and climbing from the pit only to be caught by a trap. Both use successive dangers to expose the failure of attempted escape.
5:20|Zephaniah 1:14-15|shared description of the Lord's day as gloom|Amos asks whether the Lord's day is darkness and gloom without brightness. Zephaniah likewise calls the near Lord's day a day of darkness, gloom, cloud and mist within an announcement of wrath.
5:21|Isaiah 1:13-14|the Lord's rejection of religious festivals|Amos's divine speaker hates and rejects the people's festivals and festal gatherings. Isaiah likewise declares that His soul hates their festivals and cannot endure their observances. Both challenge the acceptability of outward religious celebrations.
5:22|Jeremiah 6:19-20|burnt offerings and sacrifices declared unacceptable|Amos's divine speaker refuses the people's whole burnt offerings and sacrifices. Jeremiah likewise declares burnt offerings unacceptable and sacrifices displeasing after saying that the people rejected God's words and law.
5:23|Isaiah 1:15|refusal to hear religious expression|Amos's divine speaker will not listen to instrumental music in the sequence of rejected festivals and offerings. Isaiah likewise says He will not listen to multiplied supplications because the people's hands are full of blood. One passage names music and the other prayer, while both reject religious expression.
5:24|Isaiah 48:18|righteousness described with moving-water imagery|Amos pictures judgment rolling as water and righteousness as an impassable torrent. Isaiah likewise uses a river for peace and a sea wave for righteousness. The comparison concerns moving-water imagery, without making the passages' surrounding statements identical.
5:25|Acts 7:42|explicit later quotation of the wilderness-sacrifice question|Amos asks Israel's house whether it offered sacrifices in the wilderness for forty years. Acts explicitly introduces words from the prophets and repeats that question about sacrifices, forty years and the wilderness.
5:26|Acts 7:43|explicit later quotation about Moloch and Raiphan|Amos names Moloch's tent, the star of the god Raiphan and figures made by the people. Acts' prophetic quotation repeats these named objects and the making of their images, adding that the images were made for worship.
5:27|Acts 7:43|later quotation with a different deportation destination|Amos announces deportation beyond Damascus. Acts' quotation following the same Moloch-and-Raiphan charge announces deportation beyond Babylon. The corresponding deportation statement is retained together with the explicit difference in destination.
6:4|Luke 16:19-21|luxurious living beside neglected suffering|Amos pictures ivory beds, couches and choice meat in a passage that proceeds to indifference toward Joseph's shattering. Luke likewise places a rich man's daily splendor and table beside the afflicted Lazarus longing for its food. The comparison concerns luxury juxtaposed with suffering.
6:5|Isaiah 5:11-12|instrumental music within indulgent drinking|Amos describes clapping to instruments within the sequence of luxurious dining and wine drinking. Isaiah likewise describes lyre, harp, drums and flutes accompanying wine while condemning lack of attention to the Lord's works. The shared scene is musical indulgence; Amos's statement about things reckoned lasting is not replaced.
6:6|Isaiah 22:12-13|wine and pleasure when mourning is called for|Amos describes wine and fine perfumes without suffering over Joseph's shattering. Isaiah likewise contrasts a divine call to weeping and lamentation with gladness, meat and wine. Both expose pleasure pursued in the face of a need to mourn.
6:7|Isaiah 5:11-13|indulgence followed by captivity|Amos announces captivity after the descriptions of luxurious food, music, wine and indifference. Isaiah likewise moves from strong drink, instruments and neglect of the Lord's works to the people's captivity. Both place that outcome after the account of indulgence.
6:8|Jeremiah 22:5|a divine self-oath announcing desolation|Amos introduces the Lord swearing by Himself before announcing removal of the city and its inhabitants. Jeremiah likewise records Him swearing by Himself that the royal house will become a desolation if His commands are not carried out.
6:9|Amos 5:3|same-book warnings of shrinking numbers of survivors|Amos 5 reduces the remaining numbers from cities, including a hundred reduced to ten. Amos 6 describes ten remaining in a house and announces their death while also mentioning those left over remaining. Both develop the book's population-loss warnings without identifying the groups as the same people.
6:11|Amos 3:15|same-book destruction reaching different kinds of houses|Amos 6 announces fractures in the great house and cracks in the small house. Chapter 3 likewise announces striking colonnaded and summer houses and the loss of ivory houses. Both portray judgment reaching different kinds of dwellings, with distinct categories in each passage.
6:12|Isaiah 5:7|judgment and righteousness transformed into harmful results|Amos accuses the people of turning judgment into anger and righteousness's fruit into bitterness. Isaiah likewise contrasts expected judgment and righteousness with the lawlessness and outcry actually produced. Both describe the failure of expected just conduct through opposed outcomes.
6:13|Deuteronomy 8:17-18|claims of achievement by one's own strength|Amos quotes people claiming to have acquired horns by their own strength. Deuteronomy warns against saying that one's own strength gained wealth and instead directs remembrance of the Lord who gives strength. Both quote people crediting their own strength, while naming different things acquired.
6:14|Jeremiah 5:15-17|the Lord raises a foreign nation against Israel|Amos announces the Lord raising a nation against Israel to crush it. Jeremiah likewise announces bringing a distant nation against Israel, with strong attackers consuming its produce and attacking fortified cities.
