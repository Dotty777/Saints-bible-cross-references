Hosea 1:1
The word of the Lord that came to Hosea son of Beeri in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam son of Joash, king of Israel.
  Amos 1:1 — overlapping prophetic dating by named kings: Both openings place the prophetic words in the reigns of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam son of Joash of Israel. Hosea also lists three additional Judean kings; Amos adds the earthquake dating.

Hosea 1:2
The beginning of the Lord’s word to Hosea. The Lord said to Hosea, “Go, take for yourself a woman of prostitution and children of prostitution, for the land will utterly prostitute itself away from following the Lord.”
  Jeremiah 3:6-9 — parallel portrayal of Israel's unfaithfulness as prostitution: Hosea's commanded household sign explains the land's departure from the Lord as prostitution. Jeremiah likewise describes Israel's worship on mountains and beneath trees as prostitution and adultery with wood and stone.

Hosea 1:3
He went and took Gomer daughter of Debelaim; she conceived and bore him a son.
  Isaiah 8:3-4 — related prophetic births bearing announced national signs: Hosea takes Gomer, who bears a son; Isaiah approaches the prophetess, who likewise bears a son. The surrounding divine naming commands give both births a sign concerning national events.

Hosea 1:4
The Lord said to him, “Call him Jezreel, for in a little while I will avenge Jezreel’s blood upon Jehu’s house and put an end to the kingdom of Israel’s house.
  2 Kings 10:6-11 — named historical setting for Jehu and bloodshed at Jezreel: Kings records Jehu's demand for the royal sons' heads at Jezreel and his killing of Ahab's remaining household there. Hosea announces vengeance for Jezreel's blood upon Jehu's house; the comparison preserves the different judgments expressed.

Hosea 1:5
On that day I will shatter Israel’s bow in the Valley of Jezreel.”
  Psalms 45:10 — shared image of God shattering a bow: Hosea locates the breaking of Israel's bow in Jezreel's valley. The psalm describes the Lord shattering a bow while ending wars across the earth; the shared weapon image has different stated scopes.

Hosea 1:6
She conceived again and bore a daughter. He said to him, “Call her Not Shown Mercy, for I will certainly no longer show mercy to Israel’s house, but I will surely set Myself against them.
  Hosea 2:25 — later reversal of the named child's exclusion from mercy: The daughter is named Not Shown Mercy as Israel's house faces withheld mercy. The later promise expressly names Not Shown Mercy as the recipient of mercy, reversing the sign's announced condition.

Hosea 1:7
But I will show mercy to Judah’s sons and save them by the Lord their God. I will not save them by bow, sword, war, chariots, horses or horsemen.”
  Isaiah 31:1-3 — parallel distinction between divine rescue and military reliance: Hosea promises Judah rescue by the Lord without bows, swords, chariots or horses. Isaiah rebukes reliance on Egyptian horses and chariots instead of seeking God and declares the helpers unable to secure survival.

Hosea 1:9
He said, “Call him Not My People, for you are not My people, and I am not yours.”
  Romans 9:25 — explicit apostolic reuse of the not-My-people naming: Hosea names the son Not My People and announces the broken relationship. Paul expressly cites Hosea when describing those not God's people being called His people, presenting the naming in its promised reversal.

Hosea 2:1

  Romans 9:26 — explicit quotation of sons of the living God: Paul reproduces the promise that the place where people were called not God's people becomes the place where they are called sons of the living God. Romans places the quotation in an argument concerning those called from Jews and Gentiles.
  Genesis 22:17 — shared sea-sand image for the multitude of descendants: Hosea compares Israel's sons to unmeasurable sea sand. The promise to Abraham compares his multiplied seed to sand on the seashore; this connection concerns the numerical image, distinct from Hosea's restored naming.

  Amos 5:19 — shared lion-and-bear danger imagery in judgment: Hosea compares God's encounter to a bear in distress and adds devouring forest lion cubs and field beasts. Amos depicts someone escaping a lion only to meet a bear and then a snake; it supplies a different sequence without saying Hosea's bear lost cubs.

Hosea 13:9
In your destruction, Israel, who will help?
  Deuteronomy 32:37-39 — Rhetorical parallel: challenging alternative helpers in destruction: Hosea asks who will help Israel in destruction, between declaring that no one saves apart from God and challenging the king to save them. Deuteronomy similarly challenges trusted gods to arise and help, then declares that no god is beside the Lord and no one can deliver from His hands. Both questions expose the insufficiency of rival sources of rescue when God judges. Hosea's immediately challenged king is not equated with Deuteronomy's foreign gods.
