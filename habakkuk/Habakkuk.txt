Habakkuk 1:1
The oracle that Habakkuk the prophet saw.
  Habakkuk 3:1 — same person: Both headings of the book name Habakkuk the prophet, the first for the oracle he saw and the second for his prayer with a song.
  Bel and Dragon 1:1 — same person: Bel and the Dragon opens 'from the prophecy of Habakkuk', naming the same prophet. It adds that he was the son of Joshua of the tribe of Levi.

Habakkuk 1:2
How long, Lord, shall I cry and Thou wilt not listen? I shall cry to Thee while being wronged, and Thou wilt not save?
  Psalms 12:2-5 — lament over apparent divine delay amid oppression: Habakkuk asks how long he must cry while wronged without being heard or saved. The psalm likewise repeatedly asks how long the Lord will turn away, then asks Him to hear lest the enemy prevail. Both voice distressed waiting for divine help.

Habakkuk 1:3
Why didst Thou show me toil and pain, to look upon misery and impiety? Judgment has come against me, and the judge takes.
  Ecclesiastes 3:16 — wrongdoing encountered in the place of judgment: Habakkuk complains of misery and impiety, with judgment against him and a judge who takes. Ecclesiastes likewise observes an ungodly person in the place of judgment and righteousness. Both locate wrongdoing where justice should be administered.

Habakkuk 1:4
Therefore law has been scattered, and judgment is not brought to completion; the impious oppresses the righteous, so judgment will go forth distorted.
  Isaiah 59:14-15 — judgment distorted or driven back: Habakkuk describes scattered law and distorted judgment while the impious oppresses the righteous. Isaiah likewise says judgment was driven backward, righteousness withdrew and the Lord was displeased at the absence of judgment. Both connect public injustice with the failure of judgment.

Habakkuk 1:5
Look, you despisers, observe, marvel at wonders and vanish! For I am doing a work in your days that you will certainly not believe if someone recounts it.
  Acts 13:40-41 — explicit later quotation of an unbelievable divine work: Habakkuk addresses despisers and announces a work they will not believe even when recounted. Acts explicitly warns about what the prophets spoke and repeats the address, wonder and vanishing, and the work that will not be believed. The wording directly connects the warning.

Habakkuk 1:6
For look, I am raising up against you the Chaldean warriors, the bitter and swift nation, who traverse the breadth of the earth to possess dwellings not their own.
  Jeremiah 5:15-17 — the Lord brings an invading nation: Habakkuk announces the Lord raising Chaldean warriors who seize dwellings not their own. Jeremiah likewise announces a distant nation brought against Israel to consume its produce and attack fortified cities. Both present the invading force within a divine announcement, without assigning Jeremiah Habakkuk's national label.

Habakkuk 1:7
Fearsome and conspicuous are they; their judgment will come from themselves, and their claim will proceed from themselves.
  Isaiah 10:13-14 — an imperial force claims authority for itself: Habakkuk describes the invaders' judgment and claim proceeding from themselves. Isaiah quotes the Assyrian ruler crediting his own strength and wisdom for seizing nations without resistance. Both expose the conqueror's self-assertion, in distinct descriptions of imperial power.

Habakkuk 1:8
Their horses will leap beyond leopards and be fiercer than the wolves of Arabia. Their horsemen will ride out and rush from afar, flying like an eagle eager to eat.
  Deuteronomy 28:49 — a distant invading nation compared to an eagle: Habakkuk describes horsemen rushing from afar and flying like an eagle eager to eat. Deuteronomy likewise announces a nation from afar coming like an eagle's swoop. The distance and predatory flight imagery connect the threats.

Habakkuk 1:9
An end will come upon the impious who stand facing them in opposition, and they will gather captives like sand.
  2 Kings 24:14-16 — the gathering of large numbers of captives: Habakkuk pictures the invading force gathering captives like sand. Kings recounts Babylon's king deporting rulers, warriors and craftsmen in large stated numbers. The historical account illustrates mass deportation without equating its numerical totals with the sand comparison.

Habakkuk 1:10
They will revel among kings, and tyrants will be their toys; they will mock every fortress, throw up a mound and seize it.
  Ezekiel 26:7-9 — siege works used against fortified cities: Habakkuk says the invaders mock fortresses, throw up a mound and seize them. Ezekiel's oracle names Babylon's king and describes building around Tyre, enclosing it with a palisade and weapons, and throwing down its walls. Both portray siege construction overcoming defenses.

Habakkuk 1:11
Then the spirit will change and pass through and make propitiation. This strength belongs to my God.
  Habakkuk 3:19 — same-book recognition of strength as God's: Habakkuk 1:11 concludes that this strength belongs to the speaker's God. The final verse likewise calls the Lord God the speaker's strength. This explicit shared attribution is retained without changing 1:11's preceding spirit and propitiation wording.

Habakkuk 1:12
Art Thou not from the beginning, Lord, my holy God? We shall certainly not die. Lord, Thou hast appointed him for judgment, and He formed me to reprove his discipline.
  Isaiah 10:5-7 — a hostile power appointed within divine judgment: Habakkuk addresses the Lord as having appointed the threatening figure for judgment. Isaiah likewise calls Assyria a rod of divine anger, while distinguishing the ruler's destructive intention. The connection concerns the power's assigned role, not agreement with every purpose of the invader.

Habakkuk 1:13
Thine eye is pure, not to see evil things, and Thou wilt not be able to look upon distress. Why dost Thou look upon despisers? Wilt Thou remain silent when the impious swallows the righteous?
  Psalms 72:12-14 — the difficulty of wicked prosperity beside righteous suffering: Habakkuk questions God's silence when the impious swallows the righteous. The psalm likewise places prosperous sinners beside the speaker's apparently vain righteousness and daily scourging. Both articulate the distress caused by the apparent advantage of the wicked.

Habakkuk 1:14
Thou wilt make people like the fish of the sea and like creeping things with no leader.
  Ecclesiastes 9:12 — people pictured as vulnerable fish: Habakkuk likens people to fish without a leader, before describing their capture. Ecclesiastes likewise compares people caught by an evil time to fish in an evil net. Both use the vulnerability of fish to describe human exposure to harm.

Habakkuk 1:15
They hauled up an entire catch with a hook, dragged it in a casting net and gathered it in their seines; therefore they will rejoice and their heart will be glad.
  Jeremiah 16:16-18 — the catching of people represented as fishing: Habakkuk depicts a whole catch hauled by hook, casting net and seines, continuing the comparison of people with fish. Jeremiah likewise describes fishermen sent to fish for the people, followed by hunters and repayment for wrongdoing. Both apply fishing to the taking of people, with distinct agents and instruments.

Habakkuk 1:16
Therefore they will sacrifice to their seine and burn incense to their casting net, for through them they fattened their portion, and their foods are choice.
  Isaiah 44:15-17 — worship directed to material things associated with food and benefit: Habakkuk says the captors sacrifice to nets because these provide their rich food. Isaiah describes wood used for warmth and cooking, with the rest fashioned into a god and worshiped. Both expose worship directed toward material means of benefit, while the actions upon the material differ.

Habakkuk 1:17
Therefore they will cast their casting net and will not spare nations from continual killing.
  Habakkuk 2:8 — same-book accusation of destructive action against nations and its consequence: Habakkuk 1:17 describes continuing to cast the net and kill nations without sparing them. The later woe charges the despoiling of many nations and bloodshed, then promises the remaining peoples will despoil the aggressor. The later accusation and reversal answer the earlier portrayal of unchecked harm.

Habakkuk 2:1
I will stand at my watch and step onto a rock; I will look out to see what He will speak within me and what I shall answer concerning my reproof.
  Isaiah 21:6-10 — prophetic waiting represented by a watcher's station: Habakkuk stands at his watch to look for what God will speak. Isaiah likewise receives an instruction to station a watcher, who stands day and night and reports what is seen and heard from the Lord. Both use the watcher's position for attentive reception of a message.

Habakkuk 2:2
The Lord answered me: “Write a vision clearly on a boxwood tablet, so that the one reading these things may pursue them.
  Isaiah 30:8 — a prophetic message committed to a tablet: Habakkuk is commanded to write the vision clearly on a boxwood tablet for its readers. Isaiah likewise is commanded to write on a tablet and in a book so that the words endure. Both explicitly require a written record, with different stated purposes and materials.

Habakkuk 2:3
For the vision is still for a season, and it will rise toward the end and not toward emptiness. If he delays, wait for him, for coming he will come and certainly will not linger.
  Hebrews 10:36-37 — later use of the coming-one promise to encourage endurance: Habakkuk commands waiting if he delays because the coming one will come and not linger. Hebrews likewise urges endurance before repeating that the coming One will come and not delay. Both place the promise within an exhortation to patient expectation.

Habakkuk 2:4
If he draws back, My soul has no pleasure in him; but the righteous one shall live by My faithfulness.
  Hebrews 10:38-39 — later quotation joining righteous life and the danger of drawing back: Habakkuk says God's soul takes no pleasure in one drawing back and that the righteous one lives by My faithfulness. Hebrews repeats both clauses in reversed order, saying My righteous one lives by faith. The connection preserves the distinct placement of My and the native faithfulness wording.
  Romans 1:16-17 — explicit later use of the righteous-one life statement: Habakkuk declares that the righteous one lives by My faithfulness. Romans introduces Scripture saying the righteous one lives by faith within its account of the gospel and God's righteousness. The shared statement is direct, while Habakkuk's possessive and faithfulness wording remain distinct.
  Galatians 3:10-12 — later use of the righteous-one statement in discussion of law and faith: Habakkuk says the righteous one lives by My faithfulness. Galatians quotes the righteous one living by faith while arguing about justification before God and law. The later argumentative use is recorded without silently replacing the source's My faithfulness wording.

Habakkuk 2:5
But the wine-soaked, contemptuous, boastful man will accomplish nothing. He widened his appetite like Hades and is like death, never filled; he will gather all nations and receive all peoples to himself.
  Proverbs 27:20 — insatiable human appetite compared to Hades: Habakkuk portrays the boastful conqueror's appetite as Hades and death, never filled despite gathered peoples. Proverbs likewise says Hades and destruction are not filled and compares them with insatiable human eyes. Both expressly link human insatiability with Hades.

Habakkuk 2:6
Will not all these take up a parable against him and a riddle for his account? They will say, ‘Woe to the one multiplying for himself what is not his—how long?—and heavily weighting his collar!’
  Isaiah 14:3-7 — a saying taken up against an oppressive ruler: Habakkuk announces a parable and riddle taken up against one accumulating what is not his. Isaiah likewise introduces a lament against Babylon's king after release from harsh slavery and describes the taskmaster's end. Both turn the oppressor into the subject of a public saying after exploitation.

Habakkuk 2:7
For suddenly those biting him will rise, and those plotting against you will awake; you will become their plunder.
  Habakkuk 2:8 — same-chapter plunder threatened and explained as reversal: Habakkuk 2:7 tells the addressed oppressor that he will become plunder when adversaries rise. The following verse explains that remaining peoples will despoil him because he despoiled many nations. The next unit gives the explicit reciprocal reason for the threatened loss.

Habakkuk 2:8
Because you despoiled many nations, all the remaining peoples will despoil you, because of human bloodshed and impiety against land, city and all its inhabitants.
  Obadiah 1:15 — recompense corresponding to harm done to others: Habakkuk says the despoiler of many nations will himself be despoiled by remaining peoples. Obadiah likewise announces that what was done will return upon the doer's own head. Both explicitly state reciprocal judgment, with Habakkuk specifying plunder and bloodshed.

Habakkuk 2:9
O you who acquire evil gain for your house, setting your nest on high to escape the hand of evils!
  Obadiah 1:3-4 — a high nest imagined as security from danger: Habakkuk condemns setting a nest high to escape evils while acquiring evil gain. Obadiah likewise describes a high dwelling, imagined security from being brought down and a nest among stars from which God will bring its occupant down. Both challenge safety sought through elevated habitation.

Habakkuk 2:10
You planned shame for your house, brought many peoples to an end, and your soul sinned.
  Jeremiah 22:13-17 — a house established through wrongdoing becomes an accusation: Habakkuk says the plot brought shame upon the house and involved the end of many peoples. Jeremiah likewise condemns a house built without righteousness, unpaid labor, greed and innocent blood. Both connect the household's apparent advancement with culpable harm.

Habakkuk 2:11
For a stone will cry from a wall, and a beetle from wood will utter these things.
  Luke 19:39-40 — stones given the role of crying out: Habakkuk depicts a stone crying from a wall and a beetle from wood uttering words. Jesus likewise says that stones will cry out if the disciples fall silent. The personified stone's speech is shared; the settings differ, and no beam is substituted for Habakkuk's beetle.

Habakkuk 2:12
Woe to the one building a city with bloodshed and preparing a city with injustices!
  Micah 3:9-10 — building a city with bloodshed and injustice: Habakkuk condemns building a city with bloodshed and preparing it with injustices. Micah likewise addresses leaders who build Zion with bloodshed and Jerusalem with injustices. The distinctive paired accusation directly connects the passages.

Habakkuk 2:13
Are these things not from the Lord Almighty? Many peoples failed in fire, and many nations lost heart.
  Jeremiah 28:58 — verbal echo: Both pair peoples and nations with fire in a saying of judgment. Habakkuk has 'Many peoples failed in fire, and many nations lost heart', and in Jeremiah Babylon's 'high gates burned', with 'Peoples will not labor in vain, and nations will perish at the beginning.' The wording differs in detail.

Habakkuk 2:14
For the earth will be filled with knowing the Lord’s glory, as water will cover them.
  Isaiah 11:9 — earth filled with knowledge of the Lord compared to covering waters: Habakkuk announces the earth filled with knowing the Lord's glory as water covers. Isaiah likewise declares the whole earth filled with knowledge of the Lord as abundant water covers the seas. Both combine universal knowledge of God with the covering-water comparison.

Habakkuk 2:15
O you who make your neighbor drink with a turbid overthrow and intoxicate him, so that you may look upon their caves!
  Jeremiah 28:7 — shared image: Both texts picture an oppressor making others drunk: Habakkuk's woe on one who makes his neighbor drink and intoxicates him, and Babylon as a cup making the whole earth drunk. The 'caves' of Habakkuk have no counterpart in Jeremiah.

Habakkuk 2:16
Drink, you too, a fullness of dishonor instead of glory; be tossed and shaken! The cup of the Lord’s right hand has come around to you, and dishonor has gathered upon your glory.
  Isaiah 51:17-23 — the cup of divine judgment passes to those who harmed others: Habakkuk commands the aggressor to drink dishonor because the cup from the Lord's right hand has reached him. Isaiah describes the Lord's cup of anger taken from Jerusalem and thrown into its oppressors' hands. Both portray judgment through a cup reaching wrongdoers.

Habakkuk 2:17
For Lebanon’s impiety will cover you, and the misery of wild animals will terrify you, because of human bloodshed and impiety against land, city and all its inhabitants.
  Habakkuk 2:8 — same-chapter repeated indictment for bloodshed and impiety: Both units expressly accuse the addressed power of human bloodshed and impiety against land, city and all inhabitants. The repeated charge connects the woes while 2:17 also names Lebanon and wild animals, details absent from 2:8.

Habakkuk 2:18
What use is a carved image, that they carved it? They formed it as a cast object, a false appearance, because its maker trusted his creation, making mute idols.
  Isaiah 44:9-17 — the futility of worshiping objects made by their worshipers: Habakkuk questions the use of carved and cast objects and the maker's trust in mute idols. Isaiah likewise calls fashioned gods useless and narrates a craftsman making an image from wood and worshiping it. Both expose reliance on human fabrication.

Habakkuk 2:19
Woe to the one saying to wood, ‘Wake and rise!’ and to stone, ‘Be lifted up!’ It is an appearance, a plate of gold and silver, and no breath at all is in it.
  Jeremiah 10:3-5 — wood adorned with precious metal cannot act as a living being: Habakkuk mocks calling wood to wake and stone to rise, describing a gold-and-silver plate with no breath. Jeremiah likewise describes worked wood adorned with gold and silver, unable to move or take a step. Both oppose the decorated object to the powers expected of life.
  Jeremiah 10:14-15 — cast images have no living spirit: Habakkuk explicitly says the decorated image has no breath at all. Jeremiah likewise says cast objects are false and have no spirit in them, then calls them futile works that perish. Both deny life to the fabricated object.

Habakkuk 2:20
But the Lord is in His holy sanctuary; let all the earth revere Him.”
  Psalms 10:4 — the Lord located in His holy sanctuary: Habakkuk contrasts mute idols with the Lord in His holy sanctuary. The psalm likewise places the Lord in His holy temple, with His throne in heaven and eyes examining people. Both explicitly identify His holy dwelling, while Habakkuk calls the earth to reverence.

Habakkuk 3:1
A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet, with a song.
  Habakkuk 1:1 — same person: Both headings of the book name Habakkuk the prophet, here for the prayer with a song and in 1:1 for the oracle he saw.
  Bel and Dragon 1:1 — same person: Bel and the Dragon introduces its account 'from the prophecy of Habakkuk', naming the same prophet, whom it calls the son of Joshua of the tribe of Levi.

Habakkuk 3:2
Lord, I have heard Thy report and was afraid; I considered Thy works and was astonished. In the midst of two living creatures Thou wilt be known; when the years draw near Thou wilt be recognized; when the season arrives Thou wilt be revealed. When my soul is troubled, in wrath Thou wilt remember mercy.
  Psalms 76:8-12 — mercy remembered in the face of divine wrath: Habakkuk declares that God will remember mercy in wrath when his soul is troubled. The psalm likewise asks whether God will forget pity or shut His compassions in wrath, then recalls the Lord's works. This connection concerns mercy and wrath, without interpreting the two living creatures.

Habakkuk 3:3
God will come from Teman, the Holy One from a mountain shaded and thickly wooded. Diapsalma. His excellence covered the heavens, and the earth is full of His praise.
  Deuteronomy 33:2-3 — divine appearance described as coming from a mountain region: Habakkuk depicts God coming from Teman and the Holy One from a shaded mountain, with excellence covering heaven. Deuteronomy likewise describes the Lord coming from Sinai, appearing from Seir and hastening from Mount Paran. The mountain-coming pattern is shared without equating the named locations.

Habakkuk 3:4
His brightness will be like light; horns are in His hands, and He established mighty love of His strength.
  Ezekiel 1:27-28 — brightness in the description of divine glory: Habakkuk describes divine brightness like light, followed by horns in His hands and mighty love. Ezekiel likewise depicts brightness around the visible likeness of the Lord's glory, comparing it to a bow in cloud. The link concerns radiance, without treating the other visionary details as identical.

Habakkuk 3:5
A word will go before Him, and He will go out, His feet in sandals.
  Wisdom of Solomon 18:15-16 — Personification: a moving word in divine intervention: Habakkuk places a word going before God as He goes out with His feet in sandals. Wisdom portrays God's all-powerful word leaping from heaven as a warrior into the doomed land and standing upon the earth. Both give a word movement within a scene of divine intervention. Habakkuk does not call its word a warrior or a sword, and the sandals belong to the One going out rather than being transferred to Wisdom's personified word.

Habakkuk 3:6
He stood, and the earth shook; He looked, and nations melted. Mountains were crushed violently; everlasting hills melted.
  Nahum 1:5-6 — earth and mountains shaken and peoples melted before God: Habakkuk describes the earth shaking, nations melting and mountains crushed. Nahum likewise depicts mountains and earth shaken, rulers melted by fury and rocks crushed. Both present divine presence through these combined images of shaking, melting and crushing.

Habakkuk 3:7
I saw His everlasting journeys in place of toils; the dwellings of Ethiopians will be terrified, and the tents of the land of Midian.
  Judges 7:19-22 — terror and flight in Midian's camp: Habakkuk says the tents of Midian will be terrified. Judges narrates panic and flight in the camp identified earlier as Midian's, with the Lord setting swords against neighbors. The narrative supplies a particular example of Midianite camp terror, without asserting that Habakkuk identifies this event exclusively.

Habakkuk 3:8
Wast Thou angry with rivers, Lord? Was Thy fury against rivers, or Thine onrush against the sea, that Thou wilt mount Thy horses and Thy riding is salvation?
  Psalms 76:16-20 — salvation associated with God's passage through waters: Habakkuk questions fury against rivers and sea and calls God's riding salvation. The psalm recalls the redemption of His people, fearful waters and depths, and His way in the sea. Both combine divine passage through waters with the people's deliverance.

Habakkuk 3:9
Thou wilt surely bend Thy bow against scepters, says the Lord. Diapsalma. The earth will be torn apart by rivers.
  Psalms 7:12-14 — God depicted bending a bow for judgment: Habakkuk pictures God bending His bow against scepters. The psalm likewise presents God as righteous judge and describes Him bending a bow and preparing deadly arrows. The martial image portrays divine judgment, with different objects specified.

Habakkuk 3:10

  Psalms 76:17-19 — waters and depths described with sound during divine appearance: Habakkuk combines peoples' travail, scattered waters and the abyss giving voice in God's passage. The psalm likewise portrays waters seeing God in fear, troubled depths, sounding waters and clouds giving voice. Both animate the waters in the depiction of divine appearance.

Habakkuk 3:11
The sun was raised, and the moon stood in its appointed place. Thy missiles will go into light, into the brightness of Thy flashing weapons.
  Joshua 10:12-14 — sun and moon within an account of divine battle: Habakkuk describes the sun raised, the moon standing in its appointed place and bright weapons. Joshua's battle account likewise names the sun standing and the moon remaining still while the Lord fights for Israel. The common celestial and battle setting is retained, while Habakkuk's sun-raised wording is not changed.

Habakkuk 3:12

  Isaiah 34:1-2 — divine fury directed against nations: Habakkuk describes nations brought down in divine fury and earth diminished by threat. Isaiah likewise summons nations and earth to hear because the Lord's wrath and anger are against nations to hand them to slaughter. Both explicitly connect judgment upon nations with divine fury.

Habakkuk 3:13
Thou didst go out for Thy people’s salvation, to save Thine anointed ones. Thou didst cast death upon the heads of lawless people and didst raise bonds up to the neck. Diapsalma.
  Psalms 17:51 — salvation and mercy for God's anointed: Habakkuk describes God going out to save His people and His anointed ones. The psalm likewise magnifies His king's salvations and mercy to His anointed, David and his seed. The connection preserves the source's plural anointed ones and the psalm's specifically named royal recipient.

Habakkuk 3:14

  Psalms 109:5-6 — Divine-war imagery: rulers and heads struck down: Habakkuk addresses God as cutting rulers' heads in bewilderment during the saving intervention described nearby. The psalm says the Lord crushes kings in anger and crushes heads while judging the nations. Both depict divine victory over ruling power through blows to heads. The psalm does not explain Habakkuk's ensuing bridles and secretly eating poor person, and those native clauses are not replaced with another battle description.

Habakkuk 3:15

  Psalms 76:20 — God's way through the sea's waters: Habakkuk pictures God's horses driven into the sea and troubling much water. The psalm likewise describes God's way in the sea and paths in many waters. Both portray divine movement through water, without assigning horses to the psalm.

Habakkuk 3:16

Habakkuk 3:17

  Joel 1:18 — cattle and sheep without food: Habakkuk describes sheep failing for lack of food and no cattle at feeding troughs. Joel likewise says cattle have no pasture and sheep disappeared. The animals' food failure is a distinct shared element of the agricultural calamity.

Habakkuk 3:18

  Luke 1:46-47 — rejoicing in God as Savior: Habakkuk declares that he will exult in the Lord and rejoice in God his Savior. Mary's song likewise magnifies the Lord and says her spirit rejoiced in God her Savior. The closely corresponding declaration links the two songs.

Habakkuk 3:19
The Lord God is my strength; He will set my feet toward completion and lead me onto the heights, to conquer in His song.
  Psalms 17:33-35 — God's strength places the speaker's feet upon heights: Habakkuk names God as his strength, setting his feet toward completion and leading him onto heights. The psalm likewise describes divine power, a blameless way, firmly set feet and standing on heights. The shared cluster is retained without importing the psalm's deer comparison into Habakkuk.
