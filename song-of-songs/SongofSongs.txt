Song of Songs 1:1
The Song of Songs, which belongs to Solomon.
  1 Kings 5:12 — Solomonic song attribution: The heading assigns this song to Solomon; Kings explicitly includes songs among Solomon's compositions. This links the attribution without identifying this poem as a particular item in that larger collection.

Song of Songs 1:2
Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for your breasts are better than wine.
  Song of Songs 4:10 — Repeated wine comparison: The opening values the beloved's breasts above wine; the later bridal praise repeats the comparison of breasts with wine. The connection follows this edition's actual wording.

Song of Songs 1:3
The fragrance of your perfumes surpasses all spices; your name is perfume poured out. That is why young women loved you.
  Song of Songs 4:10 — Repeated perfume comparison: The opening compares the beloved's perfumes with all spices; the later praise compares the fragrance of the bride's garments with all spices. Both use surpassing fragrance to praise the beloved.

Song of Songs 1:4
They drew you; we will run after you into the fragrance of your perfumes. The king brought me into his chamber. Let us exult and rejoice in you; we will love your breasts more than wine. Uprightness loved you.
  Song of Songs 1:2 — Same-chapter repeated declaration: The chamber scene repeats the opening declaration that the beloved's breasts are valued above wine, placing that preference within the invitation to rejoice together.

Song of Songs 1:5
I am black and beautiful, daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the tent coverings of Solomon.
  Song of Songs 1:6 — Same-chapter appearance and explanation: The declaration of being black and beautiful is followed by the speaker's explanation that the sun darkened her while she was made to keep vineyards. The second statement explains the appearance named in the first.

Song of Songs 1:6
Do not look at me because I have been darkened, because the sun looked upon me. My mother’s sons fought against me; they made me a keeper in vineyards. My own vineyard I did not keep.
  Song of Songs 8:12 — Repeated personal vineyard motif: The speaker says she did not keep her own vineyard; the closing poem again says my own vineyard and places it before the speaker. The repeated personal vineyard language links contrasting statements about its care and possession.

Song of Songs 1:7
Tell me, you whom my soul loved, where you pasture, where you rest at noon, lest I become like a woman covering herself beside your companions’ herds.
  Song of Songs 1:8 — Same-chapter question and answer: The lover asks where the beloved pastures his herds; the response directs the seeker along the flocks' tracks to the shepherds' tents. The pastoral directions answer the pastoral search.

Song of Songs 1:8
If you do not know yourself, beautiful among women, go out along the heels of the flocks and pasture your young goats beside the shepherds’ tents.
  Song of Songs 1:7 — Same-chapter answer to search: The instruction to follow flock tracks and pasture goats beside shepherds' tents answers the preceding question about where the beloved pastures and rests at noon.

Song of Songs 1:10

  Song of Songs 4:9 — Neck ornament in bridal praise: The companion's neck is praised through necklaces; the later bride's single neck ornament is said to stir the speaker's heart. Both make the neck ornament a particular feature of the beloved's appeal.

Song of Songs 1:11

  Song of Songs 1:10 — Same-chapter adornment motif: The promise to make gold likenesses with silver dots follows praise of cheeks and a neck like necklaces. The promise of crafted adornment develops the immediately preceding ornament imagery.

Song of Songs 1:12

  Song of Songs 4:13-14 — Repeated nard fragrance: Nard gives its fragrance while the king reclines; nard appears again among the bride's garden plants and finest perfumes. The same named perfume connects the intimate scene and the garden portrait.

Song of Songs 1:13

  Song of Songs 5:5 — Repeated intimate myrrh imagery: The kinsman is likened to a bundle of myrrh held close; later the speaker's hands drip myrrh while opening to him. Both associate myrrh with the kinsman, first as a personal comparison and later as the scent on the speaker's hands while opening to him.

Song of Songs 1:16

  Song of Songs 1:15 — Same-chapter reciprocal praise: The praise of the beautiful kinsman answers the preceding praise of the beautiful companion. The adjacent declarations turn the repeated beauty formula into an exchange between the lovers.

Song of Songs 2:1

  Song of Songs 2:2 — Same-chapter lily comparison: The speaker calls herself a lily of the valleys; the response compares the companion to a lily among thorns. The second image takes up the first and distinguishes her among the daughters.

Song of Songs 2:3

  Song of Songs 2:2 — Same-chapter reciprocal plant comparisons: The kinsman is an apple tree among the sons just after the companion is a lily among the daughters. Both comparisons single out the beloved through a contrasting plant image.

Song of Songs 2:9

  Song of Songs 8:14 — Repeated gazelle comparison: Both passages compare the kinsman to a gazelle or young stag in a mountain setting. One describes his approach near the wall; the other turns the comparison into the poem's final appeal.

Song of Songs 2:10

  Song of Songs 7:13 — Repeated flowering-vine invitation: Flowering vines accompany the call to rise and come; later the lovers are invited to rise early and inspect the flowering vine and pomegranates. Both combine an invitation to rise with the garden's flowering.

Song of Songs 2:14

Song of Songs 2:16

Song of Songs 2:17

Song of Songs 3:1

  Song of Songs 5:6 — Repeated unsuccessful search formula: Both passages say the speaker sought the beloved without finding him and called without an answer. The later door scene repeats the unsuccessful-search language of the earlier night scene.

Song of Songs 3:2
I will rise now and go around the city, through the marketplaces and squares, and seek the one my soul loved. I sought him but did not find him.
  Song of Songs 5:6-7 — Repeated city-search sequence: The speaker rises to search the city without finding the beloved; the later scene again moves from an unsuccessful search to meeting the city's guards. The repeated sequence connects the two searches.

Song of Songs 3:3
The watchmen going around the city found me. “Have you perhaps seen the one my soul loved?”
  Song of Songs 5:7 — Contrasting encounters with watchmen: The city watchmen find the searching speaker in both scenes. Here she asks about the beloved; later the guards strike, wound and strip her covering, creating a marked contrast between the encounters.

Song of Songs 3:4

  Song of Songs 8:2 — Repeated destination in the mother's house: Both passages speak of bringing the beloved into the mother's house and the chamber of the woman who conceived the speaker. The repeated destination links finding him with the later proposed welcome.

Song of Songs 3:5

  Song of Songs 8:4 — Repeated adjuration refrain: Both addresses adjure Jerusalem's daughters by the powers and strengths of the field concerning awakening love. The later form asks why love should be stirred before it wishes.

Song of Songs 3:6

  Song of Songs 8:5 — Repeated coming-up question: Both scenes open by asking who is coming up. The earlier figure rises from the wilderness amid perfume; the later figure leans on her kinsman. The repeated question frames distinct arrival portraits.

Song of Songs 3:7

  Song of Songs 3:8 — Same-chapter guard description: The sixty mighty men surrounding Solomon's couch are immediately described as armed and trained for war, with swords against night terror. The second verse explains the protective role of the named attendants.

Song of Songs 3:8

  Psalms 44:4-6 — Royal sword imagery: The couch's mighty guards bear swords upon their thighs; the royal psalm addresses a mighty one with a sword upon his thigh and victorious weapons. The shared royal martial imagery does not identify the guards with the psalm's king.

Song of Songs 3:9

  1 Kings 5:20-24 — Solomon's use of Lebanon timber: Solomon's carrying chair is made from Lebanon timber; Kings records Solomon obtaining timber from Lebanon through Hiram. The passages share the king and timber source while describing different construction projects.

Song of Songs 3:11

  Isaiah 61:10 — Bridal adornment and rejoicing: Solomon's wedding crown accompanies his heart's rejoicing; Isaiah describes joyful salvation through a bridegroom's turban and a bride's ornaments. The connection is bridal festivity and adornment, not the same historical wedding.

Song of Songs 4:6

Song of Songs 4:7

Song of Songs 4:8

Song of Songs 4:9
